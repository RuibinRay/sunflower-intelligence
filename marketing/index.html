Personal identity

Van Gogh painted the sunflower so often that it became his signature, the way a brand comes to own a colour. Owning a place in people’s minds starts with knowing them: who buys, how often, and what they will pay.

Young sunflowers turn through the day to follow the sun; once mature, they settle facing east, where the morning warms them first and bees visit most. Customers move too. These tools show who is warm, who is cooling, how much a price change will cost you, whether each new customer is worth what it costs to win, and whether a test result is real.

Customer segments

RFM scores every customer from 1 to 5 on recency (days since the last order), frequency (number of orders) and monetary value (total spend), then groups them so each group can get the message that fits.

Orders

Defaults to the day after the latest order.

  • Scores are quintiles within this customer base, so a “5” means top fifth here, not an absolute standard. Customers with identical values share a score.
  • RFM looks backwards. Pair it with margins and acquisition cost before deciding where to spend.

Price sensitivity

Fit demand on a log-log scale, ln(units) = a + b · ln(price), so the slope b reads directly as the price elasticity: the percentage change in units for a 1% change in price.

—price elasticity

  • The model assumes the same elasticity at every price. Trust it inside the range you tested; demand often turns sharply elastic near a price ceiling.
  • The “1% rule” (units change ≈ elasticity × price change) is a small-change approximation; the exact figure above uses the full curve.
  • Prices that moved with promotions, seasons or competitors will blur the estimate. Randomised price tests are the cleanest evidence.
