How it works

Three models from the research on learning, each small enough to run in your browser. Try them below.

Finding your level: item response theory

Each question has a difficulty b, a sharpness a and a guessing floor c. The chance that someone of ability θ answers correctly is

P(θ) = c + (1 − c) / (1 + e−a(θ − b))

After each answer the check updates its estimate of θ (the average over a bell-shaped prior, weighted by how well each θ explains your answers) and asks next the question carrying the most information at that estimate: I(θ) = P′(θ)² / P(θ)(1 − P(θ)).

Tracking mastery: Bayesian knowledge tracing

Each skill is either mastered or not, and only your answers hint at which. After an answer the chance of mastery is updated with Bayes’ rule, allowing for slips (a master answering wrong, 8%) and guesses (a beginner answering right, 20%), then for the chance you learned something on the way (15%).

after a right answer: P(L | ✓) = P(L)(1 − s) / [P(L)(1 − s) + (1 − P(L)) g]

Your starting mastery is individualised: the placement estimate shifts every skill’s prior, σ(logit prior + θ).

Remembering it: half-life regression

Memory fades on a curve. The chance you still recall a skill after Δ days is 2−Δ/h, where the half-life h grows with right answers and shrinks with wrong ones:

h = 2√right − √wrong − 0.5 × difficulty days

A skill comes back for review when the predicted chance of recall falls to 50%, which is exactly one half-life after you last practised it.

What these numbers are, and aren’t. The question parameters and model settings here are reasoned starting values, not calibrated from learners: calibrating item response theory needs hundreds of people answering the same questions, and the half-life weights in Settles and Meeder’s model were learned from millions of reviews. Treat your estimate as a guide to what to practise, not a grade.
