Data quality reporting and a reproducible workflow

Cleaning is finished only when someone else can see what you did, why, and what it means for the results, and can repeat it. Learn to write a data quality report, keep a cleaning log, and run a reproducible workflow from the raw export to analysis-ready data.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain who reads a data quality report and what each reader needs from it.
  • Write the sections of a data quality report, including each issue, the decision taken and its rationale, and the limitations that remain.
  • Calculate before-and-after profile figures and describe how exclusions change who the data represent.
  • Construct a reproducible cleaning workflow that keeps the raw data untouched and records its lineage.
  • Evaluate reporting choices for honesty, bias and the protection of personal data.

  • Transforming and reshaping data — The report records every transformation, with the row-count and total checks that go with it.
  • Missing values — Deciding whether an exclusion biases a result uses the reasons values go missing.
  • Percentages, and the difference between a change in percentage points and a percentage change.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana Lim is meeting a potential investor next week. Lantern’s analyst, Jun Park, has spent the past few weeks cleaning the lantern-orders export and has produced the figures Ana will show: revenue by region for March to June 2026, how subscribers’ orders compare with one-off orders, and the average customer rating. The investor’s analyst will ask three things before believing any of it. Where did the data come from? What did you change? Could anything you removed change the story? If the answers live only in Jun’s memory and in a spreadsheet he edited by hand, Lantern can’t give them.

A data quality report answers those questions. It travels with the cleaned data and records where the data came from, what was wrong with them, what was done about each problem and why, what those decisions mean for the results, and how to repeat every step. It is the last stage of cleaning, not an optional extra, because data are trustworthy only to someone who can check how they were made.

Who reads it

Different readers come to the report for different parts, and a good report lets each find theirs quickly.

  • Decision makers (Ana, the investor) want to know whether they can rely on a figure and what caveats come with it. They read the summary, the effect of the decisions on the results, and the limitations.
  • Analysts who will reuse or extend the data, including you in six months, want the exact steps, the definitions and the assumptions. They read the data dictionary and the cleaning log.
  • Data owners and reviewers (the team that runs the shop platform, an auditor, a regulator) want to know where the data came from, who handled them, and what happened to personal information. They read the source, the lineage and the privacy notes.

Quality is relative to a use. The UK government’s Data Quality Framework describes data quality as “fitness for purpose”, and the idea applies to any organisation. Lantern’s cleaned file can be good enough to report monthly revenue and not good enough to judge customer satisfaction, because ratings are missing far more often for some kinds of customer than for others. So a report states its purpose first, and the reader judges fitness against that purpose.

Raw data, the log and reproducibility

Raw data are the data exactly as received from their source, before any change: here, the export file downloaded from the shop platform. Treat the raw file as read-only. Every cleaned table is produced from it by recorded steps, and the ordered record of those steps is the cleaning log. Each entry says what was done, to which rows and columns, by what rule, and with what effect on the row count.

Two properties follow when you work this way. Reproducibility: running the same steps on the same raw data gives the same result every time, so anyone can check your figures by rerunning them. Data lineage: every figure in the report can be traced back through the steps to the raw file it came from, and to the version of the steps that produced it. When the investor’s analyst asks where the March revenue for the North region comes from, lineage is the answer: this file, these steps, this version.

Key idea: If you can’t rerun it, you can’t report it

A figure is ready to report when you could delete every cleaned file, rerun the recorded steps on the raw export and get exactly the same figure. Any change made by hand and not recorded breaks that chain.

What a report is not

A data quality report is not a list of apologies, and it is not a sales document. It doesn’t hide problems to make the data look better, and it doesn’t bury the reader in every trivial fix. Its test is simple: could a sceptical reader, with the raw file and the report, understand every decision, judge whether it was reasonable, and reproduce the cleaned data? If so, the report has done its job, even when the honest conclusion is that some questions can’t be answered with these data.

In depth

The sections of a report

There is no single required template, but the sections below cover what reviewers ask for. They draw on published guidance: Broman and Woo’s advice on organising data in spreadsheets (including a separate data dictionary and an untouched primary data file), Wilson and colleagues’ “good enough practices” for scientific computing (save the raw data; record every processing step), and the “datasheets for datasets” proposal of Gebru and colleagues, which asks dataset creators, among much else, whether the raw data were saved and whether the cleaning software is available.

Two pairs of sections are easily confused. Effect on the analysis describes the consequences of decisions you made (you excluded these rows, so this group is under-represented); limitations describe problems you could not solve (these values are missing and can’t be recovered). Assumptions are things you believe but haven’t proved, such as how an ambiguous date should be read; if an assumption is wrong, a figure is wrong, so state it where the reader can find it.

A fingerprint, usually a checksum computed from the file’s bytes, lets anyone confirm that the file they hold is identical to the one you cleaned, which matters when exports are regenerated and quietly change.

Profiles before and after

The profile section puts the same measures side by side for the raw and the cleaned data: rows, columns, the share of missing values in each column, the number of duplicates, and the number of rows failing each validation rule. Report counts and their denominators, because cleaning changes the denominator. The missing share of a column is

missing %=missing values in the columnrows×100\text{missing \%} = \dfrac{\text{missing values in the column}}{\text{rows}} \times 100missing %=rowsmissing values in the column​×100

and the share of rows kept is rows after ÷ rows before × 100. When rows are removed, a missing share can rise even though missing values fell, simply because the denominator fell faster, as the first worked example shows. Compare shares in percentage points (the difference between two percentages: 8% to 5% is a fall of 3 points) and say so; a fall from 8% to 5% is also a 37.5% relative fall, and mixing up the two misleads.

Writing a decision and its rationale

The heart of the report is one entry per issue. Each entry has five parts: the evidence (what you saw and how you found it, usually a validation rule), the scale (how many rows, and which), the decision, the rationale (why this option, with the evidence that supports it), and the alternatives considered and what they would have done to the results. Here is an entry for Harborview Hotel’s bookings:

Issue
Some bookings have a nightly rate of 0 or 9999, outside the hotel’s price range.
Evidence and scale
Found by the range rule on rate; the entry lists the bookings affected. For every one of them, total ÷ nights gives a rate inside the usual range for its room type.
Decision
Replace the rate with total ÷ nights, and add a flag column marking the replaced values.
Rationale
The totals agree with the price list and with similar bookings, so they are the reliable field; 0 and 9999 are placeholder codes, not prices.
Alternative
Excluding the bookings would remove real stays and bias the average rate (see the applied example).

A rationale that says only “cleaned the rates” or “removed bad rows” is not a rationale. The reader must be able to disagree with you, and they can only do that if you show what you saw and what you weighed.

Effect on the analysis: who is left out

Every exclusion changes whom the data represent. If the removed rows are like the rest, results computed on what remains are unbiased, only less precise. If they differ, the results shift. The test is to compare the excluded and the kept rows on the variables that matter for the question: customer type, region, channel, month. When the groups differ, report the figure computed on the kept rows, say which groups are under-represented, and, where you can, show an adjusted figure, for example a weighted average of group results using each group’s true size, together with the assumption it rests on.

This is where the reasons for missingness from the lesson on missing values reach the report. Lantern’s ratings are missing more often for some customers than others, so an average over the ratings that exist describes the customers who rate, not all customers. That is not a reason to hide the average; it is a reason to label it honestly.

Reproducibility and lineage in practice

  • Keep the raw file pristine. Store it read-only in its own folder, record its fingerprint, and back it up in more than one place. Broman and Woo put it plainly: the primary data file should stay a pristine store of data, with calculations done elsewhere.
  • Make every change a recorded step. Wilson and colleagues recommend writing scripts for every stage of data processing: a pandas or R script, a SQL query, or a tool that records steps. Excel’s Power Query, for example, saves every transformation as an applied step that can be inspected and rerun.
  • One step, one purpose, in a fixed order, with the row count logged after each step.
  • Keep parameters in one place: thresholds such as the 1.5 × IQR fence, mapping tables for categories, date conventions and cut-off dates. Changing a rule should mean changing one line.
  • Treat outputs as disposable. Never hand-edit a cleaned file or a report figure. Fix the step and rerun.
  • Version the steps and the outputs together, and note the software used, so that version 1.1 of the report can always be traced to version 1.1 of the steps.
  • Test the chain: rerun everything from the raw file into an empty folder and confirm the outputs are identical.

Order is part of the recipe. Trim spaces and standardise case before removing duplicates, or “north ” and “North” keep two rows apart that are really one. Convert types before checking ranges, because a price stored as text can’t be compared with a number. Remove duplicates before joining, or each duplicate multiplies. Split training and test data before fitting any scaler or encoder, so that nothing learned from the test set leaks into training. If you must work in a spreadsheet, keep the raw sheet untouched, do the work in a copy, and keep a log sheet that lists each step in words precise enough for someone else to repeat.

The Turing Way, a community handbook on reproducible research, distinguishes reproducing a result (same data, same analysis) from replicating it (new data, same analysis). A data quality report makes the first possible and supports the second: when Lantern’s July export arrives, the same steps run on new data, and any new kind of problem shows up as a rule failure in the profile.

Honesty and personal data

Cleaning is full of judgement calls, and judgement can drift towards the answer you hoped for. A few rules keep it honest. Decide the rules before looking at the results they produce, where you can. Apply the same rule to convenient and inconvenient values: a one-star rating is a valid value on a one-to-five scale, not an outlier to be trimmed. Disclose every exclusion, with counts. And when a contested decision changes the conclusion, show the result both ways and explain which you prefer and why.

The data often contain personal information, and Lantern’s export holds customers’ e-mail addresses. Data protection laws such as the EU and UK GDPR require personal data to be “adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary” for the purpose, the principle of data minimisation. In practice: use e-mail addresses only where they are needed (for example, to recover a missing customer number), then drop them from the analysis file and work with customer_id, a pseudonymised key; restrict access to the raw file; never put personal details in the report itself; and avoid publishing figures for groups so small that a reader could identify the people in them.

Caution: Cleaning is not editing the truth

Removing a value because it is inconvenient, such as a bad month, an angry review or a region that underperformed, is not cleaning; it is changing the answer. The defensible reasons to change or exclude a value are evidence that it is wrong or evidence that it is out of scope, and both belong in the report.

Common mistakes

  • “The report is just the list of cleaning steps.”

    The log says what was done; the report also says why, what it changed, and what it means for the results. A reader can’t judge “removed 30 rows” without knowing which rows, by what rule, and whether their removal shifts who the data represent.

  • “Fewer missing values means a lower missing percentage.”

    Not if rows were removed too. The share is missing values ÷ rows, and cleaning changes both. Report counts and denominators, and compare percentages in percentage points.

  • “I’ll fix the odd cell by hand; it’s only one.”

    One unrecorded edit breaks reproducibility: the next export brings the problem back, and nobody can trace the figure that changed. Express even a single correction as a rule or a documented replacement table, and run it as a step.

  • “Excluding incomplete rows is neutral.”

    It is neutral only if the excluded rows are like the rest. When some groups are missing more often, the remaining data over-represent the others and averages shift. Compare kept and excluded rows, and report the effect.

  • “Outliers should come out before reporting.”

    Only values shown to be errors, or out of scope, should be changed or excluded, and the report says which and why. A genuine bulk order or a one-star review is real data; removing it to tidy a chart changes the answer.

  • “Personal data can stay in the file; it’s internal.”

    Keep personal data only where the purpose needs it. Once e-mail addresses have helped recover missing customer numbers, drop them from the analysis file, keep the raw file access-restricted, and never quote personal details in the report.

In practice

Here is how Jun takes Lantern’s order export from raw to reported, step by step. Each step uses a lesson from this course, and each leaves an entry in the cleaning log and a line in the report.

  1. Freeze the source. He saves the export exactly as downloaded, makes it read-only, and records the extraction date, the file’s fingerprint, and its rows and columns as received. That is the report’s source section.
  2. Describe the data. One row per order, order_id as the key, fourteen variables with their types and units in a data dictionary (the lesson on datasets and variables).
  3. Profile and write rules. Quantities must be whole numbers from 1 to 200, the data dictionary’s range; dates must fall between 1 March and 30 June 2026; regions must be one of five names; order_total must equal quantity × unit_price − discount to the cent. The raw profile records how often each rule fails (profiling and validation).
  4. Fix records and formats. He trims spaces and standardises case first, then removes exact duplicates; for order ids that repeat with a changed value, he keeps the copy the shop system confirms. He converts the several date formats and the text prices, including the one written with a decimal comma, and maps region spellings with a mapping table (duplicates; types and formats; text and categories).
  5. Decide about missing and unusual values. Missing e-mails are recovered from the same customer’s other orders where possible and otherwise left missing; missing ratings stay missing and are not imputed for the rating analysis. The genuine bulk order stays, flagged; the typing-error outlier is corrected only if other evidence, such as the order total or the price list, shows the right value. Right-skewed totals are reported with medians (missing values; outliers; skewed data).
  6. Transform for the questions. He derives expected totals and months, aggregates by region and month with totals reconciled, and joins the product-cost table after checking that its key is unique (transforming data). Scaling and encoding, if a model is built, are fitted on the training set inside the modelling pipeline (scaling; encoding categories).
  7. Protect personal data. With duplicates and recoveries done, he drops email from the analysis file and works with customer_id.
  8. Profile again, write the report, and rerun. The after profile shows every rule passing or every remaining failure explained. He writes the decisions, their effects and the limitations, deletes the outputs, reruns the steps from the raw file, confirms identical results, and labels the report version 1.0.

The report Ana takes to the investor is short at the front: what the data cover, what the figures are, and the three caveats that matter (ratings come mostly from the customers who rate most; one order was a genuine bulk purchase that lifts March to June revenue; a few customers can’t be matched across orders). Behind it sit the dictionary, the profiles, the decision entries and the log. When the investor’s analyst asks how the regional revenue was computed, Jun can show the step, rerun it in front of her, and get the same number.

The same discipline appears wherever data inform decisions that others check. Harborview’s revenue manager attaches a short quality note to each monthly report, listing placeholder rates recovered and channel-manager duplicates removed, so that the owners can compare months fairly. Northfield University’s planning office converts grade point averages recorded on two scales to one, and states the conversion rule in every report that uses them, because a regulator or an accreditation panel may ask. In each case the report costs little to write once the steps are recorded, and it is what turns a cleaned file into evidence.

This lesson closes the course. You began with a messy export and a question; you can now describe the data, profile them, fix their records and formats, decide about missing and unusual values, prepare them for analysis, and, finally, show your work so that someone else can trust it and repeat it.

Summary

  • A data quality report records where the data came from, what was wrong, what was done and why, what that means for the results, and how to repeat it. Quality is judged against a stated purpose.
  • Core sections: purpose and scope, source, data dictionary, profile before and after, issues and decisions, effect on the analysis, assumptions, limitations, cleaning log, version.
  • Each decision entry gives the evidence, the scale, the decision, the rationale and the alternatives, so that a reader can disagree with it.
  • Report counts with their denominators: removing rows changes every share. Compare percentages in percentage points, and keep relative changes separate.
  • Exclusions change whom the data represent. Compare kept and excluded rows, report the effect, and give an adjusted figure with its assumption where possible.
  • Keep the raw data read-only and make every change a recorded, rerunnable step in a fixed order; reproducibility and lineage follow.
  • Be honest and minimise personal data: never remove values because they are inconvenient, disclose every exclusion, and keep personal details out of analysis files and reports.

Terms introduced

Data quality report
A document that travels with a cleaned dataset and records its source, its problems, the decisions taken and why, their effect on the results, the remaining limitations and how to reproduce the cleaning.
Raw data
Data exactly as received from their source, before any cleaning or transformation. Kept read-only so that every cleaned version can be rebuilt from them.
Cleaning log
The ordered record of every cleaning step: what was done, to which rows and columns, by what rule, and the row count afterwards.
Reproducibility
The property that running the same steps on the same data gives the same result every time.
Data lineage
The traceable path from a figure or cleaned table back through every step to the raw data and the version of the steps that produced it.
Percentage point
The unit for the difference between two percentages: a change from 8% to 5% is a fall of 3 percentage points (and a relative fall of 37.5%).
Data minimisation
The data-protection principle that personal data should be adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary for the purpose.
Pseudonymised
Describes data in which direct identifiers such as names or e-mail addresses are replaced by codes, so that records can still be linked but people can’t be identified without separately held information.
