Prediction and intervals

Use a fitted line to predict, and say how far to trust the prediction: the confidence interval for the average at a given x against the prediction interval for one new case, why both widen away from the mean of x, why the prediction interval never shrinks below the residual spread, and why extrapolation escapes them both. Then roster a Saturday’s peak from a weather forecast, by hand and in Excel.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate a point prediction ŷ₀ = b₀ + b₁x₀ for a new x, and explain why the same number estimates both the average at x₀ and a single new case.
  • Calculate the confidence interval for the mean response and the prediction interval for one new case, using 1/n + (x₀ − x̄)²/Sxx, by hand and with FORECAST.LINEAR, STEYX, DEVSQ and T.INV.2T.
  • Explain why both intervals are narrowest at x̄ and widen away from it, and why the prediction interval can’t shrink below about ±t × s however much data there is.
  • Identify extrapolation, both beyond the range of x and to conditions the sample didn’t cover, and explain why no interval allows for it.
  • Use a prediction bound to make a planning decision, and state what the prediction interval assumes about the errors.

Review first

  • Inference for coefficients — The population model, its conditions, standard errors and the t multiplier on n − 2 degrees of freedom.
  • Residuals and R² — The residual standard error s, which sets the width of every prediction interval.
  • Confidence intervals — Intervals as estimate ± critical value × standard error, and what the confidence level promises.
  • Descriptive statistics and regression in Excel — FORECAST.LINEAR and its argument order; B03 introduced it for point predictions only.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Friday’s forecast for Saturday is a high of 80°F and no rain. Harbourside’s manager has to set the 7–11 a.m. barista roster tonight, and Ana’s rule is to keep peak transactions per barista hour at about 7 or fewer, where B05 found that waits stay short. The previous lesson’s 49 weekend days give ŷ = 26.10 + 1.005x. How many transactions should the manager plan for, and how sure can she be?

A point prediction, and two questions it answers

Put the forecast into the line: y^0=26.10+1.005×80=106.5\hat{y}_0 = 26.10 + 1.005 \times 80 = 106.5y^​0​=26.10+1.005×80=106.5 transactions. That single number, the point prediction, answers two different questions, and they need different intervals.

  • What is the average number of transactions on weekend days with a high of 80°F? This is a question about the population line at x₀ = 80, the mean response. Its uncertainty comes only from not knowing β₀ and β₁ exactly.
  • How many transactions will there be this Saturday? This is a question about one new day. Its uncertainty includes the line’s and, on top, the day’s own error ε: rain that wasn’t forecast, a coach party, a quiet morning.

The first gets a confidence interval for the mean response, from 100.5 to 112.5 transactions. The second gets a prediction interval, from 75.3 to 137.8. The manager is rostering one Saturday, so the prediction interval is hers. Its width is the honest summary: even with a good model and a reliable forecast, a single 80°F Saturday could plausibly bring anything from about 75 to 138 transactions.

Key idea: Two intervals, two questions

The confidence interval for the mean response is about the average of all days like this one; it shrinks towards zero width as data accumulate. The prediction interval is about one new day; however much data there is, it can’t be narrower than the scatter of single days about the line, roughly ±t × s. When the decision is about a single case (this Saturday’s roster, one customer’s bill, one machine’s output), use the prediction interval.

In depth

Why the intervals widen away from x̄

The term (x0−xˉ)2/Sxx(x_0 - \bar{x})^2 / S_{xx}(x0​−xˉ)2/Sxx​ is zero at the mean of x and grows with the square of the distance from it. The least-squares line always passes through (xˉ,yˉ)(\bar{x}, \bar{y})(xˉ,yˉ​); an error in its slope swings it round that point like a seesaw, so the line is pinned most firmly in the middle of the data and least at the ends. At x0=xˉx_0 = \bar{x}x0​=xˉ the standard error of the mean response is simply s/n=15.24/7=2.18s/\sqrt{n} = 15.24/7 = 2.18s/n​=15.24/7=2.18, the standard error of a mean; at 80°F it is 2.99 and at the warmest day in the data, 94°F, 4.45.

The interval for the mean more than doubles in width from x̄ to 94°F, while the prediction interval hardly moves: the day’s own error, s, dwarfs the uncertainty in the line. That is typical of business data with a modest R² (here 0.56). Better estimates of the line help the mean much more than the single case; only a better model, one that explains more of each day’s scatter, narrows the prediction interval substantially.

The seesaw also says where data are most useful. A prediction at the warm end is helped by more warm days, which pull xˉ\bar{x}xˉ towards 80°F, and by a wide spread of highs, which raises SxxS_{xx}Sxx​. A sample of days all near 64°F would predict 64°F well and 90°F badly, even with the same n and the same s. If a model will be used mostly at one end of its range (Ana rosters with care on hot weekends, rarely on cold ones), that end is where the data should be densest.

The prediction interval’s floor

As n grows, 1/n1/n1/n and (x0−xˉ)2/Sxx(x_0 - \bar{x})^2/S_{xx}(x0​−xˉ)2/Sxx​ both shrink towards 0, so SEmean→0SE_{\text{mean}} \to 0SEmean​→0 but SEpred→sSE_{\text{pred}} \to sSEpred​→s. With ten years of weekends Ana would know the average at 80°F almost exactly, and a single Saturday would still range about ±1.96 × 15 transactions. Penn State’s STAT 462 puts it this way: the prediction interval is always wider than the confidence interval at the same x, and the extra term never goes away. The only way to narrow it is to reduce s itself. Rain is the obvious candidate: with the day’s rain in the model as well (multiple regression, module 3), s falls from 15.24 to 12.91.

Extrapolation

The intervals assume the straight line holds at x0x_0x0​. Inside the data’s range, 33°F to 94°F, the data can check that; outside it, nothing can. A forecast of 100°F gives ŷ = 126.6 with a prediction interval of 94.2 to 159.0, but the interval only measures uncertainty about a line that is assumed to continue. Real trade may level off as the waterfront becomes too hot to sit in, or the café may simply run out of seats. That is extrapolation, and NIST’s e-Handbook lists poor extrapolation among the main weaknesses of linear least squares. OpenStax’s statistics texts draw the same line between interpolation (predicting inside the observed x values) and extrapolation (outside them).

Extrapolation is not only about x. Ana’s 49 days are Harbourside weekends from July, after the café had built up its regulars. Applied to March, inside the range of temperatures but outside the period, the line overpredicts every one of the eight March weekend days, by 16 transactions on average: the café was still in its first months. The same model is equally unfit for a weekday, for Millbrook, or for a festival day. A prediction is safe only for cases like the ones the model was fitted to, in x and in everything the model leaves out.

What the prediction interval assumes

The interval for the mean leans on the same conditions as the slope’s inference; the prediction interval leans on them harder, because it describes where one error will fall, not where an average will.

  • Equal spread. The interval has the same half-width (apart from the small seesaw term) at every x. If the spread grows with the mean, it is too wide for cool days and too narrow for warm ones. The case’s generator is multiplicative, so this is the direction to expect; in these 49 days the residuals’ standard deviation is 15.8 on days of 70°F or more against 13.2 below 55°F, a modest difference the Breusch–Pagan test can’t distinguish from chance.
  • Roughly normal errors. The ±t × SE recipe sets the 95% limits where a normal error would put them. For the slope, the central limit theorem rescues the interval as n grows; for one new case it doesn’t, because a single error is not an average. Skewed errors make one tail of the prediction interval too short at any n. Module 4’s normal Q–Q plot checks this.
  • Independence and the right model. The day must be like those the model was fitted to, and the model must not have left out a trend that has moved since.

A rough check is how many of the fitted days fall outside their own 95% prediction intervals: here 2 of 49 (4%), about the 5% expected. That is reassuring, though not a test: the intervals were computed from the same days.

Reporting a prediction

A prediction is only useful with four things beside it: the point value, the interval and which kind it is, the confidence level, and the scope (the range of x and the kind of case the model was fitted to). Software names the two intervals differently: OpenStax’s business statistics text calls them the confidence interval and the prediction interval, Penn State’s course the confidence interval for the mean response and the prediction interval for a new response, and some statistical packages label them “mean” and “obs” (observation). Whatever the label, ask whether it contains the 1 under the square root. If a report gives a single interval for a forecast without saying which, ask; one much narrower than the residual standard error suggests is probably the interval for the mean.

In Excel

The ToolPak’s Regression tool prints no interval for a new x, so build them from functions. With the highs in B2:B50, transactions in C2:C50 and the new high in F1:

  • Point prediction: =FORECAST.LINEAR(F1,C2:C50,B2:B50) (new x first, then known_y’s, then known_x’s; Microsoft’s page notes it replaced FORECAST in Excel 2016). =TREND(C2:C50,B2:B50,F1) gives the same value.
  • The pieces: s is =STEYX(C2:C50,B2:B50), Sxx is =DEVSQ(B2:B50), n is =COUNT(B2:B50) and the multiplier is =T.INV.2T(0.05,COUNT(B2:B50)-2).
  • Half-width of the prediction interval: =T.INV.2T(0.05,COUNT(B2:B50)-2)*STEYX(C2:C50,B2:B50)*SQRT(1+1/COUNT(B2:B50)+(F1-AVERAGE(B2:B50))^2/DEVSQ(B2:B50)). Drop the 1+ for the interval for the mean.

One-sided bounds for planning

A 95% prediction interval leaves 2.5% in each tail. A roster only worries about the upper tail: too few baristas is the costly mistake. A one-sided upper bound puts all the risk on that side: y^0+t0.90, n−2×SEpred\hat{y}_0 + t_{0.90,\,n-2} \times SE_{\text{pred}}y^​0​+t0.90,n−2​×SEpred​ is exceeded on about 10% of such days, if the model and its conditions hold. At 80°F that is 106.5+1.300×15.53=126.7106.5 + 1.300 \times 15.53 = 126.7106.5+1.300×15.53=126.7 transactions, with 1.300 from =T.INV(0.9,47). Which tail probability to accept is a business choice, the cost of an idle barista against the cost of long waits; the statistics only price each choice.

Common mistakes

  • Using the confidence interval for a single day

    The interval for the mean, 100.5 to 112.5 at 80°F, is about the average of all such days. One Saturday scatters about that average by roughly s, so its interval, 75.3 to 137.8, is five times as wide. Rostering to the top of the narrow one under-staffs about a third of such Saturdays.

  • “More data will make the prediction interval as narrow as I like.”

    More data shrinks 1/n and the distance term, not the 1 under the square root. The prediction interval’s floor is about ±t × s; only a model with smaller residuals lowers it.

  • Extrapolating because the formula still gives an answer

    At 100°F, or for a March weekend, FORECAST.LINEAR returns a number and the formula an interval. Neither knows the data stopped at 94°F or began after the café’s build-up. Check that x₀ and the conditions are inside what the model was fitted to.

  • Treating the interval as exact when the spread changes with x

    The prediction interval has nearly the same width at every x. If the spread grows with the mean, as a multiplicative process makes it, the interval is too narrow at the busy end, where mistakes cost most. Check the residual plot (module 4) before trusting the busy end.

  • Mixing up FORECAST.LINEAR’s arguments

    FORECAST.LINEAR takes the new x first, then known_y’s, then known_x’s; SLOPE, INTERCEPT and STEYX take known_y’s first. =FORECAST.LINEAR(80,B2:B50,C2:C50) predicts the high from transactions instead.

In practice

Fictional case

Ana’s weekend rostering sheet now has three columns beside the forecast: the predicted transactions, the one-sided 90% upper bound, and the barista hours at that bound. Her note to the manager reads: “Roster to the bound, rounded to the half hour. The model covers weekends from July to December 2027 between 33°F and 94°F; don’t use it for weekdays, festivals or forecasts outside that range. It ignores rain; on a dry 80°F forecast a model with rain gives almost the same bound (127.5 transactions against 126.7). It will be refitted with rain and the full year once 2028’s spring weekends are in.”

The sheet turns a prediction interval into a rule a manager can follow without statistics, and the note limits the rule to the cases it was built from. That is how prediction is used professionally: a point forecast for the central plan, a prediction bound for capacity, and a written scope. A bakery sets the next day’s bake from a predicted demand plus a margin for the day’s scatter; a call centre staffs to an upper bound on calls; an energy supplier hedges against the upper end of a cold-weather demand forecast.

In each case the width of the interval is itself information. A wide prediction interval says the model leaves a lot of each day unexplained; narrowing it is a job for a better model, with more predictors or a better functional form, not for more decimal places. And the interval for the mean has its own uses: a budget for the season’s average warm Saturday, or a comparison between two cafés’ typical days.

Finally, a prediction is only as good as its inputs. A 95% prediction interval treats the forecast high as known. If the forecast itself might be 5°F out, the transactions it implies are more uncertain still; a slope of about 1 transaction per °F makes that roughly 5 transactions either way, small beside the interval’s ±31 but worth knowing.

Summary

  • ŷ₀ = b₀ + b₁x₀ estimates both the average at x₀ and a single new case; FORECAST.LINEAR(x₀, known_y’s, known_x’s) computes it.
  • Interval for the mean response: ŷ₀ ± t* × s√(1/n + (x₀ − x̄)²/Sxx). Prediction interval for one new case: the same with 1 + under the root.
  • Both are narrowest at x̄ and widen with (x₀ − x̄)²; the interval for the mean shrinks towards 0 with more data, the prediction interval never below about ±t* × s.
  • Extrapolation, beyond the data’s x or to conditions the sample didn’t cover, is outside what any interval measures.
  • The prediction interval depends strongly on constant spread and roughly normal errors, at any sample size.
  • For planning against one costly side, use a one-sided bound: ŷ₀ + T.INV(1 − α, n − 2) × SE_pred.

Terms introduced

Point prediction
The value of a fitted regression at a new x, ŷ₀ = b₀ + b₁x₀: the estimate of the mean response there and the best single guess for one new case.
Confidence interval for the mean response
An interval for the average of y over all cases with a given x, ŷ₀ ± t* × s√(1/n + (x₀ − x̄)²/Sxx) in simple regression. It narrows towards zero width as the sample grows.
Prediction interval
An interval for the value of y in one new case at a given x, ŷ₀ ± t* × s√(1 + 1/n + (x₀ − x̄)²/Sxx) in simple regression. Always wider than the interval for the mean, because it adds the new case’s own error.
