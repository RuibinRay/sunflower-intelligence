Residuals and R²

Judge how well a line fits: residuals, the split of the total variation into SST = SSR + SSE, R² as the share of variation the line accounts for, and the residual standard error in y’s own units. Read a first residual plot, see a line with R² = 0.88 that is the wrong shape, and learn three things R² can’t tell you.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate residuals and interpret their sign and size in the units of the response.
  • Calculate the total, regression and residual sums of squares, show that the first is the sum of the other two, and find R² from them.
  • Interpret R² with careful wording, and show that it equals r² when there is one explanatory variable.
  • Calculate and interpret the residual standard error, by formula and with STEYX.
  • Read a residual plot for curvature, and explain why a high R² doesn’t show a line is right, a low R² doesn’t show it is useless, and R² depends on the range of x.

Review first

  • The least-squares line — The least-squares line, its slope and intercept, and the normal equations that make residuals add to zero.
  • Curves, thresholds and restricted ranges — The S-shaped iced-share curve and the way a restricted range shrinks r, both met again here through R².
  • Describing distributions — Sums of squares, variance and standard deviation, which R² and the residual standard error are built from.
  • Descriptive statistics and regression in Excel — RSQ, STEYX and the ToolPak’s R Square and Standard Error lines, read here more closely.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana now has a line for Harbourside: sales = −76.58 + 6.64 × transactions. On Wednesday 14 July the café made 81 transactions and took $484.45; the line says $461.35. On Saturday 29 May, a festival day, it made 293 and took $2,108.92; the line says $1,869.26. Is a line that misses by $23 on one day and $240 on another a good line? She needs ways to say how close the days lie to it, in dollars and in proportion.

Residuals

A residual is the observed value minus the fitted value, e=y−y^e = y - \hat{y}e=y−y^​, in y’s units. On 14 July it is 484.45−461.35=+23.10484.45 - 461.35 = +23.10484.45−461.35=+23.10 dollars: the café took $23.10 more than the line expects for 81 transactions. On 29 May it is +$239.66. A negative residual means a day below the line. Each residual is the part of that day’s sales the line doesn’t account for: the ticket mix, a coach party, the till rounding, chance.

Two facts from the least-squares line’s normal equations hold for every fit with an intercept: the residuals add up to exactly zero, and they have zero correlation with x. So a residual’s sign and size carry information about the day, not about which side of the line the method favours.

Splitting the variation

Without any line, the best single guess for a day’s sales is the mean, $358.17, and the misses from it measure how much sales vary. The total sum of squares adds up their squares: SST=∑(y−yˉ)2\text{SST} = \sum (y - \bar{y})^2SST=∑(y−yˉ​)2. Each day’s deviation from the mean splits into two pieces, the part the line accounts for and the residual:

y−yˉ=(y^−yˉ)+(y−y^)y - \bar{y} = (\hat{y} - \bar{y}) + (y - \hat{y})y−yˉ​=(y^​−yˉ​)+(y−y^​)

Square both sides and add up over the days, and for a least-squares line with an intercept the cross-term vanishes (In depth shows why), leaving

∑(y−yˉ)2⏟SST=∑(y^−yˉ)2⏟SSR+∑(y−y^)2⏟SSE\underbrace{\sum (y - \bar{y})^2}_{\text{SST}} = \underbrace{\sum (\hat{y} - \bar{y})^2}_{\text{SSR}} + \underbrace{\sum (y - \hat{y})^2}_{\text{SSE}}SST∑(y−yˉ​)2​​=SSR∑(y^​−yˉ​)2​​+SSE∑(y−y^​)2​​

SSR, the regression sum of squares, is the variation in the fitted values; SSE, the residual (error) sum of squares, is what least squares made as small as it could. For Harbourside’s 306 days, SST = 15,397,215, SSR = 15,157,236 and SSE = 239,979 (all in dollars squared). Almost all of the day-to-day variation in sales lies along the line.

R², the share of variation accounted for

R2=SSRSST=1−SSESSTR^2 = \frac{\text{SSR}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}}R2=SSTSSR​=1−SSTSSE​

For Harbourside R2=15,157,236÷15,397,215=0.984R^2 = 15{,}157{,}236 \div 15{,}397{,}215 = 0.984R2=15,157,236÷15,397,215=0.984. Say it carefully: “the line accounts for 98.4% of the variation in Harbourside’s daily sales around their mean.” Textbooks and software often say “explains”, and that is the conventional term, but it is a statement about squared deviations, not about causes. It is not “98.4% accurate”, and not “transactions cause 98.4% of sales”. R squared runs from 0 (the line does no better than the mean) to 1 (every point on the line).

With one explanatory variable, R² is the square of the correlation: 0.99222=0.9840.9922^2 = 0.9840.99222=0.984. It is also the squared correlation between y and the fitted values, which is how the idea carries over to models with several explanatory variables in module 3. Because squaring loses the sign, a line with a negative slope has r=−R2r = -\sqrt{R^2}r=−R2​.

The residual standard error

R² is a proportion. Ana also needs to know how far off the line typically is, in dollars. The standard error of the regression, or residual standard error, is

s=SSEn−2s = \sqrt{\frac{\text{SSE}}{n - 2}}s=n−2SSE​​

For Harbourside s=239,979÷304=28.10s = \sqrt{239{,}979 \div 304} = 28.10s=239,979÷304​=28.10 dollars: the line’s fitted sales are typically about $28 away from a day’s actual sales. Compare it with the standard deviation of sales, $224.68, the typical miss from the mean alone. In Excel it is =STEYX(known_y's,known_x's), the “Standard Error” line in the ToolPak’s regression output (B03). On 298 of the 306 days (97%) the residual is within two residual standard errors, about ±$56, a rough guide that module 4 refines.

Key idea: Report both, and look at the residuals

R² says what share of the variation lies along the line; s says, in y’s units, how far off the line typically is. Neither says whether a straight line is the right shape. A residual plot does.

In depth

Why the sums of squares add up

Squaring the split gives ∑(y−yˉ)2=SSR+SSE+2∑(y^−yˉ) e\sum (y - \bar{y})^2 = \text{SSR} + \text{SSE} + 2\sum (\hat{y} - \bar{y})\,e∑(y−yˉ​)2=SSR+SSE+2∑(y^​−yˉ​)e. The fitted value is y^=b0+b1x\hat{y} = b_0 + b_1 xy^​=b0​+b1​x, so the cross-term is 2∑(b0−yˉ) e+2b1∑x e2\sum (b_0 - \bar{y})\,e + 2b_1 \sum x\,e2∑(b0​−yˉ​)e+2b1​∑xe, and both sums are zero by the normal equations (∑e=0\sum e = 0∑e=0 and ∑xe=0\sum x e = 0∑xe=0). The identity holds for least squares with an intercept, which is what Excel’s SLOPE, INTERCEPT, trendline and ToolPak fit. For a line forced through the origin, or a line fitted any other way, SST ≠ SSR + SSE and “1 − SSE ÷ SST” loses its meaning; Excel then reports an R² computed about zero instead of about the mean, which is why the least-squares-line lesson warned against comparing it.

The same three sums appear in the ToolPak’s ANOVA table: SS for Regression, Residual and Total. Dividing each by its degrees of freedom gives mean squares, and their ratio is the F statistic; that is module 2’s test.

Why n − 2

The residual standard error divides by n−2n - 2n−2, not n. Two coefficients were estimated from the same data, and the residuals satisfy two constraints (the normal equations), so only n−2n - 2n−2 of them are free to vary. Dividing by n would understate the typical miss, slightly with 306 days and badly with 6. More generally the divisor is n−pn - pn−p, with p coefficients counting the intercept, which is why STEYX and LINEST’s third row agree with the ToolPak.

A first residual plot

B05 found a correlation of 0.94 between the day’s high and the share of drinks served iced, over both cafés’ 612 days. The least-squares line is iced share = −0.747 + 0.0176 × high, with R² = 0.883 and s = 0.096 (9.6 percentage points). By R² this is an excellent line. Plot the residuals against the high and it isn’t.

The residuals are positive on cold days, negative from 45 to 64 °F, near zero around 70 and positive again from 75 to 84: a wave. A straight line through an S-shaped relationship misses in a pattern: the line runs below the curve on the coldest days, above it through the 50s and early 60s, and below it again in the late 70s and early 80s. Worse, the line gives a negative iced share on the coldest days: it crosses zero at 42.5 °F, and on the 32 café-days below 40 °F it fits −0.13 on average where the observed share was +0.06. The data were generated with a logistic curve in temperature, centred near 69 °F, so this pattern is the truth showing through.

Read a residual plot by looking for structure. If the line is the right shape, the residuals scatter evenly around zero with no trend, curve or funnel. A curve says the relationship bends; a funnel says the spread changes with x; isolated large residuals say some days don’t fit. Harbourside’s sales line, R² = 0.984, has a pattern too: its residuals average +$26.52 on days with fewer than 40 transactions, −$11.21 and −$22.98 in the 60–79 and 80–119 bands, and +$32.92 on the 11 days with 120 or more. Five of the 11 busiest days are festival days; the other six sit below the line (−$27 on average), because the festivals pull the line steeper. Fitted without them, the residuals still curve upward at the top (+$24 on the six busiest days), so the bend is real: tickets are larger on busy days, so sales grow a little faster than in proportion to transactions. The assumptions-and-diagnostics lesson reads such plots in full; the transformations-and-influence lesson shows what to do about curves.

What R² can’t tell you

Whether the line is the right shape. Penn State’s regression course puts it bluntly: a high R² does not mean the line fits well, and a perfect curved relationship can have an R² near zero. The iced-share line, at 0.883, describes a curve with a straight line and predicts impossible values. Always plot.

Whether the line is useful. At Harbourside, footfall on the day’s high gives R² = 0.190, a figure many people would dismiss. Yet the slope is 2.0 people per °F, and March days averaged 53.1 °F against 83.5 °F in July, so the line puts July’s typical footfall about 61 people above March’s, about one extra barista hour at the morning peak, even though on any single day the line misses by about s = 62 people, because weekends, rain and festivals matter as much as temperature. Rain is weaker still, R² = 0.052, with a slope of −81 people per inch. A low R² says the line leaves most day-to-day variation unexplained; it doesn’t say the slope is unimportant. Part of that 2.0 is the cafés’ opening build-up, which ran through the cold spring; module 3 holds it fixed and finds a smaller slope (1.79 with the build-up alone held fixed, 1.44 once weekends, rain and festivals are added).

Anything apart from the range of x it was computed on. R² compares the spread along the line with the total spread of y, and the total spread depends on how widely x varies. Fit Harbourside’s sales line only on the 121 days with 50 to 70 transactions and R² falls from 0.984 to 0.859, while the typical miss falls too, from $28.10 to $13.51. The line fits those days at least as closely; there is simply less variation in sales for it to account for. This is B05’s restricted-range effect on r, squared. Comparing R² across data sets with different ranges of x compares the ranges as much as the fits.

Causes, or accuracy on new data. R² measures association in the data used to fit the line. It doesn’t show that x causes y, and it is not predictive accuracy: a line fitted to one year can do worse on the next, especially when many explanatory variables are tried (the multicollinearity-and-model-choice lesson tests models on months they weren’t fitted to). Nor can R² be compared between models of different responses, such as sales and the logarithm of sales.

Note: Is 0.6 a “strong” R²?

Verbal labels for R² (or r) vary between textbooks and fields, and none is a standard; what counts as high in social science would be poor in a laboratory calibration. If you use a label, say whose convention it is. Better, report R² with the residual standard error in y’s units and let the decision decide whether the fit is good enough.

In Excel

With transactions in B2:B21, sales in C2:C21, the fitted values in E2:E21 and the residuals in F2:F21: =RSQ(C2:C21,B2:B21) gives R²; =STEYX(C2:C21,B2:B21) gives s; =DEVSQ(C2:C21) is SST; =SUMSQ(F2:F21) is SSE; and =1-SUMSQ(F2:F21)/DEVSQ(C2:C21) rebuilds R² from them. =LINEST(C2:C21,B2:B21,TRUE,TRUE) returns R² and s in its third row, and SSR and SSE in its fifth. A residual plot is a scatter chart of column F against column B (or against E); the ToolPak’s “Residual Plots” box draws it for you.

Common mistakes

  • “R² = 0.88, so the model is 88% accurate.”

    R² is the share of the variation of y around its mean that the line accounts for, not a hit rate or an error percentage. The iced-share line has R² = 0.883 and still misses by about 10 percentage points on a typical day. Report the residual standard error for accuracy in y’s units.

  • Trusting a high R² without a residual plot

    A straight line through a curve can have a high R²: the iced-share line’s residuals run positive, negative, positive across temperatures, and it predicts negative shares below 42.5 °F. Plot the residuals before believing any line.

  • “R² is low, so x doesn’t matter.”

    Harbourside’s footfall on the high has R² = 0.190, yet its slope of 2.0 people per °F puts July about 61 people above March. A low R² says much of the variation comes from elsewhere (weekends, rain), not that the slope is negligible.

  • Comparing R² across different ranges of x

    Restricting Harbourside’s days to 50–70 transactions drops R² from 0.984 to 0.859 while the typical miss halves. A study with a wider spread of x will show a higher R² for the same relationship.

  • Dividing SSE by n for the residual standard error

    Two coefficients have been estimated, so the residuals have n − 2 degrees of freedom: s = √(SSE ÷ (n − 2)), which is what STEYX computes. Dividing by n understates the typical miss, badly in small samples.

  • Reading “explained” as “caused”

    “Transactions explain 98% of the variation in sales” is a statement about squared deviations. It says nothing about what causes sales, and the same R² would come from regressing transactions on sales.

In practice

Fictional case

Ana’s review of the café models has one line per model. “Sales on transactions, Harbourside: R² = 0.984, typical miss $28 (STEYX), residuals bend gently upward at the top even without the festival days; fine for budgeting inside the usual range. Iced share on the forecast high: R² = 0.883, typical miss 10 points, but the residuals wave, so I order from last year’s averages by 5 °F band instead. Footfall on the high, Harbourside: R² = 0.19, typical miss 62 people; useless for any single day, useful for the season (once module 3 has separated the build-up).”

Each line gives three things: R² for the share of variation, s in the response’s units for the size of a typical miss, and a sentence about the residual plot. That combination is what lets a reader decide whether a model is good enough for a purpose. A budget can live with $28 a day; a barista roster for one particular Saturday can’t live with ±62 people, but a seasonal hiring plan can.

In other businesses the same habits apply. A retailer forecasting store revenue from floor area might find R² = 0.6 and a typical miss of $400,000 a year: the R² sounds respectable, the miss decides whether it can set targets with it. A battery-storage installer modelling installation hours on system size may see residuals fanning out for larger systems, a sign that a single s overstates precision on small jobs and understates it on big ones (module 4 deals with this). And a delivery firm comparing two cities’ models by R² should first check whether the cities’ routes cover the same range of distances.

Two cautions travel with every R² Ana reports. It is computed on the days used to fit the line, so it flatters how well the line will do on next year’s days. And it describes association: a high R² between baristas rostered and sales (B05 found they go together because the roster follows expected trade) would not mean that adding baristas raises sales.

Summary

  • A residual is e = y − ŷ, in y’s units; for least squares with an intercept the residuals add to zero and are uncorrelated with x.
  • SST = SSR + SSE: the total variation of y around its mean splits into the part along the line and the residual part.
  • R² = SSR ÷ SST = 1 − SSE ÷ SST is the share of the variation the line accounts for; with one explanatory variable it equals r². It is not accuracy and not causation.
  • The residual standard error s = √(SSE ÷ (n − 2)) (STEYX) is the typical miss in y’s units. Report it with R².
  • A residual plot shows whether a line is the right shape: the iced-share line has R² = 0.883 but its residuals wave and it predicts negative shares below 42.5 °F.
  • A low R² can go with a slope that matters (footfall: R² = 0.19, 2.0 people per °F).
  • R² depends on the range of x: Harbourside’s sales line on 50–70 transactions has R² = 0.859 against 0.984 for all days, with a smaller typical miss.

Terms introduced

Total sum of squares
SST = Σ(y − ȳ)²: the variation of the response around its mean, before any explanatory variable is used. Excel’s DEVSQ.
Regression sum of squares
SSR = Σ(ŷ − ȳ)²: the part of the total sum of squares that the fitted values account for. With least squares and an intercept, SSR = SST − SSE.
Residual sum of squares
SSE = Σ(y − ŷ)²: the sum of the squared residuals, the quantity least squares makes as small as possible. Also called the error sum of squares.
Residual plot
A scatterplot of a model’s residuals against an explanatory variable, the fitted values or the order of the observations. Residuals with no pattern around zero suggest the model’s form fits; a curve, a funnel or a trend says it doesn’t.
