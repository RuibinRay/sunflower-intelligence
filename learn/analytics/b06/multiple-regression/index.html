Multiple regression

Fit one equation with several predictors, read each slope as a comparison with the others held fixed, and see why a slope changes when a predictor joins the model. Then judge the whole model with adjusted R² and the overall F test, and read the output in Excel.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Interpret each slope of a multiple regression as the change in the fitted value per unit of its predictor, holding the other predictors fixed, with units.
  • Explain why a slope changes when a predictor is added or left out, and calculate the change with the omitted-variable formula.
  • Calculate adjusted R² and the overall F statistic from R², n and the number of predictors, and say what each tests or measures.
  • Read a multiple-regression Summary Output from the ToolPak and the coefficients LINEST returns for several x columns, in their reverse order.
  • Evaluate standardised slopes and a time trend left out of the model, and say when each misleads.

Review first

  • The least-squares line — The least-squares line, its slope and intercept, and why the criterion is the sum of squared residuals.
  • Residuals and R² — R², SST = SSR + SSE and the residual standard error, which carry over unchanged.
  • Inference for coefficients — The t test and interval for one slope, read here for each slope in turn.
  • Partial correlation — Holding a variable fixed with residuals, and the barista-hours and waits puzzle this lesson turns into a slope.
  • Descriptive statistics and regression in Excel — LINEST and the ToolPak Regression tool for one x, which this lesson extends to several.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana’s Harbourside manager wants to know what a warm day is worth. The least-squares line of footfall on the day’s high temperature over Harbourside’s 306 days has a slope of 2.02: each extra °F goes with about two more people through the door. But warm days at the waterfront differ in other ways too. The five harbour-festival days all fell in late May, July and August, and on those days the crowds had nothing to do with the thermometer. Ana wants the warm-day figure with those other differences taken out.

One equation, several predictors

A multiple regression predicts y from several predictors at once: y^=b0+b1x1+b2x2+⋯+bkxk\hat y = b_0 + b_1x_1 + b_2x_2 + \dots + b_kx_ky^​=b0​+b1​x1​+b2​x2​+⋯+bk​xk​. The coefficients are chosen by the same rule as for one predictor, least squares: they make the sum of squared residuals, SSE=∑(y−y^)2\text{SSE} = \sum (y - \hat y)^2SSE=∑(y−y^​)2, as small as possible. With two predictors the fitted values lie on a flat plane above the (x₁, x₂) grid rather than on a line; with more, no picture helps, but the arithmetic is the same and software does it.

For Harbourside, Ana fits footfall on four predictors: the day’s high (°F), the day’s rain (inches), a weekend column that is 1 on Saturdays and Sundays and 0 otherwise, and a festival column that is 1 on the five festival days. The table shows the high temperature’s slope as predictors join the model one model at a time.

Read the third model’s equation as the line for days that are alike on everything else:

Its slope on the high, 1.66, says that among days with the same rain, the same weekend-or-weekday status and the same festival status, each extra °F goes with about 1.66 more people. The rain slope says that, holding the temperature, the day of the week and festivals fixed, each inch of rain goes with about 76 fewer people. The weekend slope compares a Saturday or Sunday with a weekday of the same weather: about 69 more people. A slope in a multiple regression is a partial slope: it answers its question only for days that match on the other predictors.

Key idea: Holding the other predictors fixed

Every slope in a multiple regression is a comparison between rows that are alike on the other predictors in the model. Change the other predictors and you change the question, so the slope can change too. Say which predictors were held fixed whenever you quote one.

Why the slope changed

The high temperature’s slope fell from 2.02 to 1.66 when the weekend and festival columns joined. The festival days explain most of it: their highs were 77–84°F against an average of 63.9°F on the other days, and their crowds were about 330 people above a normal day. With temperature alone in the model, the line has to account for those hot, packed days, so it tilts up: part of the festival effect is credited to the weather. Fitting high and festival alone gives a slope of 1.55. The weekend column changes the slope hardly at all, because Saturdays are no warmer than Wednesdays.

This is omitted-variable bias: a slope estimated without a predictor that both affects y and goes with the included x absorbs part of that predictor’s effect. It is the regression version of a confounding variable, and it is the same thing the partial-correlation lesson did with residuals. A multiple-regression slope can even reverse sign, as the next example shows.

Baristas and waits, as slopes

B05 found that peak barista hours and peak waits correlate at +0.53 over the 612 café-days, but at −0.72 holding transactions fixed. The regression of the peak wait on barista hours alone has a slope of +0.548: days with an extra barista hour had waits about half a minute longer. Add transactions as a second predictor and the slope on barista hours becomes −1.264: on days with the same number of transactions, an extra barista hour goes with a peak wait about 1.26 minutes shorter. The t statistic for that slope is −25.95 on 609 degrees of freedom, exactly the t of B05’s partial correlation; the two methods ask the same question and share one test.

The omitted-variable formula shows where the +0.548 came from. Let δ\deltaδ be the slope of the omitted predictor (transactions) on the included one (barista hours): 23.72 transactions per barista hour, because managers roster more baristas on busier days. Then the short regression’s slope is the long one’s plus the omitted predictor’s slope times δ\deltaδ:

b~1=b1+b2 δ=−1.264+0.0764×23.72=0.548\tilde b_1 = b_1 + b_2\,\delta = -1.264 + 0.0764 \times 23.72 = 0.548b~1​=b1​+b2​δ=−1.264+0.0764×23.72=0.548

The identity is exact for least squares, not an approximation. Barista hours look harmful alone because they carry the busy-day effect of transactions with them. The case’s generator planted this: waits depend on transactions per barista hour, and managers roster from the trade they expect. The waits rise along a curve rather than a line (B05 found the steep rise beyond about seven transactions per barista hour), so −1.264 is an average slope; the transformations-and-influence lesson fits the curve.

In depth

The model

The population model behind a multiple regression is y=β0+β1x1+⋯+βkxk+εy = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_1 + \dots + \beta_kx_k + \varepsilony=β0​+β1​x1​+⋯+βk​xk​+ε, with errors ε\varepsilonε that average zero at every combination of the x’s. The fitted coefficients b0,…,bkb_0, \dots, b_kb0​,…,bk​ estimate the β\betaβ’s. There are p=k+1p = k + 1p=k+1 coefficients counting the intercept, and the residual standard error is s=SSE/(n−p)s = \sqrt{\text{SSE}/(n - p)}s=SSE/(n−p)​, in y’s units (the standard error of the regression). The conditions for the t tests and intervals are those of the inference-for-coefficients lesson, applied to the model as a whole; the assumptions-and-diagnostics lesson checks them.

The model is additive: each predictor’s slope is the same whatever the values of the others. On a weekend and on a weekday the high temperature is assumed to add the same 1.66 people per °F. When that is false (the cafés respond differently to the weekend, for example) the model needs an interaction term, which the next lesson adds.

A partial slope is a slope of residuals

There is a concrete way to see “holding the others fixed”. Regress y on all the other predictors and keep the residuals; regress x1x_1x1​ on the same predictors and keep its residuals. The least-squares slope of the first set of residuals on the second is exactly b1b_1b1​ from the multiple regression (a result known as the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem). The residuals of x1x_1x1​ are the part of x1x_1x1​ the other predictors can’t predict, so b1b_1b1​ is estimated only from that part. B05’s partial correlation is the correlation of the same two sets of residuals; the partial slope is their slope, in y’s units per unit of x1x_1x1​.

This explains a practical rule. A slope can be estimated well only if its predictor varies once the others are held fixed. If a predictor is almost a copy of the others, its residuals are tiny and its slope is poorly determined: that is multicollinearity, the subject of the multicollinearity-and-model-choice lesson.

Omitted-variable bias

Suppose the true model contains x1x_1x1​ and x2x_2x2​, and you fit x1x_1x1​ alone. The short slope is b~1=b1+b2δ\tilde b_1 = b_1 + b_2\deltab~1​=b1​+b2​δ, where δ\deltaδ is the slope of x2x_2x2​ on x1x_1x1​. Two conditions make the bias: x2x_2x2​ must matter for y (b2≠0b_2 \neq 0b2​=0) and it must go with x1x_1x1​ (δ≠0\delta \neq 0δ=0). Its direction is the sign of b2×δb_2 \times \deltab2​×δ: festivals add footfall and go with warm days, so leaving them out pushes the temperature slope up. Leaving out something unrelated to the included predictors, like the weekend column for the temperature slope, costs precision (a larger s) but causes no bias.

Not every extra predictor reduces bias. Adding a variable that lies on the path from x to y (a mediator, in B05’s terms) removes part of the effect you want; adding one caused by both x and y (a collider) creates a false link. The choice of predictors is a judgement about how the business works, which the multicollinearity-and-model-choice lesson takes further.

A trend left out

New cafés take time to find their customers. The generator planted a build-up: each café’s trade starts at 72% of its eventual level on 1 March and reaches 100% after four months. The weather follows the season, and March and April were both cold and quiet; so a model without the build-up credits some of the build-up to temperature. The third model’s residuals show it: they average −21.5 people in March and −13.8 in April, and +9.5 to +11.4 from October to December. The model over-predicts the early months and under-predicts the late ones.

The fourth model adds a build-up column that rises evenly from 0 on 1 March to 1 on 29 June and stays at 1, the shape the generator used. Its slope, 35.5 people, is the build-up’s size; the temperature slope falls to 1.44, and the monthly residual averages shrink to within ±4 people. The planted per-°F effect, averaged over Harbourside’s days, is 1.46 people, so the fourth model recovers it and the third overstates it by about 13%. With real data no one tells you the trend’s shape. A straight-line “day” column is the wrong shape here and makes things worse (the temperature slope rises to 1.79); plotting residuals in time order, as the assumptions-and-diagnostics lesson does, is how you find out what is missing.

Caution: Rows that aren’t independent

The standard errors in every model here treat Harbourside’s 306 days as independent. Neighbouring days share the season and the build-up, so they aren’t: the third model’s residuals have a lag-1 autocorrelation of 0.22, which makes its standard errors too small and its p-values too small. Adding the build-up brings it to −0.08. Models that pool both cafés have a second problem, since the two rows of a day share its weather.

R² and adjusted R²

R² keeps its meaning, the share of the variation in y around its mean that the fitted values account for: R2=1−SSE/SSTR^2 = 1 - \text{SSE}/\text{SST}R2=1−SSE/SST. It can never fall when a predictor is added, because least squares can always set the new slope to zero and do no worse. So R² rewards adding anything, even a column of random numbers. Adjusted R-squared charges for each coefficient:

Rˉ2=1−(1−R2) n−1n−p\bar R^2 = 1 - (1 - R^2)\,\dfrac{n - 1}{n - p}Rˉ2=1−(1−R2)n−pn−1​

For the third model, 1−(1−0.9221)×305/301=0.92111 - (1 - 0.9221) \times 305/301 = 0.92111−(1−0.9221)×305/301=0.9211. Adjusted R² rises when a new predictor’s t statistic is larger than 1 in size and falls otherwise, so it can go down. It is a rough guard against padding a model, not a test, and it is not a share of anything: it can even be negative. The next lesson but one compares models more carefully.

The overall F test

Each slope’s t test asks whether that predictor adds anything, holding the others fixed. The overall F test asks whether the predictors together add anything at all: H0:β1=β2=⋯=βk=0H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \dots = \beta_k = 0H0​:β1​=β2​=⋯=βk​=0. Its statistic compares the variation explained per predictor with the residual variation per degree of freedom:

F=SSR/kSSE/(n−p)=R2/k(1−R2)/(n−p)F = \dfrac{\text{SSR}/k}{\text{SSE}/(n - p)} = \dfrac{R^2/k}{(1 - R^2)/(n - p)}F=SSE/(n−p)SSR/k​=(1−R2)/(n−p)R2/k​

on k and n − p degrees of freedom. For the third model, F=(0.9221/4)÷(0.0779/301)=891F = (0.9221/4) \div (0.0779/301) = 891F=(0.9221/4)÷(0.0779/301)=891 on 4 and 301 df, and the p-value (Significance F in Excel) is far below 0.001. With one predictor F is the slope’s t squared; with several it is a single test of all of them. The two kinds of test can disagree: when two predictors carry the same information, F can be large while neither t is, because the data show that something matters but can’t say which. The general F test that compares any two nested models is in the multicollinearity-and-model-choice lesson.

Reading the output in Excel

The ToolPak’s Regression tool takes an Input X Range of several adjacent columns and prints the same three blocks as for one predictor: Regression Statistics (Multiple R, R Square, Adjusted R Square, Standard Error, Observations), the ANOVA table (Regression, Residual and Total rows with df, SS, MS, F and Significance F) and one row of coefficients per predictor with its standard error, t Stat, P-value and 95% limits. The Explore widget prints it for any set of Harbourside’s predictors.

LINEST accepts several x columns too, but returns the coefficients in reverse order: for x’s in columns C, D and E, =LINEST(B2:B31,C2:E31) spills bE,bD,bC,b0b_E, b_D, b_C, b_0bE​,bD​,bC​,b0​ across one row (Microsoft’s documentation states the order as mn, mn−1, …, m1, b). To pick out the slope on column C, take the third entry: =INDEX(LINEST(B2:B31,C2:E31),1,3). With stats set to TRUE, row 2 holds the standard errors in the same order, row 3 R² and s, row 4 F and the residual df, and row 5 SSR and SSE. TREND with several x columns predicts from the fitted equation: =TREND(B2:B31,C2:E31,G2:I2) for a new day whose x values are in G2:I2.

Standardised slopes, with care

Slopes come in their predictors’ units (people per °F, per inch, per festival), so their sizes can’t be compared directly. A standardised slope rescales each to standard deviations, bj∗=bj sxj/syb_j^{*} = b_j\,s_{x_j}/s_ybj∗​=bj​sxj​​/sy​: the change in y, in SDs of y, per SD of xjx_jxj​. For the fourth model they are 0.31 for the high, −0.21 for rain, 0.45 for the weekend, 0.61 for the festival and 0.16 for the build-up.

They are easy to over-read. A 0/1 column’s SD depends on how common the 1s are, not on any “typical change”: the festival column’s SD is only 0.13 because festivals are rare, and its 0.61 means 330 people times that small SD. Rain’s SD is inflated by a few wet days, since most days are dry. And standardising uses this sample’s spreads, so the same effect gets a different standardised slope in a sample with a narrower range. Use them to describe one sample, not to rank causes.

Common mistakes

  • “The slope on temperature is the effect of one more degree.”

    It is the difference in fitted footfall per °F between days alike on the other predictors in this model. Leave out the build-up and it becomes 1.66; leave out everything but temperature and it becomes 2.02. A coefficient is an association with the others held fixed; it becomes an effect only with an argument that nothing important is left out.

  • “Adding a predictor can only make the other slopes more accurate.”

    It removes omitted-variable bias only when the new predictor is a confounder. A mediator (on the path from x to y) or a collider (affected by both) makes the slope answer a different question or creates a link that isn’t there. Choose predictors from how the business works.

  • “R² went up, so the new predictor belongs in the model.”

    R² can’t go down when a predictor is added, whatever it is. Adjusted R² charges for each coefficient, and the t test asks whether the predictor adds anything given the others. Neither replaces judgement about what the model is for.

  • “The first coefficient LINEST returns belongs to the first x column.”

    With several x columns LINEST returns them in reverse order: the last column’s slope first and the intercept last. INDEX(LINEST(…),1,k + 1 − j) is the slope on the j-th of k columns. The ToolPak prints them in the original order with their names, which is why it is easier to read.

  • “The largest standardised slope is the most important predictor.”

    Standardised slopes depend on the sample’s spreads, and for 0/1 or skewed predictors the SD isn’t a meaningful change. Harbourside’s festival column has the largest standardised slope, but only five days in the year. Compare slopes in their own units against changes that are realistic for the decision.

  • “The p-values are tiny, so the slopes are certain.”

    The standard errors assume independent rows. Neighbouring days share the season and the cafés share their weather, so the standard errors are too small; a time trend left out (the build-up) makes residuals run in streaks. Check the residuals in time order before trusting the third decimal place.

In practice

Multiple regression is the everyday tool for “what goes with what, other things equal”. A retailer estimates how much a promotion adds to a store’s weekly sales holding the store’s size, the season and local holidays fixed. A hotel asks how much an extra night of advance booking is worth at the same room type and day of the week. An energy supplier models daily demand on temperature, the day of the week and public holidays, because each of those changes demand and they go together through the year. In each case the analyst’s main work is choosing what to hold fixed and checking what was left out; the arithmetic is the easy part.

Fictional case

Ana’s note to the Harbourside manager: “Over 2027, each extra °F went with about 1.4 more people through the door, comparing days with the same rain, day of the week and festival status, after allowing for the café’s first four months of build-up (model with five predictors, R² = 0.94, s = 16 people). The simple line through footfall and temperature gives 2.0 per °F, because it also credits the weather with the festival crowds and with the quiet, cold spring when the café was new. Rain costs about 76 people per inch and a weekend adds about 69. These are associations in observational data, and the standard errors are somewhat too small because neighbouring days are alike.”

Her note does what a good regression report does. It gives the slope with its units and with the list of what was held fixed. It sets the multiple-regression slope beside the simple one and explains the gap in terms of the business, not the algebra. It reports the fit (R² and s) so the reader knows how much the model leaves unexplained, and it flags what could make the standard errors misleading. It doesn’t rank the predictors by standardised slopes or claim that warm weather “drives” 1.4 extra people; it says what the data show for days that were alike.

The same habits carry into the rest of the module. The categorical-predictors-and-interactions lesson lets the weekend’s effect differ between the two cafés, which pooling them hides. The multicollinearity-and-model-choice lesson asks what happens when two predictors carry nearly the same information, such as footfall and transactions, and how to choose between models with more and fewer terms without fooling yourself.

Summary

  • A multiple regression ŷ = b₀ + b₁x₁ + … + b_kx_k is fitted by least squares; each slope is the change in ŷ per unit of its predictor, holding the other predictors fixed.
  • A partial slope equals the slope of y’s residuals on x_j’s residuals after both are regressed on the other predictors: the same idea as B05’s partial correlation, in y’s units.
  • Leaving out a predictor that matters and goes with an included one biases its slope: b̃₁ = b₁ + b₂δ, exactly. Barista hours’ slope on waits is +0.548 alone and −1.264 holding transactions fixed.
  • A time trend can be the omitted predictor: without the build-up, Harbourside’s temperature slope is 1.66 people per °F; with it, 1.44, close to the planted 1.46.
  • R² never falls when a predictor is added; adjusted R² = 1 − (1 − R²)(n − 1)/(n − p) charges for each coefficient.
  • The overall F test, F = (R²/k) ÷ ((1 − R²)/(n − p)) on k and n − p df, tests whether all the slopes are zero; each t test asks about one slope given the others.
  • LINEST returns several slopes in reverse order; the ToolPak prints them in order with their names. Standardised slopes describe one sample and mislead for 0/1 or skewed predictors.

Terms introduced

Multiple regression
A least-squares model that predicts y from two or more predictors at once, ŷ = b₀ + b₁x₁ + … + b_kx_k.
Partial slope
A slope in a multiple regression: the change in the fitted value of y per unit of one predictor, holding the other predictors in the model fixed. Also called a partial regression coefficient.
Omitted-variable bias
The change in a slope caused by leaving out a predictor that affects y and goes with the included predictor; for least squares the short slope is b₁ + b₂δ, δ being the slope of the omitted predictor on the included one.
Adjusted R-squared
R² adjusted for the number of coefficients, 1 − (1 − R²)(n − 1)/(n − p). It falls when a predictor adds too little, and can be negative.
Overall F test
The test of a regression model’s null hypothesis that every slope is zero, F = (SSR/k) ÷ (SSE/(n − p)) on k and n − p degrees of freedom; Significance F in Excel’s output.
Standardised slope
A slope rescaled to standard-deviation units, b_j × s(x_j) ÷ s(y): the change in y, in SDs, per SD of the predictor in the sample.
