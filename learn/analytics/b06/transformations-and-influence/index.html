Transformations and influence

When the diagnostics fail, change the model, not the data. Fit footfall in logs and read its slopes as percentages, bend a line with polynomial and hinge terms, and find the few rows that move a regression, then decide what to do with them honestly.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Transform y with a natural log, interpret the coefficients as percentage changes (approximately 100b, exactly 100(eᵇ − 1)) and back-transform a prediction and its interval.
  • Use polynomial and hinge terms to follow a curve or a threshold, and choose between them with residual plots and fit statistics.
  • Calculate leverage, studentized residuals and Cook’s distance, and use them to tell outliers, high-leverage points and influential points apart.
  • Handle errors and genuine extremes correctly: check the source, correct, report with and without, and never delete a point because it spoils a result.

Review first

  • Assumptions and diagnostics — The residual plots and tests that show when a model needs changing: the funnel and the drift this lesson repairs.
  • Categorical predictors and interactions — Dummy variables, used here for the festival days.
  • Curves, thresholds and restricted ranges — The threshold in the peak wait and the hinge transformation, met there as correlations.
  • Outliers and influence — The footfall slip and the festival days, and leave-one-out influence on a correlation.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

The diagnostics left Ana with three problems. Harbourside’s footfall model has a funnel: busy days vary by more people than quiet days, so a single prediction interval is wrong for both. The peak wait doesn’t rise in a straight line with the load on the baristas. And her raw log still contains the slip she found in B05, Millbrook’s 14 September footfall typed as 2,566 instead of 256, along with five festival days that are real but extreme. Each calls for a different response.

Logs: when effects are percentages

Footfall grows by percentages. A weekend adds a share of an ordinary day’s trade, not a fixed number of people, and the same is true of a warm day or a festival. When effects multiply like this, a model of footfall itself fits a straight-line sum to a product, and the residuals fan out as the level rises. Taking the natural log transformation of y turns products into sums: if footfall = base × weekend effect × weather effect, then ln(footfall) = ln(base) + ln(weekend effect) + ln(weather effect), which is exactly the form a regression fits.

Ana refits Harbourside’s model with ln(footfall) as y and the same predictors (the high, rain, weekend, festival and the day count). The funnel goes: Breusch–Pagan falls from LM = 67.0 in levels to 6.7 in logs, a p-value of 0.24. The time-order plot shows one more thing. With a straight-line day count, the log model’s residuals average −0.052 in March, +0.036 in June and −0.027 in December: an arch. The café’s build-up was not steady all year; it stopped. A day count that rises to 121 (29 June, the end of the first four months) and then stays flat removes the arch: the residuals’ monthly averages all lie within ±0.017, Durbin–Watson is 1.97, and Breusch–Pagan gives LM = 5.4 (p = 0.37).

Reading a log model’s coefficients

In a model for ln⁡y\ln ylny, a slope b is the change in ln y for a one-unit change in x, holding the other predictors fixed. Since a change of b in a log multiplies y by ebe^beb, the slope means a change of 100(eb−1)100(e^b - 1)100(eb−1)%. For small b this is close to 100b%: the slope for the day’s high, 0.0109, means about 1.1% more people per °F warmer, holding rain, the weekend, festivals and the build-up fixed. For larger b the shortcut fails. The weekend coefficient, 0.469, does not mean 47% more people: e0.469−1=0.598e^{0.469} - 1 = 0.598e0.469−1=0.598, so weekend days have about 60% more footfall than weekdays with the same weather. The festival coefficient, 0.891, means e0.891=2.44e^{0.891} = 2.44e0.891=2.44 times an ordinary weekend day’s footfall.

These are the numbers the café’s fictional log was built from, recovered: footfall at Harbourside was generated with 1.1% more per °F near 60°F, a weekend base 61% above the weekday one, festival days at 2.3 times normal (inside the coefficient’s 95% interval, 2.23 to 2.66) and a build-up from 72% to full trade over four months. The levels model could not express any of these as single numbers, because in levels the weekend adds more people on a warm day than on a cold one.

Bending a line: polynomial and hinge terms

The peak wait rises with the load, the peak transactions per barista hour, but not in a straight line. B05 found the shape: waits barely change up to a load of about 7 and climb ever faster beyond it. A regression can follow a curve by adding a transformed copy of x as another predictor. A polynomial regression adds x², and perhaps x³. A hinge transformation adds max(0, x − 7), which is zero up to the threshold and rises after it; squaring it, max(0, x − 7)², lets the rise steepen.

Across 612 café-days the straight line accounts for 68.9% of the variation in waits, the quadratic 94.3% and the squared hinge 96.1%. The squared hinge’s coefficient, 0.226 minutes per squared transaction above 7, is close to the 0.22 the fictional data were built with. At a load of 10 the model predicts a wait of 3.7 minutes, at 12 a wait of 7.3. The quadratic’s good R² hides a fault the figure shows: a parabola must turn somewhere, and this one has its minimum at a load of 6.0, so it predicts that waits rise again as the load falls, 4.0 minutes at a load of 2, where the 11 days with a load below 4 averaged 1.8.

Influence: the rows that move a regression

In B05 one mistyped footfall cut the correlation between footfall and transactions from 0.981 to 0.679. In a regression the same day does more visible damage. With the slip, the least-squares slope of transactions on footfall across all 612 café-days is 0.260 transactions per person through the door; corrected, it is 0.560. One row among 612 more than halved the slope.

Three numbers say why. The slip’s footfall, 2,566, lies so far from the mean that its leverage (in regression) is 0.551: more than half of its own fitted value is set by its own y. Its residual from the line fitted with it is −583.5 transactions, and its studentized residual, the residual divided by the standard deviation it should have, is −23.8 (−91.2 when the standard deviation is estimated without it). Cook’s distance, which combines the two into the change in all the fitted values when the row is left out, is 349; the conventional point at which to worry is 1. A point that is far out in x and far from the line is influential; a point that is only one of those may not be.

In depth

The log transformation in detail

Logs need y > 0; for counts that can be zero, B02’s ln⁡(1+y)\ln(1 + y)ln(1+y) is a common workaround, though its coefficients no longer read as percentages. Logging y changes three things at once: the form (effects multiply), the spread (errors proportional to the level become constant in logs) and the shape of the errors (a right skew in y often becomes symmetric in ln y). Check all three after the change; here the residual standard deviation in logs, 0.098, means a typical day is within about 10% of its predicted footfall, busy or quiet.

Logs can go on x instead, or on both. With ln⁡x\ln xlnx as the predictor, a 1% rise in x goes with a change of about b/100 in y. With logs on both sides, b is an elasticity: a 1% rise in x goes with about b% change in y. Choose the scale from how the effect works (fixed amounts or percentages) and from the residual plots, not from which version has the larger R².

Caution: Don’t compare R² across a change of y

The levels model has R² = 0.943 and the log model 0.939. They measure different things: the share of the variation in footfall, and in ln(footfall). Compare models with different y by their residual plots, or by their errors on a common scale (back-transform the log model’s predictions and compare errors in people).

Back-transforming a prediction

A log model predicts ln⁡y^\widehat{\ln y}lny​. Exponentiating gives eln⁡y^e^{\widehat{\ln y}}elny​, which estimates the median footfall for that day, not the mean, because the exponential stretches the upper half of the errors more than the lower. To estimate the mean, multiply by a correction: Duan’s smearing factor, the average of eeie^{e_i}eei​ over the log model’s residuals, or es2/2e^{s^2/2}es2/2 if the errors are normal. For Harbourside both are 1.005, a 0.5% correction, small because s is small; with s = 0.5 it would be 13%.

Prediction intervals back-transform directly: exponentiate the two limits. The result is not symmetric about the prediction, and it widens with the level, which is the point. For a dry 75°F Saturday after the build-up the log model predicts a median of 227 people, with a 95% prediction interval from 187 to 276. For a dry 45°F Tuesday it predicts 103, from 85 to 124. The levels model gives each day the same ±33 people, too narrow for the Saturday and too wide for the Tuesday.

A polynomial is quick to try, but its shape is global: a squared term bends the whole curve, including where the data are flat, and higher powers swing wildly at the ends of the data. A hinge is local: it leaves the line alone below the threshold and changes it only above. The price is choosing the threshold. Ana’s came from B05’s bands, chosen before this fit; trying thresholds from 5 to 9 gives R² from 0.930 to 0.961, highest at 7, so the data agree. Choosing a threshold by searching many values and picking the best fit overstates how well the model will do on new data; if you search, say so, and check on data not used to choose it.

Interpret transformed terms through predictions, not single coefficients. In the squared-hinge model the effect of one more transaction per barista hour depends on where you start: below 7 it is the load slope, −0.005 minutes (indistinguishable from zero); at a load of 10 it is −0.005+2×0.226×3=1.35-0.005 + 2 \times 0.226 \times 3 = 1.35−0.005+2×0.226×3=1.35 minutes. The squared term on its own is not “the effect of load”.

Leverage

Leverage measures how far a row’s predictor values are from the centre of all the rows’. With one predictor, hi=1/n+(xi−xˉ)2/∑(x−xˉ)2h_i = 1/n + (x_i - \bar x)^2 / \sum (x - \bar x)^2hi​=1/n+(xi​−xˉ)2/∑(x−xˉ)2. Leverages lie between 1/n and 1 and average p/n, where p counts the coefficients; a common rule of thumb flags rows above 2p/n (here 2 × 2 ÷ 612 = 0.0065). For the slip, h=1/612+(2566−186.8)2/10,305,017=0.551h = 1/612 + (2566 - 186.8)^2 / 10{,}305{,}017 = 0.551h=1/612+(2566−186.8)2/10,305,017=0.551, 84 times the flag. With several predictors leverage comes from the hat matrix, but the idea is the same: an unusual combination of x values, such as a festival day on a weekend with heavy rain, can have high leverage even if no single value is extreme.

Studentized residuals

Residuals of high-leverage rows are small by construction, because the line is pulled towards them. Dividing by the standard deviation each residual should have puts them on one scale: the internally studentized residual is ri=ei/(s1−hi)r_i = e_i / (s\sqrt{1 - h_i})ri​=ei​/(s1−hi​​). The externally studentized (deleted) version, tit_iti​, uses s from the fit without row i, so a large error can’t hide by inflating s; it follows a t distribution on n − p − 1 df if the model is right. Values beyond ±3 are worth checking, beyond ±2 worth a look in small samples; Penn State’s regression notes treat both as warning flags, not rules.

Cook’s distance

Cook’s distance asks how much all the fitted values would move if row i were left out, scaled by the model’s precision: Di=ri2p⋅hi1−hiD_i = \dfrac{r_i^2}{p} \cdot \dfrac{h_i}{1 - h_i}Di​=pri2​​⋅1−hi​hi​​. It is large only when both the residual and the leverage are. Rules of thumb vary: Penn State’s notes suggest a closer look above 0.5 and call values above 1 quite likely influential; others use 4/n. The most useful check is visual, a value that stands far apart from the rest.

The festival days show why both parts matter. In a log model of Harbourside’s footfall without a festival term, the five festival days have studentized residuals from 5.0 to 7.4, extreme outliers. But they are ordinary weekend days in their predictor values, with leverages from 0.015 to 0.024, and their Cook’s distances are 0.08 to 0.20: above the 4/n line (0.013), far below 0.5. They still matter: all five fell on weekends, and leaving them in without a term raises the weekend coefficient from 0.469 to 0.521 (60% to 68% more people). Once corrected, the most influential rows in the footfall-and-transactions regression are the same festival days (29 May has the largest Cook’s distance, 0.56): their footfall is high, and they sit below the pooled line because Harbourside converts about 47% of visitors where Millbrook converts 55%, a sign that the pooled model needs a café term.

What to do with an unusual point

  1. Check it against the source. The door counter’s export showed 256 for 14 September: an error. Correct it, and record the change.
  2. If it is real, ask whether the model is missing something. The festival days are real and different. A festival dummy gives them their own coefficient; the other coefficients are then almost exactly those from leaving the five days out (weekend 0.4691 either way), and the model still describes them.
  3. Report with and without. When a real point moves a conclusion and no term explains it, show both fits and say which conclusions depend on it.
  4. Never delete a point because it spoils the result, and don’t delete automatically by a cut-off. Rules such as |t| > 3 or D > 1 tell you where to look, not what to remove.

Key idea: Influence is not error

A point can be influential and correct (a festival day), or an error with little influence (a mistyped footfall near the middle of the data). Diagnostics find the points that matter; only the source can say whether they are right.

Common mistakes

  • “The log coefficient is 0.47, so weekends have 47% more people.”

    The shortcut 100b works only for small b. The exact change is 100(e^b − 1): e^0.469 − 1 = 60%. For negative coefficients the shortcut overstates the fall: rain’s −0.784 per inch is a 54% fall, not 78%.

  • Exponentiating a log prediction and calling it the mean

    e to the predicted log is an estimate of the median. Totals and averages built from it run low; multiply by a smearing factor (the mean of e to the residuals). For Harbourside it is 1.005; for noisier data it can be much larger.

  • Choosing between y and ln y by R²

    The two R²s measure the share of variation in different quantities. Decide by the residual plots and by errors on a common scale, and by whether the effects work as amounts or as percentages.

  • Reaching for a high-degree polynomial

    A polynomial bends everywhere. The quadratic for the wait has R² = 0.943 and still predicts that waits rise as the load falls below 6. A hinge at a threshold you can justify follows the data with fewer surprises; extrapolate neither.

  • “It has a huge residual, so it’s influential”, or “It has high leverage, so delete it”

    Influence needs both: the festival days’ studentized residuals of 5 to 7 come with Cook’s distances of 0.2 or less, and a high-leverage point that sits on the line changes nothing. Measure with Cook’s distance, then go to the source.

  • Deleting the points that spoil the result

    Points removed because the result looks better without them make every later p-value and interval misleading. Correct errors from the source, model real differences (a festival dummy), and report with and without when a real point decides a conclusion.

In practice

Fictional case

Ana’s change record for the 2027 log: “Millbrook, 14 Sep, footfall typed 2,566, door counter says 256: corrected (Cook’s D for the raw value 349). Festival days (5, Harbourside): real, kept, modelled with a dummy; without it the weekend effect reads 68% instead of 60%. Harbourside footfall now modelled in logs with a build-up term (to 29 June): residuals pass the spread and time-order checks; coefficients reported as percentages; roster limits from the log model’s prediction intervals.”

Records like this are routine wherever regression output drives money. A pricing analyst at an online retailer, say, fits demand in logs so that price effects come out as elasticities, checks the influence of the week a competitor’s site was down, and files a note saying which weeks were corrected, which were modelled and which were left as they are. The note matters as much as the model: anyone who reruns the analysis, or audits it, can see that no row was dropped to get a better answer.

Two habits separate a careful analyst from a hasty one. The first is choosing transformations for reasons that would hold before seeing the fit: effects that are percentages, a threshold known from the operation, a build-up known to have happened. Transformations chosen by trying many and keeping the best fit look better than they are on the next year’s data. The second is reporting results in the units a decision-maker uses. A log coefficient of 0.0109 means nothing to a café manager; “about 1% more people for every degree warmer” does, and so does “plan for 276 people on a warm Saturday”.

Finally, the influence checks belong at the start of an analysis as well as the end. Plotting the data and screening for leverage before fitting would have caught the slip in minutes. Done last, after a model has been presented, the same check becomes an awkward correction.

Summary

  • When effects are percentages, model ln y: products become sums, a funnel often disappears (Harbourside: Breusch–Pagan 67.0 in levels, 6.7 in logs), and coefficients read as percentage changes, exactly 100(eᵇ − 1), about 100b when b is small.
  • Back-transform a log prediction with eˣ to get a median; multiply by a smearing factor for a mean; exponentiate the interval’s limits, which then widen with the level.
  • Polynomial terms bend the whole curve; hinge terms, max(0, x − c) or its square, change it only beyond a threshold. The wait’s squared hinge at 7 has R² = 0.961 against 0.689 for a line, and a coefficient close to the planted 0.22.
  • Leverage measures how unusual a row’s x values are (average p/n); studentized residuals measure how far it is from the line on a common scale; Cook’s distance combines them into influence.
  • The raw slip had leverage 0.551, studentized residual −23.8 and Cook’s distance 349, and halved the slope; the festival days are outliers with little leverage and Cook’s distances of 0.2 or less, but still shift the weekend coefficient.
  • Check unusual points against the source; correct errors; model real differences; report with and without; never delete a point to improve a result.

Terms introduced

Polynomial regression
A regression that includes powers of a predictor (x², x³, …) as extra predictors so the fitted relationship can curve; the curve’s shape is set over the whole range of x at once.
Leverage (in regression)
How far a row’s predictor values are from the centre of all the rows’, measured by its hat value hᵢ between 1/n and 1. High-leverage rows pull the fitted line towards themselves.
Studentized residual
A residual divided by the standard deviation it should have given its leverage, so that residuals can be compared on one scale; the externally studentized version estimates that standard deviation without the row itself.
Cook’s distance
A measure of a row’s influence on a regression: how far all the fitted values move when the row is left out, scaled by the model’s precision. It is large only when a row has both a large residual and high leverage.
Smearing estimate
A correction for back-transforming predictions from a model of ln y: multiply e to the predicted log by the average of e to the residuals, to estimate the mean of y rather than its median.
