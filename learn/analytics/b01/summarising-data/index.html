Summarising data: changes, averages and spread

Most business numbers are summaries: a share, a change, an average, a growth rate. Learn to calculate each one correctly, to say percentage points when you mean them, to choose between the mean and the median, to describe spread, to weight an average properly and to round without misleading.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate proportions, percentages, absolute changes and percentage changes, and name the base of each.
  • Distinguish a percentage-point change from a percentage change and calculate both.
  • Calculate month-on-month growth rates and the compound average growth rate over several periods.
  • Calculate the mean, median and mode, and describe spread with the range, the interquartile range and the standard deviation, choosing the summary that suits the data and the question.
  • Calculate a weighted average, and explain why a shop’s overall conversion rate is a weighted average of its channels’ rates.
  • State results to a precision the data support, rounding only at the end.

Review first

  • KPIs: measuring what matters — The KPIs are the rates and averages this lesson summarises and compares.
  • Types of data — The level of measurement decides which average is meaningful.
  • Interest and inflation — Compound growth works for visits and subscribers as it does for money.

The idea

Fictional case

In January 2027 Ana’s assistant drafts a note on December: “Visits up 19.1% on November. Gift-box orders up 162.5%, and gift boxes were 39.6% of December’s orders. Average rating up 0.1.”

Every figure is a summary: a change, a percentage, an average. Each is calculated correctly, yet a reader could still misunderstand most of them. This lesson is about producing summaries that cannot be misread.

Counts, proportions and percentages

A count says how many. A proportion divides a part by the whole it belongs to, giving a number between 0 and 1; a percentage is the proportion multiplied by 100. In December Lantern took 583 orders, of which 231 were gift boxes, so gift boxes were a proportion 231÷583≈0.396231 \div 583 \approx 0.396231÷583≈0.396, or 39.6%, of orders.

A percentage means nothing until you know its whole. Gift boxes were 39.6% of December’s orders; as a share of December’s revenue they would be larger if, as December’s high average order value suggests, a gift box costs more than a typical order. Always say “of what”, and when you compare two percentages, check that they share the same kind of whole.

Absolute and percentage change

From November to December Lantern’s visits rose from 13,600 to 16,200. The absolute change is 16,200−13,600=2,60016{,}200 - 13{,}600 = 2{,}60016,200−13,600=2,600 visits. The percentage change divides that by the starting value, the base: 2,600÷13,600×100≈19.1%2{,}600 \div 13{,}600 \times 100 \approx 19.1\%2,600÷13,600×100≈19.1%.

Because the base is the starting value, percentage changes are not symmetric. If visits fell back from 16,200 to 13,600, the fall would be the same 2,600 visits but only 2,600÷16,200×100≈16.0%2{,}600 \div 16{,}200 \times 100 \approx 16.0\%2,600÷16,200×100≈16.0%, because the base is now larger. A rise of 50% followed by a fall of 50% leaves you at 1.5×0.5=0.751.5 \times 0.5 = 0.751.5×0.5=0.75 of where you started, not back at the beginning.

Percentage points

When the quantity itself is a percentage, there are two ways to describe a change, and they give different numbers. In the KPIs lesson Westerly Paper Co.’s conversion rate fell from 3.00% to 2.60%. The difference between two percentages is measured in percentage points: a fall of 3.00−2.60=0.403.00 - 2.60 = 0.403.00−2.60=0.40 percentage points. The relative change is (2.60−3.00)÷3.00×100≈−13.3%(2.60 - 3.00) \div 3.00 \times 100 \approx -13.3\%(2.60−3.00)÷3.00×100≈−13.3%: the rate is 13.3% lower than it was.

Both are correct, and both are useful. What is not acceptable is “conversion fell 0.4%”, which most readers take as a relative fall of 0.4%, a change more than thirty times smaller than the real one. Write “percentage points” (or “points”, once the context is clear) for differences between percentages, and “%” only for relative changes.

Key idea: Points for differences, percent for ratios

From 3.00% to 2.60% is −0.4 percentage points and −13.3%. When a KPI is a percentage, report the change in points, and add the relative change if it helps.

Averages: mean, median and mode

An average describes the centre of a set of values with one number. There are three common ones. The mean adds the values and divides by how many there are. The median is the middle value once they are sorted, so half lie at or below it and half at or above. The mode is the value that occurs most often.

To find the median, imagine the 118 ratings sorted from 1 to 5: the middle falls between the 59th and 60th, and since the 46 ratings of 4 stars or fewer fill positions 1 to 46, both are 5s. The median is 5, and so is the mode. The three averages tell slightly different stories: the typical customer gave 5 stars, while a few low ratings pull the mean down to 4.39.

Which average is allowed depends on the level of measurement, from the data-types lesson. The mode works for any variable, even a nominal one such as a product name. The median needs an order, so it works for ordinal data such as star ratings. The mean assumes that the steps between values are equal, which a 1–5 rating does not guarantee; averaging ratings is a common and useful convenience, but report the distribution or the median beside it.

Both branches average 11 orders a day, but branch B’s days swing from 3 to 19, and planning its baking is much harder. Three measures of spread capture the difference.

The range is the largest value minus the smallest. The interquartile range (IQR) is the width of the middle half of the data. The standard deviation is, roughly, the typical distance of a value from the mean. All three are about twice as large or more for branch B. The next section shows how each is calculated.

In depth

Percentage change: three traps

Small bases. Lantern’s gift-box orders went from 9 in October to 88 in November, a rise of (88−9)÷9×100≈877.8%(88 - 9) \div 9 \times 100 \approx 877.8\%(88−9)÷9×100≈877.8%, then to 231 in December, a rise of 162.5%. December’s percentage is far smaller, but its absolute rise, 143 orders, is almost twice November’s 79. A percentage change from a small base is dramatic and often meaningless; this is a base effect. Report the counts beside it.

Reversals. Equal percentage rises and falls do not cancel, as the concept section showed. To undo a fall of 20% you need a rise of 25%, because the second change works on a smaller base.

Changes in rates. A change in a percentage has a points version and a relative version. Say which one you mean, every time.

The tempting answer is the mean of the five monthly rates, about 11.0%. It is wrong: 9,800 visits growing at that average rate, 11.02% a month, for five months would reach about 16,500, not 16,200. Growth compounds, so the right average is the compound growth rate, the constant rate that turns the starting value into the final value over the same number of periods:

A growth rate needs the number of steps, not the number of months: July to December is five steps. And an average growth rate describes the past; Lantern’s last two months were boosted by Christmas gift buying, and projecting 10.6% a month into 2027 would be a mistake that the next lesson explains.

The three averages in detail

For nnn values x1,x2,…,xnx_1, x_2, \dots, x_nx1​,x2​,…,xn​, the mean is xˉ=(x1+x2+⋯+xn)÷n\bar{x} = (x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n) \div nxˉ=(x1​+x2​+⋯+xn​)÷n. The mean has a property the others lack: multiplied by the count, it gives the total. That is why average order value is a mean: AOV × orders = revenue. If you need totals, for a budget, a forecast or a stock order, use the mean.

The median of nnn sorted values sits at position (n+1)÷2(n + 1) \div 2(n+1)÷2; when nnn is even, it is halfway between the two middle values. Because it depends only on the order of the values, a few extreme values cannot drag it far. When data are skewed, the mean is pulled towards the long tail: above the median when there are a few very large values, as with order values, and below it when there are a few very small ones, as with Lantern’s ratings. For “what does a typical customer do?”, the median is usually the better answer.

The mode is the only average for nominal data (the most common product, region or channel) and is useful for counts with few possible values, such as packs per order. Some data have two modes, which often signals two groups mixed together: one-pack orders and gift boxes, say.

Measuring spread

The range is the simplest measure: branch A’s is 15−7=815 - 7 = 815−7=8. It depends on just two values, the most extreme, so one unusual day changes it completely.

Quartiles split sorted data into four parts. The first quartile, Q1, has a quarter of the values below it and the third, Q3, three-quarters; the median is the second. There are several ways to calculate quartiles, and B02 explains the choice. This course uses quartiles by linear interpolation, as B02 does (and as Excel’s QUARTILE.INC does): the value at position 1+(n−1)p1 + (n - 1)p1+(n−1)p in the sorted list, where ppp is 0.25 for Q1 and 0.75 for Q3, interpolating between neighbours when the position isn’t a whole number.

For branch A, n=9n = 9n=9, so Q1 is the 3rd value, 10, and Q3 the 7th, 12. The IQR is 12−10=212 - 10 = 212−10=2: the middle half of branch A’s days lie within 2 orders of each other. For branch B the same positions give 8 and 14, an IQR of 6.

The standard deviation uses every value. Take each value’s distance from the mean, square it, add the squares, divide by n−1n - 1n−1 and take the square root. For branch A the distances from 11 are −4, −2, −1, −1, 0, 1, 1, 2 and 4; their squares add to 44; 44÷8=5.544 \div 8 = 5.544÷8=5.5; and 5.5≈2.35\sqrt{5.5} \approx 2.355.5​≈2.35 orders. For branch B the squares add to 220, and 220÷8≈5.24\sqrt{220 \div 8} \approx 5.24220÷8​≈5.24.

Squaring makes every distance positive and gives large distances extra weight. Dividing by n−1n - 1n−1 rather than nnn is the convention for a sample, which B04 explains. For roughly symmetric, bell-shaped data, about 95% of values lie within two standard deviations of the mean (the empirical rule, also in B04).

Which measure to use follows the same logic as the averages. The standard deviation goes with the mean and suits roughly symmetric data. The IQR goes with the median and resists extreme values, which is why B02 builds its outlier fences on it. The range is quick, but describes only the two extremes.

Weighted averages

In a weighted average each value counts in proportion to a weight, xˉw=∑wixi÷∑wi\bar{x}_w = \sum w_i x_i \div \sum w_ixˉw​=∑wi​xi​÷∑wi​. The most important example in this course is the overall conversion rate. In July Lantern’s website converted 205÷7,400≈2.770%205 \div 7{,}400 \approx 2.770\%205÷7,400≈2.770% of its visits and the app 89÷2,400≈3.708%89 \div 2{,}400 \approx 3.708\%89÷2,400≈3.708%. The plain average of the two rates is about 3.24%, but the shop’s real conversion was 294÷9,800=3.00%294 \div 9{,}800 = 3.00\%294÷9,800=3.00%. The overall rate is the channel rates weighted by each channel’s visits:

7,400×2.770%+2,400×3.708%9,800=0.755×2.770%+0.245×3.708%≈3.00%\frac{7{,}400 \times 2.770\% + 2{,}400 \times 3.708\%}{9{,}800} = 0.755 \times 2.770\% + 0.245 \times 3.708\% \approx 3.00\%9,8007,400×2.770%+2,400×3.708%​=0.755×2.770%+0.245×3.708%≈3.00%

The website had three-quarters of the visits, so its rate counts three times as much. The plain average treats the two channels as equal and overstates the shop’s conversion. The same rule applies to any average of averages: to average monthly AOVs across several months, weight each month’s AOV by its orders; to average two branches’ ratings, weight by the number of ratings. The weighted result always equals the total of the numerators divided by the total of the denominators, which is the simplest way to compute it. It also means the overall rate can move when nothing but the mix of channels changes, a point the next lesson develops.

Rounding and precision

  1. Round at the end. Rounding July’s channel rates to 2.8% and 3.7% before weighting gives (7,400×2.8+2,400×3.7)÷9,800≈3.02%(7{,}400 \times 2.8 + 2{,}400 \times 3.7) \div 9{,}800 \approx 3.02\%(7,400×2.8+2,400×3.7)÷9,800≈3.02% instead of 3.00%. Keep full precision in the working; round the answer.
  2. Match the precision to the data. July’s returning share is 38.0952…%; report 38.1%. Four decimal places suggest an accuracy that 294 orders can’t deliver.
  3. Be consistent. Give every value in a column of a table the same number of decimal places, so that differences in digits don’t look like differences in size.
  4. Rounded parts may not add up. Three shares of 33.3% total 99.9%. Say so in a note rather than fudging one figure.
  5. Know when an input was rounded. The case’s average ratings are given to one decimal place, so a change from 4.4 to 4.5 could be anything from just above 0 to just under 0.2 stars.

Common mistakes

  • “Conversion fell 0.4%.”

    From 3.00% to 2.60% is a fall of 0.4 percentage points, or 13.3% in relative terms. “0.4%” states neither. Use points for differences between percentages.

  • “Gift boxes grew 878%, our best result.”

    From 9 to 88 is a huge percentage on a tiny base. December’s 162.5% added more orders (143 against 79). Report the counts with any percentage change, especially from a small base.

  • “Average monthly growth is the mean of the monthly rates.”

    Growth compounds. Kettle & Leaf’s mean monthly rate, 7.50%, overstates its compound rate of 7.12%. Use (end ÷ start)^(1/n) − 1, with n the number of steps.

  • “The average of the channel rates is the shop’s rate.”

    Only if the channels are the same size. July’s plain average, 3.24%, overstates the shop’s 3.00% because the website, with the lower rate, had three-quarters of the visits. Weight by the denominators, or divide total orders by total visits.

  • “The mean is the typical value.”

    In skewed data it isn’t. December’s sample mean of $51.18 was above eight of eleven orders. Use the median for “typical” and the mean for totals.

  • “More decimals look more professional.”

    They suggest a precision the data don’t have, and rounding the inputs early shifts the answer (3.02% instead of 3.00% in the July example). Keep full precision while calculating, then round the result sensibly.

In practice

Official statisticians face the same choices and make them explicitly. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics reports movements in the Consumer Price Index as percentage changes rather than as changes in index points, because a change in points depends on how far the index has moved from its base period, while a percentage change does not. Its own worked example uses the all-items index for urban consumers, not seasonally adjusted: it rose from 277.948 in November 2021 to 278.802 in December 2021, 0.854 points, which is a 0.3% rise. The BLS also notes that a December-to-December change and an annual-average change are different figures, a reminder to state which comparison a growth rate makes.

In a monthly business review the same discipline applies to every line. The revenue manager of a fictional hotel reports occupancy, a percentage, as “82%, up 3 points on last year”; the average daily rate, a dollar amount, as “$184, up 4.0%”; and length of stay by its median, because a handful of month-long corporate bookings make the mean misleading. When she combines the hotel’s two buildings, she weights each building’s occupancy by its rooms, never averaging the two percentages.

Summary

  • A proportion is a part divided by its whole; a percentage is the proportion × 100. Always name the whole.
  • Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. The base is the old value, so equal rises and falls don’t cancel, and small bases give dramatic, often meaningless percentages.
  • For a quantity that is itself a percentage, report changes in percentage points (3.00% to 2.60% is −0.4 points) and, if useful, the relative change (−13.3%).
  • Average growth over n periods is the compound rate (end ÷ start)^(1/n) − 1, not the mean of the period rates.
  • Mean for totals and symmetric data; median for the typical value in skewed data; mode for categories. The level of measurement limits the choice.
  • Spread: the range (two extremes), the IQR (middle half, robust) and the standard deviation (typical distance from the mean). Pair the IQR with the median and the standard deviation with the mean.
  • An overall rate is a weighted average of group rates, weighted by the denominators: overall conversion = all orders ÷ all visits.
  • Keep full precision while calculating, round at the end, and match the decimals to what the data support.

Terms introduced

Proportion
A part divided by the whole it belongs to, a number between 0 and 1. Multiplied by 100 it is a percentage.
Percentage change
The change in a value divided by its starting value, times 100: (new − old) ÷ old × 100. The starting value is the base.
Percentage point
The unit for the difference between two percentages: a change from 8% to 5% is a fall of 3 percentage points (and a relative fall of 37.5%).
Base effect
A large percentage change produced mainly by a small or unusual starting value rather than by a large change in the quantity itself.
Compound growth rate
The constant rate per period that takes a starting value to an ending value over n periods: (end ÷ start)^(1/n) − 1. Over years it is the compound annual growth rate.
Mean
The sum of the values divided by how many there are; the arithmetic average. Multiplied by the count it gives the total.
Median
The middle value of a set of values sorted in order, with half at or below it and half at or above; with an even number of values, the average of the two middle ones.
Mode
The value that occurs most often in a set of values; the only average available for nominal data.
Range
The largest value minus the smallest: a measure of spread that depends only on the two extremes.
Quartile
One of the three values that divide sorted data into four equal parts: a quarter of the data lie below the first quartile (Q1), half below the second (the median) and three-quarters below the third (Q3).
Interquartile range
The third quartile minus the first quartile: the width of the middle half of the data (IQR).
Standard deviation
A measure of spread: roughly the typical distance of values from their mean, calculated as the square root of the average squared distance (dividing by n − 1 for a sample).
Weighted average
An average in which each value counts in proportion to a weight. The mean of all rows equals the average of the group means weighted by group size.
