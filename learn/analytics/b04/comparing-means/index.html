Comparing two means

Compare two means the way the data were collected: with a paired t test when the same units are measured twice, and with Welch’s t test when the groups are separate. Run both by hand, with T.TEST and with the Analysis ToolPak, put an interval and an effect size on the difference, and check the conditions before trusting any of it.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Choose between a paired and an independent-samples test from how the data were collected, and explain why pairing can reveal a difference that a two-sample test misses.
  • Calculate Welch’s and the pooled two-sample t statistic, degrees of freedom and p-value by hand and with T.TEST types 1 to 3, and explain why Welch’s test is the safer default.
  • Read the Analysis ToolPak’s three t-test outputs, including the one- and two-tail rows and the rounded degrees of freedom of the unequal-variances tool.
  • Construct and interpret a confidence interval for a difference in means and for a mean difference, and report an effect size with Cohen’s d.
  • Assess independence, normality or sample size, and outliers before a comparison, and recognise when a rank test such as Mann–Whitney or Wilcoxon’s is worth considering.

Review first

  • Hypothesis testing — Hypotheses, test statistics, p-values, one- and two-sided tests and the one-sample t test, which a paired test reuses.
  • Confidence intervals — The t interval, which becomes an interval for a difference.
  • Skewed data and transformations — Why right-skewed values such as order totals are often analysed on a log scale.
  • Interpreting results: charts, comparisons and causes — Correlation, causation and confounding: what a comparison of groups can and can’t show.

The idea

Fictional case

In October Ana ran a two-hour latte-art workshop for 12 trainee baristas. A senior barista scored each trainee’s pour out of 20 before the workshop and again after it. The trainees averaged 10.00 points before and 11.58 after. Before she adds the workshop to Lantern’s staff training plan, Ana wants to know whether the 1.58-point rise is more than the ups and downs of a dozen pours.

Two samples, or one sample of pairs?

The scores look like two samples, a column of 12 before and a column of 12 after, and the In depth section’s two-sample test compares two columns’ means. Run on these columns it finds nothing: Welch’s t test gives t = 0.99 and p = 0.33. The trainees differ so much from one another, with standard deviations of 3.54 points before and 4.23 after, that a 1.58-point rise in the average is lost in the noise.

But the columns are not two separate groups: they are the same 12 people measured twice. Trainee 3 scored 16 both times; trainee 5 scored 4 before and 3 after. What matters is each person’s change. Subtract each trainee’s before score from the after score and the 24 numbers become 12 differences: 2, 2, 0, 5, −1, 3, 1, 1, 1, 1, 2 and 2. Ten trainees improved, one stayed the same and one slipped by a point.

The paired t test

With the differences in hand, the question becomes a one-sample question from the previous lesson: is the mean difference μd\mu_dμd​ zero? Ana tests H0:μd=0H_0: \mu_d = 0H0​:μd​=0 against H1:μd≠0H_1: \mu_d \ne 0H1​:μd​=0, two-sided because a workshop that confused trainees would matter too. The differences have mean dˉ=1.583\bar{d} = 1.583dˉ=1.583 and standard deviation sd=1.505s_d = 1.505sd​=1.505, so:

t=dˉsd/n=1.5831.505/12=1.5830.4345≈3.64t = \dfrac{\bar{d}}{s_d / \sqrt{n}} = \dfrac{1.583}{1.505 / \sqrt{12}} = \dfrac{1.583}{0.4345} \approx 3.64t=sd​/n​dˉ​=1.505/12​1.583​=0.43451.583​≈3.64

with n − 1 = 11 degrees of freedom. The test needs independent pairs and roughly normal differences: the trainees were scored one at a time, and the 12 differences have no extreme value (the largest, 5, is 2.3 standard deviations above their mean). The two-sided p-value, =T.DIST.2T(3.644,11), is 0.0039: strong evidence that the scores rose. Same data, same question, and p = 0.004 instead of p = 0.33.

This is the paired t test. In Excel it is =T.TEST(C2:C13,B2:B13,2,1), with the before scores in B and the after scores in C: the last argument, type 1, means paired.

Why pairing helps

Both tests divide the same 1.58-point rise by a standard error; they differ in which one. The two-sample test’s standard error, 1.59 points, contains all the differences between trainees, beginners and good pourers alike. The paired test’s, 0.43 points, contains only how much the trainees’ changes vary, because subtracting a trainee’s before score from the after score cancels whatever makes that trainee good or bad.

The cancelling works because before and after scores are strongly correlated: trainees who scored high before scored high after, with r = 0.94. The variance of the differences is s12+s22−2rs1s2=17.90+12.55−2×0.9403×4.231×3.542≈2.27s_1^2 + s_2^2 - 2 r s_1 s_2 = 17.90 + 12.55 - 2 \times 0.9403 \times 4.231 \times 3.542 \approx 2.27s12​+s22​−2rs1​s2​=17.90+12.55−2×0.9403×4.231×3.542≈2.27, so sds_dsd​ = 1.505, against standard deviations of 4.23 and 3.54 for the scores themselves. The stronger the correlation, the more pairing gains.

Key idea: The design chooses the test

Use a paired test when each value in one sample belongs with one value in the other: the same customer, store, machine or trainee measured twice, or units matched in pairs before the study. Use an independent-samples test when the two groups are separate. Decide from how the data were collected, never from which test gives the smaller p-value.

What the test shows is that the scores rose by more than chance would produce. It doesn’t show that the workshop caused the rise: trainees pouring later in the day might have improved with practice anyway, and a trainee who poured unusually badly before is likely to score nearer their usual level afterwards, which is regression to the mean. Answering “did the workshop cause it?” needs a control group of trainees scored twice without the workshop, ideally assigned at random, as in an A/B test.

In depth

Independent samples and Welch’s t test

When the two samples are separate groups, such as hires from two bike stations or calls handled with two scripts, they are independent samples, and the question is whether the population means μ1\mu_1μ1​ and μ2\mu_2μ2​ differ. The null hypothesis is H0:μ1−μ2=0H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0H0​:μ1​−μ2​=0 (or a stated difference Δ0\Delta_0Δ0​, “delta-nought”), against a one- or two-sided alternative. The estimate is the difference in sample means, and its standard error combines the two samples’ standard errors:

SE=s12n1+s22n2\text{SE} = \sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}SE=n1​s12​​+n2​s22​​​

A two-sample t test divides the difference by this standard error. Welch’s t test compares the result with a t distribution whose degrees of freedom come from the Welch–Satterthwaite formula in the Formulas section. Its result always lies between the smaller of n₁ − 1 and n₂ − 1 at the low end and n₁ + n₂ − 2 at the high end. It is usually not a whole number, and it falls towards the low end when one sample is much noisier or much smaller than the other. The simple example runs the test on two bike-hire stations.

The pooled test, and why Welch is the safer default

The older pooled t test, Student’s two-sample t test, assumes the two populations have the same variance. It averages the two sample variances, weighted by their degrees of freedom, into a pooled variance sp2s_p^2sp2​, uses sp1/n1+1/n2s_p\sqrt{1/n_1 + 1/n_2}sp​1/n1​+1/n2​​ as the standard error, and has n1+n2−2n_1 + n_2 - 2n1​+n2​−2 degrees of freedom. When the variances really are equal it is slightly more powerful. With equal sample sizes the two tests give the same t, and only their degrees of freedom differ.

When the variances differ and the sample sizes differ too, the pooled test can mislead. If the smaller sample has the larger variance, pooling understates the standard error and the test raises too many false alarms; if the larger sample has the larger variance, it misses real differences. The challenging example shows a pooled p-value of 0.0066 beside Welch’s 0.071 for the same calls. Delacre, Lakens and Leys (2017) show that Welch’s test controls false alarms better when variances differ and loses little when they are equal, and recommend it by default; this course follows them.

It is tempting to choose by testing the variances first. Excel’s F.TEST(array1, array2) returns the two-tailed p-value of the F test for equal variances, which is based on the ratio of the two sample variances. Using it as a gate is a poor idea: the F test is very sensitive to departures from normality (OpenStax), and Delacre and colleagues argue that choosing between the pooled and Welch tests by a preliminary test of equal variances often gives the wrong answer. Use F.TEST, if at all, to describe how different the spreads look.

T.TEST in Excel

T.TEST(array1, array2, tails, type) returns the p-value of a two-sample or paired t test, and only the p-value. Tails is 1 or 2, and type chooses the test:

Microsoft’s page says T.TEST works from a non-negative t statistic: with tails = 2 it returns the probability of a larger |t| if the two means are equal, and with tails = 1 half of that, the tail in whichever direction the data point. So a one-tailed T.TEST never exceeds 0.5 and never tells you the direction. If your one-sided alternative says μ₁ > μ₂ and the sample means say the opposite, the p-value for your alternative is 1 minus T.TEST’s: check the means before you read a one-tailed result.

A paired T.TEST (type 1) returns #N/A if the two ranges hold different numbers of values, and it pairs the values by position, so both columns must be in the same order. For the t statistic and the degrees of freedom, calculate them from the formulas or run the ToolPak.

Confidence intervals for a difference

Every test here has an interval to go with it. For independent samples it is (xˉ1−xˉ2)±t∗×SE(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t^{*} \times \text{SE}(xˉ1​−xˉ2​)±t∗×SE, with t∗t^{*}t∗ from the t distribution with Welch’s degrees of freedom; for paired samples it is dˉ±t∗×sd÷n\bar{d} \pm t^{*} \times s_d \div \sqrt{n}dˉ±t∗×sd​÷n​ with n − 1. In Excel the paired interval’s half-width is =CONFIDENCE.T(0.05,STDEV.S(D2:D13),COUNT(D2:D13)) on the column of differences, because a paired interval is a one-sample interval for the differences.

For two independent samples there is no single function: multiply =T.INV.2T(0.05,df) by the standard error. T.INV.2T truncates a fractional df to a whole number (16.69 becomes 16), which widens the interval very slightly; the simple example shows the size of the effect.

For the workshop the paired interval is 1.583 ± 2.201 × 0.4345, from 0.63 to 2.54 points: on average the trainees gained between about half a point and two and a half points. Treated wrongly as independent groups, the interval is −1.73 to 4.89 points and includes 0, matching the non-significant p of 0.33. A 95% interval for a difference excludes 0 exactly when the two-sided test at 5% rejects H₀.

Conditions

  • Independence. Values within each sample must be independent. For independent samples the two groups must be separate; for paired samples the pairs must be independent of one another, so that one trainee’s change doesn’t affect another’s. Random sampling lets you generalise to a population, and random assignment to the groups lets you talk about cause.
  • Normality, or large samples. The t tests assume each population is roughly normal (for a paired test, the population of differences). With larger samples the central limit theorem makes each mean’s sampling distribution close to normal even for skewed data, and how large is large enough depends on how skewed, as the sampling-distributions lesson showed with Lantern’s order values.
  • No dominating outliers. A t test compares means, and one extreme value can move a mean a long way. Look at the data first, in a box plot or a histogram, and if one value drives the result, report the test with and without it.

The applied example puts these conditions to work on Lantern’s spring orders, where web and app order values are compared and one web order of $1,512 dominates.

How big is the difference?

A p-value says whether a difference is likely to be chance, not how big it is, so report an effect size with it. The plainest is the difference in means with its interval, in the units of the problem: 1.58 points out of 20, 95% interval 0.63 to 2.54. To compare effects measured on different scales, analysts also use Cohen’s d, the difference in means divided by the pooled standard deviation, d=(xˉ1−xˉ2)÷spd = (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \div s_pd=(xˉ1​−xˉ2​)÷sp​.

Cohen suggested 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 as small, medium and large, and OpenStax stresses that these labels are only indicative; what counts as large depends on the field and the decision. For the workshop, sp=(17.90+12.55)÷2=3.90s_p = \sqrt{(17.90 + 12.55) \div 2} = 3.90sp​=(17.90+12.55)÷2​=3.90 and d=1.583÷3.90≈0.41d = 1.583 \div 3.90 \approx 0.41d=1.583÷3.90≈0.41: the average gain is about two-fifths of the spread between trainees. Some analysts divide a paired mean difference by sds_dsd​ instead, which gives 1.05 here; the two measure different things, so say which you used.

Rank tests in outline

When samples are small and skewed, or an outlier dominates, a rank test is an alternative. The Mann–Whitney U test, also called the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, replaces the values of two independent samples by their ranks in the combined data and asks whether values in one group tend to be larger than in the other. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test does the same for paired differences, using the ranks of their sizes. Neither assumes normality, and an extreme value counts only as the largest rank.

They answer a slightly different question from a t test, whether one group tends to have larger values rather than whether the means differ. Excel has no function for either, and the ToolPak has no rank test (its Rank and Percentile tool only ranks values); statistical software runs them. For right-skewed values such as order totals, a log transformation (B02’s skewed-data lesson) is another option: a t test on the logs compares geometric means.

Common mistakes

  • “Before and after are two columns, so I used a two-sample test.”

    When each value in one column belongs with one in the other, the data are pairs. A two-sample test on the workshop scores gives p = 0.33, because the trainees differ from one another by far more than the workshop changed them; the paired test removes those differences and gives p = 0.004. Use T.TEST type 1 or the paired ToolPak tool.

  • “F.TEST found no significant difference in variances, so I pooled.”

    Not finding a difference in variances doesn’t show they are equal, least of all in small samples, and the F test is very sensitive to non-normal data. Welch’s test costs little when the variances are equal and protects you when they aren’t: make it the default.

  • “=T.TEST(B2:B9,C2:C21,1,3) is 0.036, so Script A shortens calls.”

    A one-tailed T.TEST returns the tail in whichever direction the data point. Script A’s calls are longer on average, so 0.036 is the p-value for “A is longer”; for “A is shorter” it is 1 − 0.036 = 0.964. Compare the means before reading a one-tailed T.TEST.

  • “The ToolPak and T.TEST disagree, so one of them is wrong.”

    The unequal-variances tool rounds Welch’s degrees of freedom to a whole number (7.83 becomes 8) and works its p-value from that; T.TEST type 3 keeps 7.83. Small differences are expected. Check too that both use the same ranges, in the same order, and the same number of tails.

  • “The paired test proves the workshop works.”

    It shows the scores rose by more than chance would produce. Without a comparison group, practice and regression to the mean could explain some of the rise. A causal claim needs a control group, ideally with trainees assigned to it at random.

  • “Order values aren’t normal, so a t test is useless.”

    With 99 and 132 orders, the means’ sampling distributions are close to normal unless one value dominates. Here one does, so report the test with and without it and look at the medians, the logs or a rank test. Skew in large samples is a reason to check, not automatically a reason to abandon the t test.

In practice

Fictional case

Ana’s note for the staff training plan: “Twelve trainees were scored out of 20 before and after the two-hour latte-art workshop. Their scores rose by 1.58 points on average (95% interval 0.63 to 2.54; paired t test, p = 0.004), and 10 of the 12 improved. We didn’t score a comparison group, so some of the rise may be practice rather than the workshop. Next time I will also score six trainees twice without the workshop.”

Her note follows the habits of a good comparison. She chose the test from the design, reported the size of the change with its interval as well as the p-value, gave the count of trainees who improved, and named what the design can’t show. Analysts comparing two groups work in the same order: decide paired or independent from how the data were collected; look at the data; use Welch’s test for independent samples unless there is a reason to pool; check outliers and report with and without them; and give the difference, its interval and an effect size.

A comparison is only as fair as its groups. A two-sample test on observational data, such as customers who chose the app against those who chose the website, compares people who differ in many ways besides the label, and any gap may come from a confounding variable. Pairing and random assignment are the two main ways to make a comparison fair; when neither is possible, the report should say so plainly.

Summary

  • The design chooses the test: paired when each value in one sample belongs with one in the other (the same unit measured twice, or matched pairs), independent when the groups are separate.
  • A paired t test is a one-sample t test on the differences, t = d̄ ÷ (s_d ÷ √n) with n − 1 df. Pairing removes the variation between units, so it can find effects an independent test misses (p = 0.004 against 0.33 for the workshop).
  • For independent samples, Welch’s t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ÷ √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂), with Welch–Satterthwaite df, is the safer default; the pooled test assumes equal variances and misleads when variances and sample sizes both differ. Don’t choose between them with F.TEST.
  • =T.TEST(array1,array2,tails,type) returns only the p-value: type 1 paired, 2 pooled, 3 Welch. A one-tailed T.TEST is the tail in the direction of the data.
  • The ToolPak’s t-test tools print the means, variances, t Stat, one- and two-tail p-values and critical values; the unequal-variances tool rounds df to a whole number, so its p-value can differ slightly from T.TEST type 3.
  • Report the difference with its interval, (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ± t* × SE or d̄ ± t* × s_d ÷ √n, and an effect size such as Cohen’s d. An interval that excludes 0 matches a significant two-sided test.
  • Check independence, rough normality or large samples, and outliers. One dominating value can decide a comparison of means: report with and without it, and consider medians, logs or a rank test (Mann–Whitney, Wilcoxon signed-rank).

Terms introduced

Independent samples
Two samples whose values are unrelated, such as separate groups of customers or calls, so that nothing links a value in one sample to a particular value in the other.
Paired samples
Two sets of measurements in which each value in one belongs with one value in the other, such as the same people measured before and after, or units matched in pairs.
Two-sample t test
A test of whether two population means differ, from independent samples, using the difference in sample means divided by its standard error.
Welch’s t test
The two-sample t test that doesn’t assume equal population variances: its standard error is √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂) and its degrees of freedom come from the Welch–Satterthwaite formula. Excel’s T.TEST type 3.
Pooled t test
The two-sample t test that assumes equal population variances and combines the two sample variances into one pooled variance, with n₁ + n₂ − 2 degrees of freedom. Also called Student’s two-sample t test; Excel’s T.TEST type 2.
Paired t test
A test of whether the mean of paired differences is zero (or a stated value): a one-sample t test on the differences, with n − 1 degrees of freedom. Excel’s T.TEST type 1.
F test for equal variances
A test of whether two population variances are equal, based on the ratio of the two sample variances; Excel’s F.TEST returns its two-tailed p-value. It is very sensitive to non-normal data.
Effect size
A measure of how large an effect is, as opposed to whether it is statistically significant: a difference in means in the problem’s units, or a standardised measure such as Cohen’s d.
Cohen’s d
The difference between two means divided by the pooled standard deviation, so that it is measured in standard deviations; 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 are often called small, medium and large.
Mann–Whitney U test
A rank-based test for two independent samples, also called the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, that asks whether values in one group tend to be larger than in the other without assuming normality.
Wilcoxon signed-rank test
A rank-based test for paired data that asks whether the differences tend to be positive or negative, using the ranks of their sizes, without assuming normality.
