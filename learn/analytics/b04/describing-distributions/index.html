Describing distributions

Before you infer anything from data, describe it exactly: its centre, its spread, where a value sits within it and its shape. Learn the notation that separates a population from a sample, why the sample variance divides by n − 1, what z-scores, percentile ranks and the coefficient of variation say, and when the empirical rule or only Chebyshev’s inequality applies.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Distinguish a population parameter (μ, σ, N) from a sample statistic (x̄, s, n), and explain why the sample variance divides by n − 1.
  • Calculate the mean, median, trimmed mean, variance, standard deviation and coefficient of variation by hand and in Excel, and predict how adding a constant or changing units moves each of them.
  • Locate a value within its group with a z-score and a percentile rank, using STANDARDIZE, PERCENTRANK.INC and COUNTIF.
  • Decide whether the empirical rule or Chebyshev’s inequality applies to a distribution, and apply it.
  • Describe a distribution’s shape with the five-number summary, a box plot, SKEW and KURT, and a categorical variable with counts, proportions and the mode.
  • Compare several groups side by side with summaries that suit their shape.

Review first

  • Summarising data: changes, averages and spread — The mean, median, mode, quartiles and standard deviation are calculated by hand there.
  • Outliers and impossible values — z-scores, Tukey fences and masking, and why Lantern’s $1,512 office order was kept.
  • Skewed data and transformations — What skewness is, and why a long right tail pulls the mean above the median.
  • Descriptive statistics and regression in Excel — Excel’s AVERAGE, MEDIAN, STDEV.S, QUARTILE.INC, SKEW and KURT, and the ToolPak’s Descriptive Statistics.

The idea

Fictional case

At the end of 2026 Ana has B03’s spring review of her online shop and a full year of catering invoices, and wants to know which of them she can lean on. Two questions start the course: how unusual was LC-10144, the office order of $1,512, compared with everything else she sold, and which kind of catering event brings in most per guest, and how consistently? Both are questions about describing data exactly, which is where inference starts.

This lesson builds the vocabulary that the rest of the course uses. A distribution is the pattern of values a variable takes: which values occur and how often. Four questions describe one: where its centre is, how spread out it is, where a particular value sits within it, and what shape it has (symmetric, or with a long tail on one side).

Populations, samples and their symbols

B01 defined a population as everything a question is about, and a sample as the part you actually observe. Statistics gives their summaries different names and symbols. A number that describes a population is a parameter; the same summary calculated from a sample is a statistic. A parameter is usually unknown and fixed, and a statistic is known but would change if you drew another sample. Most of this course is about using statistics to learn about parameters.

Whether a set of numbers is a population or a sample depends on the question, not on the numbers. Lantern’s 231 spring orders are the whole population if Ana asks “what did spring 2026 look like?”. They are a sample if she treats them as evidence about her orders in general, which is how B03 used them and how most of this course will. The calculation of the mean is the same, $39.38; the standard deviation is not, as the next sections show.

Centre: the balance point and the middle

The mean is the balance point of the data. Each value’s deviation from the mean, xi−xˉx_i - \bar{x}xi​−xˉ, is positive above it and negative below, and the deviations always add to exactly 0. Picture the values as equal weights on a plank: the mean is where a support would balance it. That is why one value far out on the plank, like the $1,512 order, moves the mean a long way: it has the longest lever.

The median is the middle value, so it depends only on the order of the values. For the spring orders it is $19.50 against a mean of $39.38. A compromise between the two is the trimmed mean: sort the values, drop a fixed share from each end, and average the rest. Excel’s =TRIMMEAN(J2:J232,0.1) drops 10% of the 231 orders in total, rounded down to an even number: 22 orders, 11 from each end. The mean of the rest is $30.03: the large orders still count, but the most extreme ones no longer dominate.

Spread: deviations, squares and n − 1

Spread measures how far values typically lie from the centre. The standard deviation squares each deviation (so negatives don’t cancel positives), adds the squares, averages them and takes the square root. The total of the squared deviations, ∑(xi−xˉ)2\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2∑(xi​−xˉ)2, is called the sum of squares; Excel’s DEVSQ returns it. Dividing it by nnn gives the population variance σ2\sigma^2σ2 (VAR.P); dividing by n−1n - 1n−1 gives the sample variance s2s^2s2 (VAR.S).

Why n−1n - 1n−1? A sample’s deviations are measured from its own mean, xˉ\bar{x}xˉ, which sits in the middle of that sample. They are therefore a little smaller, on average, than the deviations from the population mean μ\muμ would be, and dividing by nnn would underestimate the population’s variance. Dividing by n−1n - 1n−1 corrects this exactly, on average over all possible samples. The In depth section shows it on a population small enough to list every possible sample.

Position: z-scores and percentile ranks

A z-score says how many standard deviations a value lies above (positive) or below (negative) the mean. The office order scores (1,512−39.38)÷100.90≈14.6(1{,}512 - 39.38) \div 100.90 \approx 14.6(1,512−39.38)÷100.90≈14.6: it lies 14.6 standard deviations above the mean, a position few data sets ever contain. In Excel, =STANDARDIZE(1512,AVERAGE(J2:J232),STDEV.S(J2:J232)) gives the same number.

z-scores also compare values from groups with different centres and spreads. A percentile rank answers a related question without using the standard deviation at all: what percentage of the group lies below this value? Both are positions within a group, not judgements: a value can be unusual and perfectly genuine.

Shape: two rules about standard deviations

Two rules link the standard deviation to the share of data near the mean. The empirical rule says that for bell-shaped, symmetric data about 68% of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within two and about 99.7% within three. Chebyshev’s inequality makes a weaker promise that holds for every data set, whatever its shape: at least 75% of values lie within two standard deviations and at least 88.9% within three.

Lantern’s orders break the empirical rule in both directions. Without it, 91.7% lie within two standard deviations, short of 95%, because the long right tail of gift boxes and 1 kg bags stretches further than a bell curve would. Chebyshev’s minimums hold in every column, as they must. The rule to remember: use the empirical rule only after you have seen that the data are roughly bell-shaped, and fall back on Chebyshev when they are not.

Why the sample variance divides by n − 1

Take a population of just three values, 2, 4 and 9 (an illustration, not Lantern data). Averaged over all possible samples, dividing by n−1n - 1n−1 gives 8.678.678.67, exactly the population variance; dividing by nnn gives 4.334.334.33, only half of it. In general the divide-by-nnn version averages (n−1)÷n(n - 1) \div n(n−1)÷n of σ2\sigma^2σ2: a large error for tiny samples and a negligible one for large samples, which is why STDEV.S and STDEV.P give $100.90 and $100.68 for the 231 orders but differ by 4.7% for 11 launch events. An estimate that is right on average is called unbiased, so s2s^2s2 is an unbiased estimate of σ2\sigma^2σ2. (The square root spoils this slightly: sss is still a little low on average, but much less so than the divide-by-nnn version.)

Another way to say it uses degrees of freedom. The nnn deviations from the sample mean must add to 0, so once you know n−1n - 1n−1 of them the last is fixed: only n−1n - 1n−1 are free to vary. The sample variance averages the squared deviations over those n−1n - 1n−1 degrees of freedom. The same idea returns in the t, chi-square and F distributions later in the course.

Adding a constant and changing units

Suppose every value is transformed as y=a+bxy = a + bxy=a+bx: aaa is added and bbb multiplies. Measures of centre and position follow the transformation: yˉ=a+bxˉ\bar{y} = a + b\bar{x}yˉ​=a+bxˉ, and the median and quartiles move the same way. Measures of spread ignore aaa and scale by ∣b∣|b|∣b∣: sy=∣b∣sxs_y = |b| s_xsy​=∣b∣sx​, and the interquartile range (IQR) likewise, while the variance scales by b2b^2b2. z-scores, skewness and kurtosis don’t change at all when bbb is positive, because they are measured in standard deviations.

Two Lantern illustrations. If Ana had added a flat $5.20 delivery charge to every spring order (a hypothetical), the mean would rise from $39.38 to $44.58 and the median from $19.50 to $24.70, but the standard deviation and the IQR would stay at $100.90 and $23.60. And the 40 Kilnworth bags she weighed in October average 1,002.75 g with a standard deviation of 2.66 g: in kilograms (b=1÷1,000b = 1 \div 1{,}000b=1÷1,000) that is 1.00275 kg and 0.00266 kg, and as overfill above the label (y=x−1,000y = x - 1{,}000y=x−1,000) it is 2.75 g and still 2.66 g.

The coefficient of variation

A standard deviation is in the data’s units, so it can’t compare the spread of groups with very different means, or of variables in different units. The coefficient of variation (CV) divides the standard deviation by the mean: CV=s÷xˉ\text{CV} = s \div \bar{x}CV=s÷xˉ, usually written as a percentage. It measures spread relative to the size of the values. For Ana’s catering, launches vary by 7.5% of their mean per guest-day and meetings by 22.3%: meetings are three times as variable, relative to what they charge.

The CV only makes sense for a variable measured from a true zero (a ratio scale, in B01’s terms) with a positive mean, such as money, weight or time. Kilnworth’s bag weights have a CV of 0.26%: they vary little relative to their size. The same bags’ overfill above 1,000 g has a CV of 97%, although nothing about the bags has changed. Overfill has no true zero and can be negative (5 of the 40 bags were under 1,000 g); moving the zero changed the mean but not the standard deviation, so the ratio means little. Don’t compute a CV for temperatures in °C, z-scores, profits that can be negative, or ratings.

Percentiles and percentile ranks

A percentile (B03) goes from a percentage to a value: the 90th percentile of order values is the value with 90% of orders at or below it. A percentile rank goes the other way, from a value to a percentage. OpenStax’s Introductory Business Statistics uses (B+0.5E)÷n×100(B + 0.5E) \div n \times 100(B+0.5E)÷n×100, where BBB is the number of values below the one you are ranking and EEE the number equal to it. Excel’s =PERCENTRANK.INC(array,x) returns a fraction instead: for a value in the data, it is the number of values below it divided by n−1n - 1n−1. Without ties, that is the share of the other values that lie below it.

Excel cuts PERCENTRANK.INC off at three digits unless you ask for more, rather than rounding: Microsoft’s own example returns 0.555 for 5 ÷ 9. Counting gives the same fraction without the truncation: =COUNTIF(B2:B29,"<"&E2)/(COUNT(B2:B29)-1). For a value that isn’t in the data, PERCENTRANK.INC interpolates between its neighbours, which counting doesn’t. Microsoft’s page doesn’t say how ties are treated; the worksheets on this site count only the values strictly below, as in the definition above.

With many ties the two definitions of a percentile rank can disagree badly. 35 of the 154 star ratings are below 4 and 51 equal 4, so a 4-star rating sits at the 39th percentile by OpenStax’s formula and at 0.228 by PERCENTRANK.INC (35 ÷ 153 = 0.2288, cut to three digits). State the method whenever ties are common.

The five-number summary and box plots

The five-number summary is the minimum, the first quartile, the median, the third quartile and the maximum. It shows the centre, the spread of the middle half and the reach of both tails, and it is what a box plot draws, as B03’s box and whisker chart did. The table and the figure compare Ana’s five kinds of catering event by invoice per guest-day (a two-day event counts its guests on both days).

Skewness and kurtosis

Excel’s SKEW measures skewness. It cubes each value’s z-score, so values far out on one side dominate it: positive for a long right tail, negative for a long left one, near 0 for symmetric data (B02, B03). The meetings’ SKEW is 2.05, and their mean is above their median, the usual sign of a right tail. The tail has a business cause: invoices include a fixed set-up charge and a minimum, so the smallest events pay most per guest. EV-047, the meeting at $17.14 per guest, had 35 guests, the fewest of any meeting.

KURT, Excel’s excess kurtosis, uses fourth powers and is about 0 for normally distributed data. Microsoft’s page describes it as the “relative peakedness or flatness” of a distribution, but Westfall (2014) showed that kurtosis says almost nothing about the peak: it measures how extreme the tails are, that is, how prone the data are to outliers. Lantern’s orders have a KURT of 199.5 because of one order, not because of a sharp peak. Read a large KURT as “check the extremes”.

Describing a categorical variable

For a categorical variable the summaries are counts, proportions and the mode. Of the 231 spring orders, 132 came through the web shop and 99 through the app, proportions of 0.571 and 0.429; the mode is web. A 0/1 variable is a special case: the mean of Lantern’s subscription flags, 1 for a subscription order and 0 otherwise, is 0.450, the proportion of subscription orders (104 of 231). That is why later lessons treat a proportion as the mean of a 0/1 variable.

Comparing groups side by side

  • Use the same summaries, the same quartile method and the same scale for every group, and show each group’s size: a mean of 13 events and a mean of 28 don’t carry the same weight.
  • Match the summary to the shape. For roughly symmetric groups the mean and standard deviation describe them; for skewed groups, or groups with outliers, add the median and IQR.
  • Use relative measures to compare spread across groups of different size or units: the CV, the IQR as a share of the median for skewed groups, or z-scores within each group.
  • Describing a difference is not testing it. Whether team days really earn more per guest than launches, or only did in these 76 events, is the question of ANOVA in module 4.

Common mistakes

  • “Dividing by n or by n − 1 makes no real difference.”

    For 231 orders the two standard deviations differ by about 0.2%; for 11 launches, by almost 5%. More important, dividing by n underestimates σ² on average over all samples, because a sample’s deviations are measured from its own mean. Use STDEV.S and VAR.S for samples, and the .P versions only when the values are the whole population you describe.

  • “About 95% of any data lie within two standard deviations of the mean.”

    That is the empirical rule, and it holds only for roughly bell-shaped data. Lantern’s skewed orders have 99.6% within two standard deviations with the office order and 91.7% without it. For data of unknown or skewed shape, only Chebyshev’s guarantee holds: at least 75%.

  • “A z-score above 3 means the value is an error.”

    A z-score measures position. The meeting at $17.14 per guest-day (z ≈ 3.5) is genuine: a small event paying a fixed set-up charge. Investigate a flagged value before deciding what it is, as B02 did with the office order.

  • “Kurtosis measures how peaked a distribution is.”

    Microsoft’s description says so, but kurtosis is driven almost entirely by the tails (Westfall, 2014). A large KURT says the data contain, or are prone to, extreme values.

  • “The coefficient of variation compares the spread of any two variables.”

    Only for ratio-scale variables with a positive mean. Kilnworth’s bags have a CV of 0.26% as weights but 97% as overfill above 1,000 g: shifting the zero changed the mean and so the ratio, and overfill can be negative (5 of the 40 bags were under 1,000 g), so it has no true zero. Don’t compute a CV for ratings, temperatures in °C or values that can be negative.

In practice

Every inference in this course starts from a description, and professionals describe before they test. A revenue manager comparing hotels, a quality engineer checking a filling line, a charity comparing donation sizes by channel: each first reports the size of each group, a centre and a spread that suit its shape, and anything extreme, and only then asks whether a difference is real. Skipping the description is how analysts end up testing a mean that one record dominates.

Service promises are often stated as percentiles rather than means, because customers experience the tail: “90% of orders dispatched within 24 hours” is a statement about the 90th percentile. For the same reason a z-score or a percentile rank is a better way to tell a manager how unusual a week was than the raw number: “the second-busiest week of the year” or “2.4 standard deviations above a typical week”.

Fictional case

Ana’s price note for 2027 gives each event type a median per guest-day and its middle half, with a line on reliability: “Launches and conferences fall in tight ranges; breakfasts vary most from one to the next; meetings and team days have the most extreme exceptions (CV 22% and 18%), because small events cost more per guest.” For the shop she reports the median order ($19.50) beside the mean, and notes that LC-10144 lies 14.6 standard deviations above the mean: by Chebyshev, no more than one of the 231 orders could be that far out.

Two habits make these descriptions trustworthy. Say what each number includes (all events or one-day events, with or without the office order, which quartile method), and give the size of every group. A summary that can’t be reproduced, or that hides a group of three behind a percentage, invites the wrong decision.

Summary

  • A parameter describes a population (μ, σ, N, p); a statistic describes a sample (x̄, s, n, p̂). Whether data are a population or a sample depends on the question.
  • The sample variance divides the sum of squares (DEVSQ) by n − 1, the degrees of freedom, which makes it unbiased; STDEV.S and VAR.S do this, STDEV.P and VAR.P divide by n.
  • The mean is the balance point and sensitive to extremes; the median and the trimmed mean (TRIMMEAN, whose percent is the total excluded) resist them.
  • Adding a constant moves the centre but not the spread; multiplying by b scales the centre by b and the spread by |b|. z-scores, SKEW and KURT don’t change when b > 0.
  • The coefficient of variation, s ÷ x̄, compares relative spread, but only on ratio scales with a positive mean.
  • z-scores (STANDARDIZE) and percentile ranks (PERCENTRANK.INC, or COUNTIF ÷ (n − 1)) locate a value within its group; neither says whether the value is wrong.
  • The empirical rule (68%, 95%, 99.7%) needs bell-shaped data; Chebyshev’s inequality (at least 1 − 1/k²) holds for any data.
  • Describe shape with the five-number summary, box plots, SKEW and KURT (tails, not peakedness); describe categories with counts, proportions and the mode.

Terms introduced

Parameter
A number that describes a population, such as its mean μ, standard deviation σ or proportion p. It is usually unknown and is estimated from a sample.
Statistic
A number calculated from a sample, such as its mean x̄, standard deviation s or proportion p̂, often used to estimate a parameter. It varies from one sample to another.
Sum of squares
The total of the squared deviations of values from their mean, Σ(x − x̄)²; Excel’s DEVSQ. Dividing it by n − 1 gives the sample variance.
Degrees of freedom
The number of values in a calculation that are free to vary. For a sample variance it is n − 1, because the deviations from the sample mean must add to 0.
Trimmed mean
The mean of the values left after a fixed share is removed from each end of the sorted data. Excel’s TRIMMEAN(array, percent) removes percent of the values in total (rounded down to an even number of values), half from each end.
Coefficient of variation
The standard deviation divided by the mean, usually as a percentage: spread relative to the size of the values. Meaningful only for ratio-scale variables with a positive mean.
Percentile rank
The percentage of values in a data set that lie below a given value (with a stated rule for ties). Excel’s PERCENTRANK.INC gives a related fraction: the values below it ÷ (n − 1), cut to three digits.
Empirical rule
For bell-shaped, symmetric data, about 68% of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within two and about 99.7% within three.
Chebyshev’s inequality
For any data set or distribution, at least 1 − 1/k² of the values lie within k standard deviations of the mean (k > 1): at least 75% within two and 88.9% within three.
Five-number summary
The minimum, first quartile, median, third quartile and maximum of a data set: the values a box plot draws.
