Discrete distributions

Many business questions are about counts: subscriptions among the next orders, orders on a day, boxes sold at a market. Learn to describe a count with a probability distribution, its expected value and its standard deviation, to recognise the binomial and Poisson distributions and check their conditions, and to calculate their probabilities by hand and with BINOM.DIST and POISSON.DIST.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Describe a discrete random variable with a probability distribution, and calculate its expected value, variance and standard deviation.
  • Identify when a count follows a binomial distribution, and calculate binomial probabilities, the mean np and the standard deviation √(np(1 − p)) by hand and with BINOM.DIST and BINOM.DIST.RANGE.
  • Identify when a count follows a Poisson distribution, calculate its probabilities with POISSON.DIST, and compare observed counts with a Poisson model.
  • Explain when the Poisson approximates the binomial, and how the exponential distribution describes the time between Poisson events.
  • Use expected values and standard deviations to compare decisions under uncertainty.

Review first

  • Probability — Independence, the multiplication rule, complements and COMBIN are the building blocks of the binomial.
  • Describing distributions — The mean, variance and standard deviation of data, which this lesson extends to random variables.
  • What finance is: money, time and risk — Expected value as a probability-weighted average of outcomes.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana is planning for the busy months. 45.0% of the spring orders were subscriptions: how many subscriptions should she expect among the next 20 orders, and how far might that number swing? The shop averaged 1.89 orders a day: how often will a day bring none, or five or more? And how many gift boxes should she take to a Saturday Christmas market, when she can only guess how many will sell? Each question is about a count that can’t be known in advance.

Random variables and their distributions

A random variable is a number determined by the outcome of an experiment: the number of orders on a day, the number of subscriptions in 20 orders, the number of boxes sold. A discrete random variable takes separate values, usually counts 0, 1, 2, …; a continuous one, such as a weight, can take any value in a range, and is the next module’s subject. Random variables are written with capitals, XXX, and the values they take with lower case, xxx.

The probability distribution of a discrete random variable lists every value it can take with its probability. Each probability is between 0 and 1, and they add to 1. For a spring day drawn at random, the number of shop orders XXX has the distribution below, from the counts of the 122 days.

Expected value and standard deviation

The expected value of a random variable, E(X)E(X)E(X) or μ\muμ, is the probability-weighted average of its values, ∑x P(x)\sum x\,P(x)∑xP(x): the long-run average if the experiment were repeated many times. For orders per day it is 0×0.148+1×0.287+⋯+5×0.033≈1.8930 \times 0.148 + 1 \times 0.287 + \dots + 5 \times 0.033 \approx 1.8930×0.148+1×0.287+⋯+5×0.033≈1.893, which is exactly 231 orders ÷ 122 days. An expected value needn’t be a possible value: no day has 1.89 orders.

The variance of a random variable weights each squared distance from μ\muμ by its probability, σ2=∑(x−μ)2P(x)\sigma^2 = \sum (x - \mu)^2 P(x)σ2=∑(x−μ)2P(x), and the standard deviation σ\sigmaσ is its square root. For orders per day, σ2≈1.767\sigma^2 \approx 1.767σ2≈1.767 and σ≈1.33\sigma \approx 1.33σ≈1.33 orders. There is no n−1n - 1n−1 here: the probabilities describe the distribution itself, like a population.

The binomial distribution

The number of subscriptions among Ana’s next 20 orders is a count of “successes” in a fixed number of tries. The binomial distribution describes such counts when four conditions hold: a fixed number of trials nnn; two outcomes on each trial, success or failure; the same probability of success ppp on every trial; and trials that are independent of each other. Then the number of successes XXX can be anything from 0 to nnn.

Where does its formula come from? Take three orders, each a subscription (S) with probability ppp or one-off (F) with probability 1−p1 - p1−p. By the multiplication rule for independent trials, any particular sequence with two subscriptions, such as SSF, has probability p2(1−p)p^2(1 - p)p2(1−p). There are (32)=3\binom{3}{2} = 3(23​)=3 such sequences, so P(X=2)=3p2(1−p)P(X = 2) = 3p^2(1 - p)P(X=2)=3p2(1−p). In general:

P(X=k)=(nk)pk(1−p)n−k,k=0,1,…,nP(X = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, nP(X=k)=(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k,k=0,1,…,n

For the next 20 orders, with p=104÷231≈0.450p = 104 \div 231 \approx 0.450p=104÷231≈0.450 from the spring, the chance of exactly 9 subscriptions is (209)(0.450)9(0.550)11≈0.1771\binom{20}{9} (0.450)^9 (0.550)^{11} \approx 0.1771(920​)(0.450)9(0.550)11≈0.1771, or =BINOM.DIST(9,20,104/231,FALSE). A binomial count has mean np=9.00np = 9.00np=9.00 and standard deviation np(1−p)≈2.22\sqrt{np(1 - p)} \approx 2.22np(1−p)​≈2.22: Ana should expect about 9 subscriptions, give or take about 2.

The Poisson distribution

Orders per day are a different kind of count: there is no fixed number of trials, only orders arriving over time. The Poisson distribution describes the number of events in a fixed interval of time or space when they happen independently of each other, at a constant average rate λ\lambdaλ (“lambda”), and never two at exactly the same moment. Its one parameter, λ\lambdaλ, is both its mean and its variance. The mean scales with the interval: if a day averages λ\lambdaλ orders, half a day averages λ÷2\lambda \div 2λ÷2 and a week 7λ7\lambda7λ, and the count in each is Poisson with that mean.

P(X=k)=e−λλkk!,k=0,1,2,…P(X = k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dotsP(X=k)=k!e−λλk​,k=0,1,2,…

With λ=231÷122≈1.893\lambda = 231 \div 122 \approx 1.893λ=231÷122≈1.893 orders a day, the chance of a day with no orders is e−1.893≈0.1506e^{-1.893} \approx 0.1506e−1.893≈0.1506, or =POISSON.DIST(0,231/122,FALSE). Multiplying each probability by 122 days gives the number of days the model expects with each count, set against what happened:

In depth

Binomial probabilities in Excel

=BINOM.DIST(number_s,trials,probability_s,cumulative) returns the probability of exactly kkk successes when cumulative is FALSE, and of at most kkk when it is TRUE. Other questions are built from these. “At least 12” is the complement of “at most 11”: =1-BINOM.DIST(11,20,104/231,TRUE) = 0.1312. =BINOM.DIST.RANGE(trials,probability_s,number_s,[number_s2]) gives the probability of a range, both ends included: =BINOM.DIST.RANGE(20,104/231,6,12) = 0.8866. Microsoft lists it for Excel 2016 and later.

Two slips cause most wrong answers. For “at least kkk” the cumulative probability to subtract stops at k−1k - 1k−1, because P(X≥12)=1−P(X≤11)P(X \geq 12) = 1 - P(X \leq 11)P(X≥12)=1−P(X≤11); subtracting P(X≤12)P(X \leq 12)P(X≤12) gives P(X>12)P(X > 12)P(X>12), 0.0583. And FALSE and TRUE answer different questions: FALSE is a single bar of the distribution, TRUE everything up to and including it. BINOM.INV goes the other way: =BINOM.INV(20,104/231,0.95) = 13 is the smallest count with at least a 95% chance of not being exceeded.

Are the binomial conditions met?

The binomial is a model, and its conditions should be checked against how the orders really arrive. Two outcomes and a fixed n are clear. Constant p is doubtful: app orders were subscriptions 57.6% of the time and web orders 35.6%, so if the next 20 orders were all app orders the right ppp would be 0.576, and P(X≥12)P(X \geq 12)P(X≥12) would be 0.507 rather than 0.131. Independence fails if one customer places several of the 20 orders, because a subscriber’s orders are all subscriptions.

Sampling without replacement breaks independence slightly, as the probability lesson showed. When the number drawn is less than about 10% of the population, the binomial is still a good model; for larger shares the hypergeometric distribution, which counts without replacement, is the exact one. A binomial probability is only as good as these conditions, so state the p you used and where it came from.

Mean and spread of a binomial count

For a binomial count, μ=np\mu = npμ=np and σ=np(1−p)\sigma = \sqrt{np(1 - p)}σ=np(1−p)​. The spread is largest when p=0.5p = 0.5p=0.5 and shrinks towards 0 as ppp nears 0 or 1, when almost every trial goes the same way. Two standard deviations either side of the mean, 4.6 to 13.5, cover the counts 5 to 13, which have probability 0.960 together: close to the empirical rule’s 95%, because a binomial with npnpnp and n(1−p)n(1 - p)n(1−p) both above 5 is roughly bell-shaped.

Poisson probabilities and conditions

=POISSON.DIST(x,mean,cumulative) works like BINOM.DIST: FALSE for exactly xxx events, TRUE for at most xxx. The chance of a busy day with 5 or more orders is =1-POISSON.DIST(4,231/122,TRUE) = 0.0436, about 5.3 days in a spring of 122; 4 happened. Microsoft’s example of a Poisson count is cars arriving at a toll plaza in one minute.

The Poisson conditions are worth testing against what you know. Orders come from many separate customers, which supports independence. A constant rate is less certain: orders may cluster after a marketing email or on certain weekdays, and either would make busy and quiet days more common than the model says, so the variance would exceed the mean. A variance well above the mean, called overdispersion, is the usual sign that a Poisson model is too simple.

The Poisson as an approximation to the binomial

When nnn is large and ppp small, a binomial count is close to a Poisson count with λ=np\lambda = npλ=np. For 200 visits converting at 2% (fictional), the binomial chance of at most 2 orders is 0.2351 and the Poisson chance with λ=4\lambda = 4λ=4 is 0.2381. OpenStax notes that there is no single accepted rule for when the approximation is good; one example it gives is nnn greater than 20 with ppp less than 0.05. With Excel’s BINOM.DIST at hand, the approximation matters mainly as a reason why rare events in large populations look Poisson.

Choosing a model for a count

  • Is there a fixed number of trials, each a success or a failure? Then start from the binomial, and ask whether p is the same for every trial and the trials are independent.
  • Are events counted over an interval of time or space, with no upper limit set by a number of trials? Then start from the Poisson, and ask whether the rate is steady and the events independent.
  • Is the quantity measured rather than counted, such as a weight, a time or an amount of money? Then it is continuous: the normal distribution (next module) and the exponential are models for such quantities.
  • Whatever the model, compare it with data before relying on it: observed against expected counts, the mean against the variance, and the conditions against what you know about how the data arise.

Time between events: the exponential distribution

If events follow a Poisson process at rate λ\lambdaλ, the time between one event and the next follows an exponential distribution with mean 1÷λ1 \div \lambda1÷λ. Its probability of a gap longer than ttt is e−λte^{-\lambda t}e−λt, and Excel’s =EXPON.DIST(x,lambda,TRUE) gives the chance of a gap of at most xxx. With 1.893 orders a day, the average gap is 0.528 days, about 12.7 hours, and a gap of more than a day has probability e−1.893≈0.1506e^{-1.893} \approx 0.1506e−1.893≈0.1506: the same as a day with no orders, as it must be.

The exponential is continuous, a preview of the next module, and it is memoryless: however long it has been since the last order, the wait for the next one has the same distribution. That is realistic for many separate customers and unrealistic for anything that wears out or follows a schedule. Real orders also arrive unevenly through the day, so a model with a constant rate is a simplification; =EXPON.DIST(0.25,231/122,TRUE) = 0.377 is the chance of an order within six hours only if the rate really is constant around the clock.

Common mistakes

  • “P(at least 12) is 1 − BINOM.DIST(12, …, TRUE).”

    That subtracts P(X ≤ 12) and leaves P(X > 12), which drops the 12 itself. “At least k” is 1 − P(X ≤ k − 1): =1-BINOM.DIST(11,20,p,TRUE), or =BINOM.DIST.RANGE(20,p,12,20).

  • “Any count of yes/no outcomes is binomial.”

    Only with a fixed number of independent trials and the same p on each. Twenty orders from a mix of app and web customers, or several from one customer, break the constant-p or independence condition. State the p you assume and check whether it holds.

  • “Plan for the expected value.”

    The expected demand for gift boxes is 23, but the stock with the highest expected profit is 30, because unsold and unmet boxes cost different amounts. In general the expected value of a profit is not the profit at the expected demand.

  • “Any count per period is Poisson.”

    The Poisson needs independent events at a constant rate. Orders that bunch after a promotion, or a weekly pattern, make the variance larger than the mean. Compare the mean and the variance, and the observed and expected counts, before relying on the model.

  • “An expected value is the most likely value.”

    It is a long-run average and needn’t be possible: 1.89 orders a day. For subscriptions in 20 orders the most likely count is 9, with a probability of only 0.177.

In practice

Counts drive many operational decisions, and the binomial and the Poisson are the first models a planner reaches for. A call centre sizes its shifts from the Poisson distribution of calls per half hour; an airline that sells more tickets than seats uses the binomial distribution of no-shows; a quality team sets an acceptance rule for a delivery from the binomial distribution of defective items in a sample. In each case the model turns an average into the probability of the bad day the business must plan for.

Expected values are how such decisions are compared, but rarely the whole story. Two plans with similar expected profits can carry very different risks, which the standard deviation and the worst case show; and the probabilities behind an expected value are often judgements, as Ana’s are. Good practice is to show the expected value, the spread and the worst case side by side, and to test how far the inputs can move before the decision changes.

Ana takes 30 gift boxes to the market and writes her stock rule for the shop: over a spring like this one, the Poisson model expects about 18 of 122 days with no orders and about 5 with five or more, so she will keep enough packed stock for a five-order day. For subscriptions she quotes the binomial range, “about 9 in the next 20, usually between 5 and 13”, and notes that the figure assumes the spring’s mix of web and app orders.

Summary

  • A discrete random variable takes separate values; its probability distribution lists each value with a probability, and the probabilities add to 1.
  • E(X) = Σ x P(x) is the long-run average; σ² = Σ (x − μ)² P(x) and σ is its square root. In Excel, SUMPRODUCT does both.
  • The binomial counts successes in n independent trials with the same p: P(X = k) = C(n, k) pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ, mean np, SD √(np(1 − p)).
  • BINOM.DIST(k, n, p, FALSE) is exactly k, TRUE at most k; “at least k” is 1 − BINOM.DIST(k − 1, n, p, TRUE); BINOM.DIST.RANGE gives a range.
  • The Poisson counts independent events at a constant rate λ: P(X = k) = e^(−λ) λᵏ ÷ k!, with mean and variance both λ; POISSON.DIST in Excel.
  • Check a model against the data: observed against expected counts, and the variance against the mean for a Poisson.
  • For large n and small p the binomial is close to a Poisson with λ = np; the time between Poisson events is exponential with mean 1/λ (EXPON.DIST).
  • Compare decisions under uncertainty by their expected values and their spread; the expected value of a profit isn’t the profit at the expected demand.

Terms introduced

Random variable
A number determined by the outcome of an experiment, such as the number of orders on a day. It is discrete if it takes separate values (usually counts) and continuous if it can take any value in a range.
Probability distribution
For a discrete random variable, a list or formula giving every value it can take and its probability, with the probabilities adding to 1; for a continuous variable, a density curve whose area over a range is the probability of that range.
Binomial distribution
The distribution of the number of successes in n independent trials, each with the same probability p of success; its mean is np and its standard deviation √(np(1 − p)).
Poisson distribution
The distribution of the number of events in a fixed interval when events occur independently at a constant average rate λ; its mean and variance both equal λ.
Exponential distribution
A continuous distribution of the time between events in a Poisson process with rate λ; its mean is 1/λ and P(T ≤ t) = 1 − e^(−λt).
