One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA)

Compare the means of several groups at once without inflating the chance of a false alarm. Split variation into between- and within-group parts, build the F statistic by hand and with Excel’s Anova: Single Factor, check the conditions, measure the effect with η², and find which groups differ with adjusted comparisons.

About 85 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain why comparing several means with a t test for every pair inflates the family-wise error rate, and calculate that rate.
  • Calculate the between-group and within-group sums of squares, degrees of freedom, mean squares and F for a one-way ANOVA, by hand and with DEVSQ.
  • Use Excel’s Anova: Single Factor and read its SUMMARY and ANOVA tables, finding p-values and critical values with F.DIST.RT and F.INV.RT.
  • Assess the conditions of one-way ANOVA (independence, normality, equal variances) and choose a response when they fail, such as Welch’s ANOVA.
  • Interpret a significant or non-significant F with η², identify which groups differ with Bonferroni-adjusted comparisons, and explain what Tukey’s HSD does.

The idea

Fictional case

B03 fitted a line to Lantern’s catering invoices and found that Ana’s rule of “about $9 a head” misprices small and large events. Now she asks a different question of the same 76 events of 2026: does the kind of event matter? Measured as invoice per guest-day (a two-day event counts its guests twice), team days averaged $11.25, conferences $10.32, meetings $9.63, breakfasts $9.47 and launches $9.00. Are those real differences between kinds of event, or the scatter of individual events that any five groups would show?

This is a comparison of several means. Comparing means compared two with a t test. With five groups the obvious move, a t test for every pair, has a problem, and the standard solution, the analysis of variance (ANOVA), works by comparing two kinds of variation. This lesson covers one-way ANOVA, in which the groups are defined by one categorical variable, here the event type.

Why not ten t tests?

Five event types make 5×4÷2=105 \times 4 \div 2 = 105×4÷2=10 pairs. Each t test at α = 0.05 has a 5% chance of a false alarm, a Type I error, when the two means are really equal. If the ten tests were independent, the chance of at least one false alarm among them would be 1−0.9510≈0.401 - 0.95^{10} \approx 0.401−0.9510≈0.40. The tests share data, so they aren’t independent and the exact figure differs, but the point stands: the more tests, the likelier a spurious “significant” difference. The probability of at least one false positive in a set of tests is the family-wise error rate.

ANOVA asks one question first, at one α. The null hypothesis is H0 ⁣:μ1=μ2=⋯=μkH_0\colon \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_kH0​:μ1​=μ2​=⋯=μk​, every group has the same population mean, against H1H_1H1​, at least one mean differs. Only if the answer is yes does it make sense to ask which groups differ, with methods that keep the family-wise error rate under control.

Two kinds of variation

Each event’s invoice per guest-day differs from the mean of all 76 events, $9.86, for two reasons. Its type’s mean differs from the grand mean (team days are higher, launches lower), and the event itself differs from its type’s mean (one meeting earned $17.14 per guest-day, another $7.01). The first is between-group variation, the second within-group variation. If event type made no difference, the type means would scatter around the grand mean only as far as the within-group variation lets the means of groups of 11 to 28 events wander by chance.

ANOVA turns the comparison into a ratio. The between-group variation, scaled to a variance, is the numerator; the within-group variation, pooled across the groups, is the denominator. If the null hypothesis is true both estimate the same quantity, the variance of individual values, so their ratio, the F statistic, should be near 1. A large F says the means are further apart than the scatter within the groups explains.

For Lantern’s event types, Excel’s Analysis ToolPak gives F = 2.75 with a p-value of 0.035: at the 5% level, evidence that the event types don’t all have the same mean invoice per guest-day. The rest of the lesson shows where those numbers come from, which conditions they rest on (here they need care), how big the differences are, and which types differ.

In depth

The one-way ANOVA model

There are k groups. Group i has nin_ini​ observations with mean xˉi\bar{x}_ixˉi​ and sample variance si2s_i^2si2​; there are N=n1+⋯+nkN = n_1 + \dots + n_kN=n1​+⋯+nk​ observations in all, with grand mean xˉ\bar{x}xˉ. The model says each observation is its group’s population mean plus a random error, xij=μi+εijx_{ij} = \mu_i + \varepsilon_{ij}xij​=μi​+εij​, where the errors are independent, normally distributed and have the same variance σ2\sigma^2σ2 in every group. OpenStax lists the conditions the same way: normal populations, random and independent samples, equal standard deviations, a categorical factor and a numerical response.

Sums of squares

ANOVA splits the total variation into its two parts. The total sum of squares, SST, adds up every observation’s squared distance from the grand mean. The sum of squares between groups, SSB, adds up each group mean’s squared distance from the grand mean, weighted by the group’s size. The sum of squares within groups, SSW, adds up every observation’s squared distance from its own group’s mean, which is the same as ∑(ni−1)si2\sum (n_i - 1) s_i^2∑(ni​−1)si2​. The three always satisfy SST = SSB + SSW.

∑i∑j(xij−xˉ)2⏟SST=∑ini(xˉi−xˉ)2⏟SSB+∑i∑j(xij−xˉi)2⏟SSW\underbrace{\sum_{i}\sum_{j} (x_{ij} - \bar{x})^2}_{\text{SST}} = \underbrace{\sum_{i} n_i (\bar{x}_i - \bar{x})^2}_{\text{SSB}} + \underbrace{\sum_{i}\sum_{j} (x_{ij} - \bar{x}_i)^2}_{\text{SSW}}SSTi∑​j∑​(xij​−xˉ)2​​=SSBi∑​ni​(xˉi​−xˉ)2​​+SSWi∑​j∑​(xij​−xˉi​)2​​

For Lantern’s 76 events, SSB = 35.687, SSW = 230.759 and SST = 266.446, in dollars squared. Excel calculates each without the ToolPak: =DEVSQ(range) returns the sum of squared deviations from the range’s own mean, so SST is DEVSQ of all the values, SSW is the sum of each group’s DEVSQ, and SSB = SST − SSW. The worksheet in the Explore section builds a whole ANOVA table this way.

Degrees of freedom, mean squares and F

Each sum of squares has its degrees of freedom: k−1k - 1k−1 between groups (k group means, tied together by the grand mean), N−kN - kN−k within groups (N observations, k group means estimated from them) and N−1N - 1N−1 in total; the first two add up to the third. A mean square is a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom: MSB=SSB÷(k−1)\text{MSB} = \text{SSB} \div (k - 1)MSB=SSB÷(k−1) and MSW=SSW÷(N−k)\text{MSW} = \text{SSW} \div (N - k)MSW=SSW÷(N−k). The F statistic is their ratio, F=MSB÷MSWF = \text{MSB} \div \text{MSW}F=MSB÷MSW.

For Lantern, MSB=35.687÷4≈8.922\text{MSB} = 35.687 \div 4 \approx 8.922MSB=35.687÷4≈8.922, MSW=230.759÷71≈3.250\text{MSW} = 230.759 \div 71 \approx 3.250MSW=230.759÷71≈3.250 and F=8.922÷3.250≈2.75F = 8.922 \div 3.250 \approx 2.75F=8.922÷3.250≈2.75. MSW is the pooled variance of the pooled t test extended to k groups, and its square root, $1.80, is the typical distance of an event from its type’s mean. F is large only when MSB is large relative to MSW: when the group means are spread out more than the within-group scatter explains.

The F distribution

If the null hypothesis and the model’s conditions hold, F follows an F distribution with k − 1 and N − k degrees of freedom, the distribution of the ratio of two independent chi-square variables, each divided by its degrees of freedom. It is never negative and is skewed to the right. Its mean, df₂ ÷ (df₂ − 2), is close to 1 when the denominator df is large: about 1.03 for Lantern’s 71. Only a large F is evidence against the null hypothesis, so the test uses the right tail; OpenStax calls one-way ANOVA “always right-tailed”.

In Excel, =F.DIST.RT(x,deg_freedom1,deg_freedom2) returns the right-tail probability, the p-value: =F.DIST.RT(2.745,4,71) ≈ 0.035. =F.INV.RT(probability,deg_freedom1,deg_freedom2) returns the critical value: =F.INV.RT(0.05,4,71) ≈ 2.50, and Lantern’s F of 2.75 lies just beyond it. Microsoft’s examples: =F.DIST.RT(15.2068649,6,4) returns 0.01 and =F.INV.RT(0.01,6,4) returns 15.20686. The numerator df comes first, and swapping the two gives a different distribution; both functions truncate a df that isn’t a whole number.

With two groups, one-way ANOVA is the pooled two-sample t test of Comparing means: F equals t², with the same p-value. Microsoft’s ToolPak page says that with only two samples you can use T.TEST instead; with type 2, the pooled test, it gives the ANOVA’s two-tailed p-value. ANOVA earns its keep with three groups or more.

Excel’s Anova: Single Factor

With the Analysis ToolPak loaded (B03 showed how), choose Data > Data Analysis > Anova: Single Factor, which Microsoft describes as a simple analysis of variance on data for two or more samples. Put each group in its own column with its name in the first row, select the block as the Input Range, set Grouped By to Columns, tick Labels in first row, and leave Alpha at 0.05 unless you want another level. The groups needn’t be the same size: leave the cells below a shorter column empty, since a 0 would count as an observation. Like B03’s Descriptive Statistics and Regression tools it writes values, not formulas, so run it again when the data change.

The output has two tables. SUMMARY lists each group’s Count, Sum, Average and Variance (the sample variance, as VAR.S gives it). ANOVA has a row each for Between Groups, Within Groups and Total, with the columns SS, df, MS, F, P-value and F crit. Lantern’s, with the event types in five columns:

Checking the conditions

Independence. The observations should be independent: ideally random samples, or units assigned to groups at random. Lantern’s 76 events are not a random sample; they are every event of 2026, and the analysis treats them as a sample of the catering Lantern will go on doing. They also come from only 18 clients, Harbor Tech alone booking 11 events, and events for one client may resemble each other (the same office, menu and budget), so they are not fully independent. That is a caveat to state, not something a calculation can fix.

Normality. Each group should come from a normal population. ANOVA tolerates moderate departures, especially as samples grow (Penn State’s STAT 500 notes call its test statistic fairly robust), but outliers matter. Lantern’s meetings are right-skewed, with two invoices far above the rest ($14.62 and $17.14 per guest-day), and one team day earned $16.11.

Equal variances. The groups should share one population variance. A common rule of thumb, given in Penn State’s STAT 500 notes, is that the largest sample standard deviation should be no more than about twice the smallest. Lantern’s groups fail it: meetings have a standard deviation of $2.15 and launches $0.68, a ratio of 3.2, because launches are priced very consistently.

When the conditions are doubtful, check whether the conclusion depends on them. Welch’s ANOVA drops the equal-variance assumption by weighting each group by nᵢ ÷ sᵢ², and Delacre and colleagues (2019) recommend it as the default for comparing means. It isn’t in the ToolPak, but statistical software runs it: for Lantern it gives F ≈ 4.13 on 4 and 30.3 df, p ≈ 0.009. Here the largest group, meetings, also has the largest spread, which makes the ordinary F conservative; when the smallest group has the largest spread it errs the other way and finds differences too often.

The Kruskal–Wallis test compares the groups’ ranks instead of their values, so it doesn’t assume normality: for Lantern H ≈ 13.0 on 4 df, p ≈ 0.011. A log transformation (B02) is a third option for right-skewed groups. Here every route points the same way, so the conclusion that the types differ doesn’t rest on the equal-variance assumption.

How big are the differences?

A p-value says whether the means differ, not by how much. The simplest effect size for ANOVA is eta squared, η2=SSB÷SST\eta^2 = \text{SSB} \div \text{SST}η2=SSB÷SST, the share of the total variation that lies between the groups. For Lantern η2=35.687÷266.446≈0.134\eta^2 = 35.687 \div 266.446 \approx 0.134η2=35.687÷266.446≈0.134: 13.4% of the variation in invoice per guest-day goes with event type, and 86.6% is variation among events of the same type. Report it with the means themselves: the gap between the highest and lowest types is $2.25 per guest-day.

What a significant F does and doesn’t tell you

A significant F says the group means are not all equal. It doesn’t say which differ, or that they all differ: one group out of line is enough. A non-significant F is not proof of equal means, only a lack of evidence against them, as with any test.

To find which groups differ, compare pairs in a way that keeps the family-wise error rate in check. Tukey’s HSD (honestly significant difference) compares every pair and is the usual choice when all pairs matter; NIST’s handbook says it has the edge when all pairwise comparisons are of interest. It needs the studentised range distribution, for which Excel has no function, so it is run in statistical software. The Bonferroni correction works anywhere: to make m comparisons with a family-wise error rate of at most α, test each one at α ÷ m.

For Lantern’s five types m = 10, so each pair is tested at 0.05 ÷ 10 = 0.005. Because the variances differ, use Welch’s t test for each pair: with the types in columns A to E in the SUMMARY’s order, =T.TEST(D2:D12,E2:E12,2,3) compares launches with team days. Only that pair clears the bar, with p ≈ 0.004 and team days $2.25 higher; the next smallest p-value, conferences against launches, is 0.018. Tukey’s HSD, run in statistical software, also finds only this pair; its usual form assumes equal variances, but the Games–Howell version, which doesn’t, agrees.

Comparisons planned before seeing the data, such as each type against one standard type, need fewer adjustments than all pairs. Picking the biggest gap after looking and testing only that one is the multiple testing trap in disguise: the biggest of ten gaps is not a fair draw. The challenging example shows it happening.

Beyond one factor

Two-way ANOVA handles two categorical factors at once, for example event type and season, and tests whether their effects interact; the ToolPak’s Anova: Two-Factor With Replication needs the same number of observations for every combination (its dialog takes a single Rows per sample). ANOVA is also a special case of regression on group indicators, which B06 Regression Analysis develops. And the Kruskal–Wallis test above is the rank-based alternative when the groups are far from normal.

Common mistakes

  • “With five groups, run a t test on every pair.”

    Ten tests at α = 0.05 have roughly a 40% chance of at least one false alarm between them. Run the ANOVA first, then compare pairs with Tukey’s HSD or a Bonferroni adjustment.

  • “A significant F means every group differs.”

    It means the means are not all equal. One group out of line is enough: for Lantern only team days against launches survives the follow-up comparisons.

  • “p = 0.07, so the four teams perform the same.”

    A non-significant F is a lack of evidence, not proof of equal means. Small groups can miss real differences; say what size of difference the data could have detected, or collect more.

  • “F.DIST gives the ANOVA p-value.”

    =F.DIST(F,df1,df2,TRUE) is the left-tail probability. The p-value is the right tail, =F.DIST.RT(F,df1,df2), with the between-groups df first.

  • “The ToolPak checks the conditions for you.”

    It prints F and a p-value whatever the data look like. Check the spread (largest SD within about twice the smallest), outliers and independence yourself. Welch’s ANOVA allows for unequal variances and Kruskal–Wallis for non-normal groups; nothing fixes a lack of independence.

  • “A bigger F means bigger differences.”

    F grows with sample size as well as with the gaps between means. Judge size from the means and η², and significance from the p-value.

In practice

Analysts use one-way ANOVA wherever several groups are compared on one measure: handling time across call-centre teams, yield across production lines, basket value across store formats, test scores across training methods. In a designed experiment, where units are assigned to groups at random, a significant F supports a causal conclusion about the groups. In observational data such as Lantern’s it describes differences and leaves their causes open.

Fictional case

Ana’s note for her catering review reads: “Invoice per guest-day differed by event type (Welch’s ANOVA, which allows for the unequal spreads, p = 0.009; ordinary one-way ANOVA F = 2.75 on 4 and 71 df, p = 0.035; η² = 0.13). After a Bonferroni adjustment, only team days ($11.25) and launches ($9.00) differ clearly. These are all 2026 events from 18 clients, most of them repeat bookers (Harbor Tech alone booked 11), not random samples.” Before adding anything to the quote sheet she checks what else goes with team days: they ran 6.6 hours on average against 4.2 for launches, so she asks B06’s question, whether hours or type drives the price, before changing it.

The reporting habit is the same as for every test: the group sizes and means, F with its two degrees of freedom and its p-value, an effect size, the follow-up comparisons with the adjustment named, and the conditions that might weaken the result. A small table of means with the ANOVA line underneath usually tells a reader more than the ToolPak’s full output.

Summary

  • Testing every pair of k groups at α makes k(k − 1) ÷ 2 tests and inflates the family-wise error rate (about 40% for 10 independent tests at 5%). ANOVA tests H₀: all means equal, once.
  • SST = SSB + SSW: variation between group means (weighted by group size) plus variation within groups. In Excel, DEVSQ gives each.
  • MSB = SSB ÷ (k − 1), MSW = SSW ÷ (N − k), F = MSB ÷ MSW. Under H₀, F follows an F distribution with k − 1 and N − k df and is near 1.
  • The test is right-tailed: p = F.DIST.RT(F, k − 1, N − k); critical value F.INV.RT(α, k − 1, N − k). Anova: Single Factor prints both, as P-value and F crit.
  • Conditions: independence, normality within groups, equal variances (largest SD no more than about twice the smallest). Welch’s ANOVA allows for unequal variances and Kruskal–Wallis for non-normal groups; nothing fixes a lack of independence.
  • η² = SSB ÷ SST measures size. A significant F says not all means are equal; Tukey’s HSD or Bonferroni-adjusted tests (each at α ÷ m) say which differ.
  • A non-significant F is not proof of equal means, and in observational data a significant one is not proof of a cause.

Terms introduced

Analysis of variance
A method for testing whether several group means are equal by comparing the variation between the group means with the variation within the groups; one-way ANOVA has one categorical factor. Abbreviated ANOVA.
Family-wise error rate
The probability of at least one false positive (Type I error) among a set of tests when every null hypothesis is true. It grows with the number of tests.
Between-group variation
The variation of the group means around the grand mean, measured by the sum of squares between groups, Σ nᵢ(x̄ᵢ − x̄)².
Within-group variation
The variation of observations around their own group’s mean, measured by the sum of squares within groups, Σ(nᵢ − 1)sᵢ².
Mean square
A sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom: an estimate of variance. In one-way ANOVA, MS between = SSB ÷ (k − 1) and MS within = SSW ÷ (N − k).
F statistic
In one-way ANOVA, the ratio of the mean square between groups to the mean square within groups. It is near 1 when the null hypothesis of equal means is true and large when the means differ.
F distribution
The distribution of the ratio of two independent chi-square variables, each divided by its degrees of freedom. It has a numerator and a denominator df, is never negative and is skewed to the right.
Eta squared
An effect size for ANOVA: the sum of squares between groups divided by the total sum of squares, the share of the variation that lies between the groups.
Bonferroni correction
A way to control the family-wise error rate when making m comparisons: test each at α ÷ m, or multiply each p-value by m.
