Probability

Probability is the language every test and interval in this course is written in. Learn to read joint, marginal and conditional probabilities from a contingency table, to use the addition and multiplication rules, to check whether two events are independent, to draw a tree diagram, and to turn a screen’s accuracy into the probability that a flagged case is real with Bayes’ rule.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Define an experiment, its sample space and its events, and distinguish classical, relative-frequency and subjective probability.
  • Calculate joint, marginal and conditional probabilities from a contingency table, and the probability of a complement, a union and an intersection.
  • Decide whether two events are independent by comparing P(A | B) with P(A), and say what independence would predict for a table.
  • Use a tree diagram and the multiplication rule for events in sequence, with and without replacement.
  • Apply Bayes’ rule to a screening problem, explaining the role of the base rate and of false positives.
  • Calculate the number of possible samples and arrangements with COMBIN and PERMUT.

Review first

  • Describing distributions — Proportions, the notation for parameters and statistics, and the 231 spring orders.
  • PivotTables — A PivotTable of channel against subscription, and % of Row Total, are the contingency tables of this lesson.
  • Interpreting results: charts, comparisons and causes — Association is not causation, and confounding, which matter when two events turn out not to be independent.
  • Collecting data: sources, samples and bias — Simple random samples, which make every order equally likely to be chosen.

The idea

Fictional case

In B03, a PivotTable of the spring orders showed that app orders were more often subscriptions than web orders, and Ana wants to say it precisely. If she picks one of the 231 orders at random, how likely is it to be a subscription, and does knowing that it came through the app change that? And when Tillwise (fictional), the company that processes Lantern’s card payments, flags an order as possible fraud, how likely is the order to be fraudulent? All three are questions of probability.

Experiments, sample spaces and events

An experiment is any process with an uncertain outcome: drawing an order at random, the next customer’s rating, whether a quote is accepted. Its sample space is the set of all possible outcomes, and an event is any set of outcomes. Picking one spring order at random has a sample space of 231 orders; “the order is a subscription” is an event containing 104 of them.

A probability is a number from 0 to 1 that measures how likely an event is: 0 for impossible, 1 for certain. The probabilities of all the outcomes in a sample space add to 1. Writing P(A)P(A)P(A) for the probability of event AAA, the chance that a randomly drawn spring order is a subscription is P(sub)=104÷231≈0.450P(\text{sub}) = 104 \div 231 \approx 0.450P(sub)=104÷231≈0.450.

Three ways to assign a probability

  • Classical: when every outcome is equally likely, P(A)P(A)P(A) is the number of outcomes in AAA divided by the number in the sample space. A draw at random from the 231 orders is exactly such an experiment, which is why 104÷231104 \div 231104÷231 is the probability.
  • Relative frequency: the share of times an event happens in a long run of repetitions. Treat 45.0% as the chance that Ana’s next order is a subscription and you are using the spring’s relative frequency as an estimate. The law of large numbers says that the relative frequency settles closer and closer to the true probability as the repetitions grow, which is why estimates from many orders are steadier than estimates from few.
  • Subjective: a judgement of how likely an event is, used when there is no long run to count. Ana’s view that there is a 60% chance her largest café client renews its contract (fictional) is a subjective probability. It must still obey the rules below, and it should be revised when evidence arrives, which is what Bayes’ rule does.

Reading probabilities from a contingency table

A contingency table counts observations by two categorical variables at once: B03’s PivotTable of channel against subscription is one. Dividing every count by the grand total turns it into a table of probabilities for an order drawn at random.

Complements and “or”

The complement of an event AAA, written A′A'A′ (“not A”), is every outcome not in AAA, so P(A′)=1−P(A)P(A') = 1 - P(A)P(A′)=1−P(A): the chance an order is one-off is 1−0.450=0.5501 - 0.450 = 0.5501−0.450=0.550. For “or”, add the two probabilities and take away the overlap, which would otherwise be counted twice: P(app or sub)=0.429+0.450−0.247=0.632P(\text{app or sub}) = 0.429 + 0.450 - 0.247 = 0.632P(app or sub)=0.429+0.450−0.247=0.632. Check it with counts: the 99 app orders plus the 47 web subscriptions make 146 orders, and 146÷231≈0.632146 \div 231 \approx 0.632146÷231≈0.632.

Two events are mutually exclusive when they can’t happen together, like “web order” and “app order” for the same order. Their overlap is empty, so the probability of one or the other is the plain sum: 0.571+0.429=10.571 + 0.429 = 10.571+0.429=1.

Independence

Two events are independent when knowing that one happened doesn’t change the probability of the other: P(A∣B)=P(A)P(A \mid B) = P(A)P(A∣B)=P(A). Here it fails. An order’s chance of being a subscription is 0.450 overall but 0.576 among app orders and 0.356 among web orders. In these orders, channel and subscription are associated.

Independence also predicts what the table would look like. If channel told you nothing about subscription, the 99 app orders would contain subscriptions in the overall proportion, 99×104÷231≈44.699 \times 104 \div 231 \approx 44.699×104÷231≈44.6, instead of the 57 observed. Whether a gap of about 12 orders is more than chance would produce in 231 orders is a test question; module 4’s chi-square test answers it. And even a real association wouldn’t show that the app causes subscriptions: app users may be Lantern’s most loyal customers in the first place.

In depth

The rules of probability

Every way of assigning probabilities must obey the same rules. For any event, 0≤P(A)≤10 \leq P(A) \leq 10≤P(A)≤1, and the whole sample space has probability 1. The complement rule follows: P(A′)=1−P(A)P(A') = 1 - P(A)P(A′)=1−P(A). The addition rule gives “or”, the union A∪BA \cup BA∪B: P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B)P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B). Here “and” is the intersection A∩BA \cap BA∩B, the outcomes in both events, and its probability is 0 for mutually exclusive events.

Conditional probability is defined from the joint and marginal probabilities, P(A∣B)=P(A and B)÷P(B)P(A \mid B) = P(A \text{ and } B) \div P(B)P(A∣B)=P(A and B)÷P(B), for any BBB with P(B)>0P(B) > 0P(B)>0. Rearranged, it is the multiplication rule: P(A and B)=P(B)×P(A∣B)P(A \text{ and } B) = P(B) \times P(A \mid B)P(A and B)=P(B)×P(A∣B). With the table, P(app and sub)=0.429×0.576≈0.247P(\text{app and sub}) = 0.429 \times 0.576 \approx 0.247P(app and sub)=0.429×0.576≈0.247, the same joint probability as before.

For independent events the multiplication rule simplifies to P(A and B)=P(A)×P(B)P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)P(A and B)=P(A)×P(B). Three checks are equivalent, and any one of them shows independence: P(A∣B)=P(A)P(A \mid B) = P(A)P(A∣B)=P(A), P(B∣A)=P(B)P(B \mid A) = P(B)P(B∣A)=P(B), or P(A and B)=P(A)P(B)P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A)P(B)P(A and B)=P(A)P(B). For channel and subscription, P(app)P(sub)=0.429×0.450≈0.193P(\text{app})P(\text{sub}) = 0.429 \times 0.450 \approx 0.193P(app)P(sub)=0.429×0.450≈0.193, against the joint probability of 0.247.

Caution: Mutually exclusive is not independent

The two ideas are often confused, but they are close to opposites. If two events with positive probabilities are mutually exclusive, knowing that one happened tells you the other didn’t: its probability drops to 0, so they can’t be independent. Independence is about whether one event carries information about the other; mutual exclusivity is about whether they can happen together.

Probabilities in Excel

A PivotTable gives the counts, and B03’s % of Grand Total, % of Row Total and % of Column Total turn them into joint and conditional probabilities. The same numbers come from formulas on the order sheet, with channel in column K and subscription in column L: =COUNTIFS(K2:K232,"app",L2:L232,"yes")/COUNT(J2:J232) is the joint probability 0.247, and =COUNTIFS(K2:K232,"app",L2:L232,"yes")/COUNTIF(K2:K232,"app") is the conditional probability 0.576. The pattern is always the same: the count of orders that meet both conditions, divided by the count of orders that meet the condition after the bar (or by all orders, for a joint probability).

Formulas have two advantages over a PivotTable for this work. They update when the data change, and they make the condition visible: anyone reading COUNTIF(K2:K232,"app") in the denominator can see that the probability is conditional on an app order. When a report quotes a percentage, the first check is the denominator, because a share of all orders, a share of app orders and a share of subscription orders are three different probabilities.

Tree diagrams

A tree diagram shows events in sequence. Each branch carries the probability of an event given everything to its left, so the first branches are marginal probabilities and later ones conditional. Multiplying along a path gives the joint probability of that path (the multiplication rule), and the paths’ probabilities add to 1.

Adding the paths that end in “subscription” recovers the marginal probability: 0.203+0.247=0.4500.203 + 0.247 = 0.4500.203+0.247=0.450. This is the law of total probability: P(A)=P(B)P(A∣B)+P(B′)P(A∣B′)P(A) = P(B)P(A \mid B) + P(B')P(A \mid B')P(A)=P(B)P(A∣B)+P(B′)P(A∣B′). It is the denominator of Bayes’ rule.

Sampling with and without replacement

Draw two spring orders at random. If the first is put back before the second is drawn (with replacement), the draws are independent and P(both subscriptions)=(104÷231)2≈0.2027P(\text{both subscriptions}) = (104 \div 231)^2 \approx 0.2027P(both subscriptions)=(104÷231)2≈0.2027. If it isn’t (without replacement), the second draw depends on the first: 104÷231×103÷230≈0.2016104 \div 231 \times 103 \div 230 \approx 0.2016104÷231×103÷230≈0.2016. Surveys and audits sample without replacement, but when the sample is a small part of the population the two answers are almost the same. That is why the next lesson can treat a few draws from a large population as independent; OpenStax’s rule of thumb is a sample of fewer than 10% of the population.

Bayes’ rule

Tillwise screens every card payment and flags some as possible fraud. Suppose (all rates fictional) that 0.4% of orders are fraudulent, the base rate; that the screen flags 95% of fraudulent orders; and that it also flags 2% of genuine ones, its false positive rate. If an order is flagged, what is the probability it is fraudulent? The screen’s 95% is P(flag∣fraud)P(\text{flag} \mid \text{fraud})P(flag∣fraud); the question asks for P(fraud∣flag)P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{flag})P(fraud∣flag). Bayes’ rule turns one into the other:

P(fraud∣flag)=P(fraud) P(flag∣fraud)P(fraud) P(flag∣fraud)+P(genuine) P(flag∣genuine)=0.004×0.950.004×0.95+0.996×0.02≈0.160P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{flag}) = \frac{P(\text{fraud})\,P(\text{flag} \mid \text{fraud})}{P(\text{fraud})\,P(\text{flag} \mid \text{fraud}) + P(\text{genuine})\,P(\text{flag} \mid \text{genuine})} = \frac{0.004 \times 0.95}{0.004 \times 0.95 + 0.996 \times 0.02} \approx 0.160P(fraud∣flag)=P(fraud)P(flag∣fraud)+P(genuine)P(flag∣genuine)P(fraud)P(flag∣fraud)​=0.004×0.95+0.996×0.020.004×0.95​≈0.160

Only about 16% of flagged orders are fraudulent. Natural frequencies make the reason visible. Of 10,000 orders, 40 are fraudulent and 38 of them are flagged; of the 9,960 genuine orders, 2%, about 199, are flagged too. So 38 of about 237 flagged orders are fraud. When the base rate is low, even a small false-positive rate produces more false alarms than real cases.

Confusing P(flag∣fraud)P(\text{flag} \mid \text{fraud})P(flag∣fraud) with P(fraud∣flag)P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{flag})P(fraud∣flag) is sometimes called the confusion of the inverse; it usually comes from ignoring the base rate (base-rate neglect), and it lies behind many bad decisions about screening tests, alarms and audits. The reverse question gives a reassuring answer: an order that is not flagged is fraudulent with probability about 0.0002, two in ten thousand. A screen like this is good at clearing orders and poor at convicting them.

Counting outcomes: COMBIN and PERMUT

Classical probability needs the number of equally likely outcomes, and counting them is often the hard part. The number of ways to choose kkk items from nnn when order doesn’t matter is the number of combinations, (nk)=n!÷(k! (n−k)!)\binom{n}{k} = n! \div (k!\,(n - k)!)(kn​)=n!÷(k!(n−k)!), Excel’s =COMBIN(n,k); when order matters it is the number of permutations, n!÷(n−k)!n! \div (n - k)!n!÷(n−k)!, =PERMUT(n,k). Here n!n!n! (“n factorial”) is n×(n−1)×⋯×1n \times (n - 1) \times \dots \times 1n×(n−1)×⋯×1.

Ana could draw 5,247,527,901 different samples of 5 orders from the 231: =COMBIN(231,5). A simple random sample makes each of them equally likely. Choosing 3 of 5 trainees for a photo can be done in =COMBIN(5,3) = 10 ways, but lining 3 of them up in order in =PERMUT(5,3) = 60 ways, because each group of 3 can be arranged in 3!=63! = 63!=6 orders. Combinations return in the next lesson, where they count the ways a binomial outcome can happen.

Common mistakes

  • “P(A | B) and P(B | A) are the same thing.”

    They answer different questions. 57.6% of app orders are subscriptions, but 54.8% of subscriptions are app orders. With a rare event the gap is enormous: the fraud screen flags 95% of fraud, yet only 16% of flagged orders are fraud.

  • “Mutually exclusive events are independent.”

    They are the opposite: if two events can’t happen together, knowing one happened makes the other impossible. Independent events can happen together, and one tells you nothing about the other.

  • “P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).”

    Only when A and B can’t both happen. Otherwise the overlap is counted twice: P(app or subscription) is 0.429 + 0.450 − 0.247 = 0.632, not 0.879.

  • “The conditional probabilities differ, so one causes the other.”

    A difference between P(subscription | app) and P(subscription | web) shows association in these orders. It may be chance (module 4 tests that), and even a real association can come from a confounding variable, such as loyal customers preferring the app.

  • “Ignore the base rate: the screen is 95% accurate.”

    A screen’s sensitivity and false-positive rate describe how it treats cases whose truth is known. What a flagged case means depends on how common the condition is. Always put the base rate into Bayes’ rule, or count natural frequencies.

In practice

Conditional probabilities are how businesses read their own data. A retailer compares the share of app customers who return with the share of web customers; an insurer prices a policy from claim rates conditional on age and region; a bank tunes a fraud model by trading its sensitivity against its false-positive rate. In each case the first question is “conditional on what?”, and the second is whether the comparison is between groups that differ in other ways too.

Bayes’ rule is the arithmetic of screening, and screening is everywhere: credit checks, quality inspections, spam filters, medical tests, audit samples. The managerial lesson is that the value of a flag depends on the base rate. A screen that is excellent in a population where the problem is common can be mostly false alarms where it is rare, so a business should decide what a flag triggers (a review, a call, a hold) from the expected numbers of true and false flags, not from the screen’s headline accuracy.

Summary

  • A probability is a number from 0 to 1; it can be classical (equally likely outcomes), a relative frequency (a long-run share) or subjective (a judgement), and all obey the same rules.
  • From a contingency table: joint probabilities come from the cells, marginal probabilities from the totals, conditional probabilities from a cell divided by its row or column total.
  • P(A′) = 1 − P(A); P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B); P(A and B) = P(B) × P(A | B).
  • A and B are independent when P(A | B) = P(A); independence predicts each cell’s count as row total × column total ÷ grand total.
  • Mutually exclusive events can’t happen together and are not independent; P(A | B) is not P(B | A).
  • A tree diagram multiplies along branches for joint probabilities; without replacement, later branches change.
  • Bayes’ rule turns P(evidence | condition) into P(condition | evidence); with a low base rate most flags can be false positives.
  • COMBIN counts unordered selections and PERMUT ordered ones; a simple random sample makes every combination equally likely.

Terms introduced

Sample space
The set of all possible outcomes of an experiment, such as the 231 orders that a random draw could pick.
Event
A set of outcomes of an experiment, such as “the order drawn is a subscription”.
Probability
A number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain) that measures how likely an event is; the probabilities of all the outcomes in a sample space add to 1.
Relative frequency
The number of times an event occurs divided by the number of trials or observations; used as an estimate of its probability.
Law of large numbers
As an experiment is repeated more and more times, the relative frequency of an event tends to get closer to its probability, and the average of the results to the expected value.
Contingency table
A table that counts observations by two categorical variables at once, one in the rows and one in the columns, with row and column totals.
Joint probability
The probability that two events both happen, P(A and B); in a contingency table, a cell count divided by the grand total.
Marginal probability
The probability of one event regardless of the others; in a contingency table, a row or column total divided by the grand total.
Conditional probability
The probability of an event given that another has happened, P(A | B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B).
Complement
All the outcomes not in an event A, written A′; P(A′) = 1 − P(A).
Mutually exclusive
Describes events that can’t happen together, so P(A and B) = 0 and P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).
Independent
Describes events where knowing one has happened doesn’t change the probability of the other: P(A | B) = P(A), equivalently P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Two categorical variables are independent when this holds for every pair of their categories.
Tree diagram
A diagram of events in sequence whose branches carry probabilities, conditional on the branches before them; multiplying along a path gives its joint probability.
Bayes’ rule
P(A | B) = P(A) P(B | A) ÷ [P(A) P(B | A) + P(A′) P(B | A′)]: how the probability of A changes once evidence B is seen.
Base rate
How common a condition is in the population before any evidence is seen, such as the share of orders that are fraudulent; the prior probability in Bayes’ rule.
False positive
A case flagged by a screen or test that does not have the condition, such as a genuine order flagged as fraud. The false-positive rate is the share of cases without the condition that the screen flags, P(flag | no condition).
