Partial correlation

Ask what remains of a correlation when a third variable is held fixed: by comparing like with like, by correlating residuals, or with the first-order formula. Then learn which variables to hold fixed (confounders) and which not to (mediators and colliders).

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain what it means to hold a third variable fixed, and compare days that are alike on it to see the idea at work.
  • Calculate a partial correlation by correlating residuals and with the first-order formula, in Excel and by hand, and test it with n − 2 − k degrees of freedom.
  • Interpret a partial correlation that differs in size or sign from the simple correlation, without claiming more than it shows.
  • Distinguish confounders, mediators and colliders, and explain why controlling for a mediator or a collider misleads.
  • Identify the limits of partial correlation: straight-line control only, measurement error in the control, and variables left out.

Review first

  • Correlation matrices — The matrix in which peak barista hours and peak waits correlate at +0.53, the puzzle this lesson resolves.
  • Testing a correlation — The t test of a correlation, which a partial correlation reuses with fewer degrees of freedom.
  • Curves, thresholds and restricted ranges — The steep rise in waits once the load passes about seven transactions per barista hour.
  • Descriptive statistics and regression in Excel — SLOPE, INTERCEPT and the least-squares line, used here only to take out a third variable’s straight-line part.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana’s correlation matrix shows peak barista hours and the peak wait correlating at +0.53 over both cafés’ 612 days. Read at face value, days with more baristas at the 7–11 a.m. peak had longer waits, and a manager under pressure to cut costs might conclude that extra baristas get in each other’s way. Ana suspects something else: both numbers rise on busy days. More customers mean longer queues, and her managers roster more baristas on days they expect to be busy.

Comparing like with like

The way to check is to compare days that were equally busy. If the extra baristas are only a sign of a busy day, then among days with about the same number of transactions the link should disappear; if baristas shorten queues, it should turn negative. The table splits the 612 days by transactions and finds the correlation of peak barista hours with the peak wait inside each band.

Inside every band the correlation is negative: −0.58 and −0.59 on days with 100 to 199 transactions, where queues build, and only −0.13 on quiet days, when the wait is about two minutes whatever the roster. Among equally busy days, more barista hours go with shorter waits. The positive correlation over all days came from mixing quiet days, with few baristas and short waits, with busy days, with more baristas and longer waits. This is the same mechanism as the groups-and-simpsons-paradox lesson, with a continuous variable instead of two cafés.

Bands are a rough tool. They throw away information (days with 101 and 149 transactions are treated alike), the bands can be few days each, and with two or three variables to hold fixed the bands multiply fast. Partial correlation does the same job with all the data at once.

Holding transactions fixed with residuals

Draw the least-squares line through the scatterplot of peak barista hours against transactions. Each day’s residual is its barista hours minus the line’s value at its number of transactions: positive on days rostered more heavily than usual for that much trade, negative on days rostered lightly. Do the same for the peak wait: a positive residual means a longer wait than usual for that many transactions. Both residuals have had transactions’ straight-line part taken out, so their correlation is the link between barista hours and waits with transactions held fixed. That correlation is the partial correlation.

For the 612 days it is rSW⋅T=−0.725r_{SW \cdot T} = -0.725rSW⋅T​=−0.725, where S is peak barista hours, W the peak wait and T transactions, and the dot is read “holding fixed”. The sign has flipped: on days with more barista hours than usual for their trade, waits were shorter than usual for their trade. The Explore widget plots the two sets of residuals; switch it to the raw values to see the +0.53 cloud again.

Key idea: A partial correlation answers a different question

The simple correlation, +0.53, asks: on days with more barista hours, were waits longer? Yes, because those were busy days. The partial correlation, −0.72, asks: on days equally busy, did more barista hours go with shorter waits? Yes. Both are correct answers to their own questions. Ana’s roster decision depends on the second.

The first-order formula

It is called first-order because it holds one variable fixed; a simple correlation is zero-order.

The case was built this way. The generator lets the peak wait depend on transactions per barista hour, so more baristas shorten the wait at a given level of trade, and lets the managers roster for the trade they expect. The partial correlation recovers the planted direction from the log; the simple correlation points the wrong way. With real data no one tells you what was planted, which is why the correlation-and-causation lesson asks what more evidence a causal claim needs.

In depth

Definition and notation

The partial correlation of x and y holding z fixed, rxy⋅zr_{xy\cdot z}rxy⋅z​, is the correlation between the residuals of x after a least-squares fit on z and the residuals of y after a least-squares fit on z. With several variables held fixed, z1,…,zkz_1, \dots, z_kz1​,…,zk​, fit each of x and y on all of them at once (multiple regression, B06) and correlate the residuals: rxy⋅z1…zkr_{xy\cdot z_1 \dots z_k}rxy⋅z1​…zk​​ is of order k. The first-order formula gives the same number as the residual method when one variable is held fixed. Like any correlation it lies between −1 and 1.

Ana can hold the café fixed as well as transactions, as a 0/1 variable (1 for Millbrook). The second-order partial correlation is −0.719, almost unchanged from −0.725, so the difference between the cafés was not what made the simple correlation positive. Inside each café separately, with transactions held fixed, it is −0.54 at Harbourside and −0.83 at Millbrook: the direction agrees, the strength differs.

Partial correlation in Excel

There is no PARTIAL function. Two routes give the same answer for one control. The formula route: three CORREL cells and the first-order formula, =(B1-B2*B3)/SQRT((1-B2^2)*(1-B3^2)) with r_xy, r_xz and r_yz in B1:B3. The residual route: a column of residuals for each variable, =C2-(INTERCEPT(C$2:C$32,$D$2:$D$32)+SLOPE(C$2:C$32,$D$2:$D$32)*$D2) with the control in column D, filled down and across, then CORREL of the two residual columns. The residual route shows each day’s residual, so you can plot them and spot the days that drive the result, and it extends to several controls with LINEST.

What a partial correlation can’t do

  • It removes only the straight-line part of the control. Waits rise along a curve as trade grows (a threshold effect), and the bands show the link between baristas and waits is weak on quiet days and strong on busy ones. The single partial correlation, −0.72, averages over that. Partial correlation on ranks (−0.47 here) or a comparison within bands can show what the straight line misses.
  • It can’t hold fixed what wasn’t measured. Anything that affects waits apart from the number of transactions, and also goes with the roster, is still in the comparison. A large catering order that ties up a barista at the peak, or an espresso machine that breaks down, lengthens waits without adding transactions; if managers tend to roster extra help on those mornings, holding transactions fixed doesn’t remove it.
  • A control measured with error controls only partly. If the door counter misses some customers, holding footfall fixed leaves some of the busy-day effect in, a problem called residual confounding.
  • It is still a correlation. A partial correlation says how two variables go together at given values of a third; whether one causes the other needs the evidence of the next lesson.

Which variables to hold fixed

The arithmetic will hold anything fixed. Whether it should depends on how the variables are connected, which the data alone can’t tell you; it comes from knowing how the business works and the order in which things happen. Three cases matter.

A confounding variable affects both variables, as a busy day drives both the roster and the queue. Holding it fixed removes the link it creates, which is what Ana did with transactions.

A mediator lies on the path from one variable to the other: x affects m, and m affects y. Holding a mediator fixed removes the part of the link that runs through it, so the partial correlation measures only what is left, not the whole effect. At Harbourside warm days bring more people to the waterfront, and more people buy more; the case’s generator lets the temperature reach sales only through footfall. Leaving out the five festival days, whose crowds have nothing to do with the weather, the high temperature and sales correlate at 0.49 over the other 301 days; holding footfall fixed gives −0.02. The 0.49 answers “do warm days go with higher sales?”, and the answer is yes. The −0.02 answers a narrower question, “at a given footfall, do warm days sell more?”, and the answer is no, which is what a mediator predicts: once footfall is fixed, the route is closed. (With the festival days in, the partial correlation is −0.19, because sales rise along a curve in footfall and the festivals sit at its far end; a straight-line control can’t remove that, which is the first limit listed above.) Within the case the peak load is the starkest example: it is transactions per barista hour, the route through which baristas shorten waits, and holding it fixed leaves barista hours and waits correlating at +0.07, as if baristas didn’t matter.

A collider is affected by both variables. Holding a collider fixed creates a link between its causes even if they are unrelated, because among days with the same value of the collider, a high value of one cause must be offset by the other (Cole and colleagues, 2010, give clear examples). At Harbourside the high temperature and rain are almost unrelated (r = 0.05), but warm days raise footfall and rain lowers it. Holding footfall fixed gives a partial correlation of 0.17 (p = 0.004): among days with the same footfall, the warmer ones were more often wet, because a warm, dry day would have drawn more people. The link is created by the analysis, not by the weather.

Caution: More controls are not always better

It is tempting to hold fixed every variable in the dataset “to be safe”. That removes confounding but can also remove the effect you care about (a mediator) or create a false link (a collider). Choose controls from what you know about the order and mechanism of the process, and say which you held fixed and why.

Common mistakes

  • “The partial correlation is the real correlation; the simple one was wrong.”

    Both are correct answers to different questions. The simple correlation describes how barista hours and waits went together across all days; the partial correlation describes how they went together at a given level of trade. Say which question you are answering.

  • “I held everything fixed to be safe.”

    Holding a mediator fixed removes the effect you want to measure (holding the peak load fixed makes baristas look useless); holding a collider fixed creates a link that doesn’t exist (temperature and rain, once footfall is fixed). Choose each control from what you know about the process.

  • “Holding transactions fixed means the busy-day effect is gone completely.”

    A partial correlation removes only the straight-line part of the control’s link, and only for the control as measured. The baristas–wait link is weak on quiet days and strong on busy ones, and anything unmeasured that drives both is still there. Look at bands or ranks, and list what you couldn’t hold fixed.

  • “I used n − 2 degrees of freedom for the partial correlation.”

    Each variable held fixed uses up one more degree of freedom: test with n − 2 − k. With 612 rows it hardly matters; with 30 rows and three controls it does.

  • “A negative partial correlation proves that baristas shorten waits.”

    It shows that, at a given level of trade, the two went together that way. An unmeasured confounder could produce the same pattern without any effect of the extra hours: if the manager put her most experienced baristas on the mornings she rostered extra hours, their speed, not the hours, could shorten the waits. Causal claims need the evidence of the next lesson.

In practice

Partial correlation is how analysts ask whether a link survives once an obvious common driver is accounted for. A retailer finds that stores with more staff have more shrinkage, and holds store size fixed. A hospital finds that wards with more nurses have more incidents, and holds the number and severity of patients fixed. A bank finds that customers who use its mobile app more often hold larger balances, and holds their income fixed. In each case the simple correlation mixes the link of interest with the effect of size, busyness or means, and the partial correlation compares like with like.

Fictional case

Ana’s note to her operations manager reads: “Over the 612 café-days, more peak barista hours went with longer waits (r = +0.53), because both rise on busy days. Holding the day’s transactions fixed, the link reverses: on days with the same trade, more barista hours went with shorter waits (partial r = −0.72, p below 0.001), in both cafés, and most clearly on days with more than 100 transactions. I did not hold the load per barista fixed, because it is the route by which baristas would shorten queues. This is observational, so before changing the roster I would like to trial it.”

Her note does what a good partial-correlation report does. It gives the simple and the partial correlation side by side, so the reader sees what holding transactions fixed changed. It names the control and explains why it is a confounder, and names the variable deliberately not held fixed and why. It shows where the link is strong and where it is weak rather than relying on one number. And it stops short of a causal claim, which the last lesson of the module takes up.

In regression (B06) the same idea appears as a coefficient: the slope on barista hours in a model that also contains transactions measures the change in waits per extra barista hour at a given level of trade. Partial correlation gives the strength of that link on the −1 to 1 scale; regression gives its size in minutes per hour. Both depend on the same choice of controls.

Summary

  • A partial correlation measures the link between two variables holding one or more others fixed. With one control it is r_xy·z = (r_xy − r_xz r_yz) ÷ √((1 − r_xz²)(1 − r_yz²)).
  • It equals the correlation of the residuals of x and y after each is fitted on the control by least squares; comparing like with like in bands shows the same idea roughly.
  • Peak barista hours and waits correlate at +0.53 because both rise on busy days; holding transactions fixed the partial correlation is −0.72, the sign the case planted.
  • Test a partial correlation with t = r√((n − 2 − k) ÷ (1 − r²)) on n − 2 − k degrees of freedom, k being the number of controls.
  • Hold confounders fixed. Don’t hold a mediator fixed when you want the whole link (holding the load fixed hides the baristas’ effect), and never hold a collider fixed (it creates a link between temperature and rain).
  • A partial correlation removes only the straight-line part of the measured controls: curves, measurement error and unmeasured drivers remain. It is still a correlation, not proof of cause.

Terms introduced

Partial correlation
The correlation between two variables after the straight-line part of their links with one or more other variables has been removed: the correlation of their residuals. Written r_xy·z for one control z.
Mediator
A variable on the path between two others, through which one affects the other (x affects m, and m affects y). Holding it fixed removes the part of the link that runs through it.
Collider
A variable affected by two others. Holding it fixed, or selecting on it, creates an association between its causes even when they are unrelated.
Residual confounding
The confounding that remains after adjusting for a confounder that is measured imperfectly or held fixed only roughly, such as by a straight line or in broad bands.
