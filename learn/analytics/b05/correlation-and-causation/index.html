Correlation and causation

Learn the six ways two variables can be correlated without one causing the other, what kinds of evidence do support a causal claim, and how to write a finding that says exactly as much as the data allow.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Identify, for a given correlation, the explanations other than cause: chance, confounding, reverse causation, common trends, selection and pooled groups.
  • Detect and remove a common trend by correlating changes or by working within shorter periods, and explain what the result shows.
  • Evaluate the evidence a causal claim needs (experiments, natural experiments, dose–response, timing, consistency and mechanism), using Bradford Hill’s considerations as considerations rather than a checklist.
  • Decide which correlations a manager can act on for prediction and which need causal evidence before an intervention.
  • Write a finding from correlational data that states the association, its size and its limits without overclaiming.

Review first

  • Partial correlation — Confounders, mediators and colliders, and the barista-hours example this lesson returns to.
  • Correlation matrices — The many-tests problem, one of the ways chance produces correlations.
  • Groups and Simpson’s paradox — Pooled groups that reverse a correlation.
  • Comparing two means — The two-sample t test that analyses a randomised trial, and why random assignment allows a causal reading.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana has three questions from her matrix and her partial correlations, and a new one from her marketing assistant: Harbourside’s loyalty-app members and the day’s high temperature correlate at +0.87 from March to July. “Warm weather gets people to sign up,” the assistant says, “so we should push the app hardest in summer.” In August–December the same two numbers correlate at −0.90. Ana wants to know which of her correlations she can act on.

Why correlation is not causation

To say that x causes y is to say that changing x would change y: if Ana rostered one more barista on a given morning, the wait that morning would be shorter than it would otherwise have been. That “otherwise” can never be observed on the same morning, so causal claims are always inferences. A correlation shows only that x and y moved together in the data. OpenStax’s business statistics text puts it plainly: “Strong correlation does not suggest that X1 causes X2 or X2 causes X1.” Six other explanations can produce a correlation, and a causal claim has to rule them out.

Six explanations other than cause

  1. Chance. With few rows, sizeable correlations arise from noise; with many tests, some cells are “significant” by luck. The correlation-matrices lesson showed 18 of 28 June cells at Harbourside passing an unadjusted test, and only 10 to 16 surviving adjustment.
  2. Confounding. A third variable drives both. Busy days bring both more baristas and longer waits (r = +0.53), so the simple correlation said the opposite of the truth until transactions were held fixed (−0.72).
  3. Reverse causation. y drives x, not x drives y. Peak barista hours correlate with sales at 0.75, but the managers roster for the trade they expect, so expected sales set the roster. Holding the day’s transactions fixed, barista hours and sales correlate at −0.13: baristas don’t bring customers in.
  4. Common trends. Two series that both drift over time correlate whatever their connection. Loyalty members only ever rise; temperature rises to July and falls after. Together they correlate at +0.87 in spring and −0.90 in autumn.
  5. Selection. The rows were chosen in a way connected to both variables. Studying only the cafés that survived, the customers who answered a survey, or the days someone complained creates links that the full population doesn’t have (the collider of the partial-correlation lesson).
  6. Pooled groups. Two groups with different levels, pooled, can show a correlation that neither has inside it, or the reverse: footfall and average ticket are −0.32 pooled but positive inside each café.

These are not exotic. In a daily log like Ana’s, every one of them is present somewhere, and a correlation that has not been checked against all six is a lead, not a finding. The word for a correlation produced by one of them rather than by cause is a spurious correlation.

Two trends: members and the weather

The assistant’s +0.87 comes entirely from time. Members are a running total, so they rise every day whatever the weather; in spring the temperature also rises, so the two correlate positively; in autumn it falls, so they correlate negatively. Over the whole year the two trends partly cancel (−0.13). A number that flips sign when the calendar moves is telling you about the calendar.

The fix is to ask the question of the right quantity. Ana’s question is whether warm days bring sign-ups, so compare each day’s new members (today’s total minus yesterday’s) with the day’s high. That correlation is +0.35 in spring and +0.39 in autumn: modest, positive and the same in both halves. Warm days do go with more sign-ups at Harbourside, probably because they bring more customers, and about 4% of transactions sign up. The trend made the link look stronger than it is in spring and reversed it in autumn.

What would show causation

The strongest evidence comes from changing x on purpose. In a randomised experiment the analyst decides which units get the change by chance, a coin or a random number for each, so that nothing else (busy days, weather, the manager’s judgement) can line up with it. OpenStax’s introductory text describes how random assignment spreads lurking variables evenly across the groups. B04’s A/B tests are randomised experiments. Ana could flip a coin each weekday morning to decide whether to add a barista hour at the peak, and after eight weeks compare waits on extra-barista days with the rest.

When an experiment is impossible or too costly, other kinds of evidence each rule out some of the six explanations:

  • A natural experiment: something outside anyone’s choice changes x for some units and not others. Rain is not chosen by the cafés, and on Harbourside weekdays with at least a quarter-inch of rain, sales averaged $206 against $296 on dry weekdays. The managers didn’t plan the rain, so the gap isn’t their roster or their promotions.
  • A dose–response relationship: more of x goes with more of y, step by step. Peak waits average about 1.6 minutes at loads under seven transactions per barista hour, 3.1 at nine to ten, and 9.4 at twelve or more. A gradient is harder for chance to produce than a single difference, though a confounder can produce one too.
  • Timing: the cause comes first. A day’s weather is fixed before its sales, so sales can’t cause the weather; the roster is set before the day, from the expected trade, which is why the roster can follow expected sales rather than cause them.
  • Consistency: the same link in different places, periods and data. The negative partial correlation of barista hours and waits holds in both cafés, in every band of trade and in single months.
  • Mechanism: a plausible route from x to y. More baristas serve more customers per minute, so queues clear faster; that is how queues work. A correlation with no conceivable mechanism, such as members and temperature, deserves extra suspicion.

What Ana can act on

Not every decision needs causation. To predict, a stable correlation is enough: warm days go with a high iced share (r = 0.94), so stocking ice and cups by the forecast works whether or not anyone understands why. To intervene, changing x to change y, the correlation must reflect cause. Rostering more baristas to shorten waits is an intervention, supported by the partial correlation, the gradient and the mechanism, and best confirmed by a trial. Pushing the loyalty app in summer “because warm weather drives sign-ups” rests on a trend, though daily sign-ups do follow warm, busy days modestly.

In depth

Confounding and reverse causation in more detail

A confounding variable is a common cause; the partial-correlation lesson held one fixed. Holding a confounder fixed works only if it is measured, measured well and controlled in the right shape, and only for the confounders you thought of. That is why observational studies, however careful, leave room for doubt, and why random assignment, which balances unmeasured confounders too, is the stronger design.

Reverse causation is common wherever decisions respond to expectations. Managers staff up for expected crowds, advertisers spend more in the season they expect to sell most, and doctors treat the sickest patients most intensively, so in each case the “treatment” correlates with the outcome it was chosen to meet. Timing helps (what was decided before what), and so does asking how x was set: if it was set from a forecast of y, the correlation of x with y is at least partly y’s doing.

Trends and other time patterns

Any two series that trend over the same period correlate, as the members show: −0.13 over the year, +0.87 and −0.90 within the halves. Three remedies are common. Correlate changes (today minus yesterday, this week minus last) rather than levels, as with daily sign-ups. Hold time fixed with a partial correlation, as long as the trend is close to a straight line: within spring, members and temperature correlate at 0.20 holding the day number fixed, and within autumn at −0.03. Or compare like periods (each Monday with the previous Monday, this June with last June). Seasonal patterns that are not straight lines, like a year of temperature, defeat a straight-line control for time: over the whole year, holding the day fixed leaves 0.61, which is still mostly season. Time-series methods that model trend and season properly belong to a forecasting course.

Selection

Selection bias arises when which rows are in the data depends on the variables being studied. Studying only successful cafés to find what makes cafés succeed is survivorship bias, one kind; studying only customers who answered a survey, who may be the most and least satisfied, is another. Selecting on a common effect of two variables is selecting on a collider, which creates a link between them. The remedy is to ask, before computing anything, how each row got into the data, and whether that process could depend on x, on y, or on both.

Experiments and their limits

A randomised experiment answers a causal question for the units, period and version of the change that were tried. Ana’s eight-week barista trial would tell her what an extra peak hour did to waits in those weeks, at those cafés; whether the effect holds in the December rush is a further question. Experiments also need enough units to detect the effect (the power ideas of B04 and of the testing-correlation lesson), a measure fixed in advance, and a plan to run the change as assigned. Where randomising is unfair or impractical, a comparison group and difference-in-differences (the change in the treated group minus the change in a comparison group) can stand in, at the cost of an assumption that the two would otherwise have moved in parallel.

Bradford Hill’s considerations

In a 1965 lecture on how to judge whether an environmental exposure causes a disease, the epidemiologist Austin Bradford Hill listed nine aspects of an association to weigh before deciding: strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, experiment and analogy. They are widely quoted as “criteria”, but Hill was explicit that they were not a checklist:

“None of my nine viewpoints can bring indisputable evidence for or against the cause-and-effect hypothesis and none can be required as a sine qua non.”

Hill also warned against letting a significance test settle the question, observing that such tests say how much chance could produce but not whether a link is causal. Statistical significance answers the first of the six explanations and none of the other five.

Writing a finding without overclaiming

The words in a finding should match the evidence. Before a causal claim is earned, describe the association: “goes with”, “is associated with”, “was higher on days when”. Give its size, its interval or p-value, the rows it covers and what was held fixed. Name the explanations you could not rule out. Then, if the evidence supports it, say how strongly it points to cause and what would settle it.

Matching the wording to the evidence
EvidenceWording that fitsWording that overclaims
A correlation in observational data“Days with more X tended to have more Y (r = 0.40).”“X drives Y.”
A partial correlation holding a confounder fixed“At a given level of Z, more X went with less Y.”“X reduces Y.”
A gradient, consistent across settings, with a mechanism“The evidence points to X reducing Y; a trial would confirm it.”“We have proved X reduces Y.”
A randomised experiment“Adding X reduced Y by … in the trial (95% interval …).”“X will always reduce Y.”

Common mistakes

  • “The correlation is significant, so the effect is real.”

    A significance test addresses chance, the first of the six explanations. It says nothing about confounding, reverse causation, trends, selection or pooling. A tiny p-value on 612 days can belong to an entirely spurious correlation, such as members and temperature in spring.

  • “We can’t prove cause without an experiment, so correlations are useless.”

    Correlations are enough for prediction (stock ice for hot days) and are how good causal questions are found. Natural experiments, gradients, timing, consistency and mechanism can make a causal reading strong even without a trial. The skill is matching the claim to the evidence, not refusing to claim anything.

  • “Both series went up this year, so one must be driving the other.”

    Any two trending series correlate. Correlate the changes, compare like periods, or hold time fixed; if the link vanishes, it was the trend. Members and temperature flip from +0.87 to −0.90 when the calendar moves.

  • “More barista hours go with higher sales, so baristas sell coffee.”

    The roster is set from the trade managers expect: sales drive the roster, not the other way round. Holding the day’s transactions fixed, barista hours and sales correlate at −0.13. Always ask how x was set.

  • “I’ll write that X drives Y, since everyone knows what I mean.”

    Readers act on words. “Drives”, “leads to” and “causes” tell a manager that changing X will change Y. If the evidence is a correlation, write “goes with” or “is associated with”, give the size, and say what would settle the causal question.

In practice

Every analyst who reports correlations to managers faces the same pressure: the manager wants to know what to change, and a correlation seems to say. Marketing teams see spend and sales rise together in December, operations teams see staffing and incidents rise together on busy shifts, product teams see that users of a feature are more loyal. In each case the professional habit is the same: name the alternative explanations, check the ones the data can check, say clearly which remain, and propose the test that would settle the question when the decision is large enough to justify one.

Fictional case

Ana’s year-end summary for her managers reads: “1. Warm days go with a higher share of iced drinks (r = 0.94); we will stock by the forecast. 2. At the same level of trade, mornings with more barista hours had shorter waits (r = −0.72); waits climb steeply above about seven transactions per barista hour. We are testing an extra peak hour on randomly chosen weekdays from February. 3. Members and temperature are two trends; daily sign-ups follow busy, warm days only modestly (r = 0.37), so we won’t time app promotions by the weather. 4. Rainy weekdays at Harbourside took about $90 less than dry ones; plan staffing for wet forecasts.”

Her summary uses the distinction this lesson rests on. The first and fourth items are predictions: she will act on them without needing to know the cause, and the fourth rests on a natural experiment, since nobody chose the rain. The second is a probable cause, stated as an association with the evidence that supports a causal reading, and she has planned the experiment that will measure it. The third is a correlation she has explained away. None of the items uses “drives” where the evidence is a correlation.

The same reasoning scales up. Large organisations run thousands of randomised experiments a year precisely because correlations in their data are so often confounded by who chooses what; regulators ask for trials before approving medicines for the same reason. When a trial is impossible, the considerations in this lesson are how analysts, economists and epidemiologists argue from observational data, and how readers should judge their arguments.

Summary

  • Causation means that changing x would change y; a correlation only shows that x and y moved together.
  • Six explanations other than cause produce correlations: chance (including many tests), confounding, reverse causation, common trends, selection and pooled groups. Check each before reading a correlation causally.
  • Trends: correlate changes, compare like periods or hold time fixed. Harbourside’s members and temperature flip from +0.87 to −0.90 with the season, while daily sign-ups and temperature correlate at about +0.37 in both halves.
  • Randomised experiments are the strongest evidence; natural experiments, dose–response gradients, timing, consistency and mechanism each rule out some alternatives. Hill’s nine considerations help weigh the evidence but are not a checklist.
  • Prediction needs only a stable correlation; intervention needs cause. Ana can stock by the weather forecast, should trial extra barista hours, and shouldn’t time promotions by temperature.
  • Write findings in the language of association, with size, interval, rows and controls, name what wasn’t ruled out, and say what would settle the causal question.

Terms introduced

Spurious correlation
A correlation between two variables that arises from something other than one affecting the other, such as a confounder, a shared trend, selection or chance.
Reverse causation
A correlation in which the supposed effect actually causes the supposed cause, such as expected sales determining how many staff are rostered.
Randomised experiment
A study in which the analyst assigns the change to units by chance, so that nothing else can systematically line up with it and a difference in outcomes can be attributed to the change.
Natural experiment
A situation in which something outside the control of the people being studied, such as the weather or a rule change, alters a possible cause for some units and not others, allowing a comparison close to an experiment.
Dose–response relationship
A pattern in which larger amounts of a possible cause go with steadily larger effects, considered evidence (though not proof) of a causal link.
Selection bias
Systematic error that arises when the rows in the data were chosen by a process related to the variables being studied, creating or distorting associations.
