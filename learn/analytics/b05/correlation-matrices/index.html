Correlation matrices

Put every pair of measures in one table, read it as a map of what goes with what, spot measures that say the same thing, and test 28 correlations at once without being fooled by the false alarms that many tests produce: Bonferroni, Holm and the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Construct a correlation matrix and its heatmap in Excel, with CORREL or the Analysis ToolPak’s Correlation tool, and read its structure: blocks, redundant measures and near-zero pairs.
  • Calculate the number of distinct pairs among m variables and explain why testing them all at α = 0.05 produces false alarms.
  • Apply the Bonferroni, Holm and Benjamini–Hochberg procedures to a set of p-values and explain what each controls: the family-wise error rate or the false discovery rate.
  • Recognise the limits of a matrix: pooled groups, nonlinear links, pairwise deletion and the impossibility of some combinations of correlations.
  • Use a matrix to choose follow-up questions rather than to report conclusions.

Review first

  • Testing a correlation — The t test of H₀: ρ = 0 that each cell of the matrix can carry, and why tiny correlations are significant with 612 pairs.
  • One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) — The family-wise error rate and the Bonferroni correction, which this lesson builds on.
  • Groups and Simpson’s paradox — Why a correlation over two pooled groups can differ from the correlation inside each.
  • Cells, references and formulas — Mixed references ($B$2:$B$31 against C$2:C$31) when filling CORREL across a grid.

The idea

Fictional case

By the end of December 2027 Ana has ten months of her cafés’ daily log: 306 days at Harbourside and 306 at Millbrook. She has looked at a few scatterplots one at a time, but she has eight daily measures that matter to her (the high temperature, rain, footfall, transactions, sales, the share of iced drinks, the barista hours rostered for the 7–11 a.m. peak and the average wait at the peak), and she wants to see everything that goes with everything before she decides what to investigate.

From pairs to a matrix

Eight measures make 8×7÷2=288 \times 7 \div 2 = 288×7÷2=28 distinct pairs. A scatterplot matrix of all 28 is possible but cramped, and the pattern across 28 small plots is hard to hold in your head. A correlation matrix puts all the pairs in one square table: one row and one column for each variable, and in each cell the correlation of the row’s variable with the column’s. The table below is the Pearson matrix for the year, both cafés together.

Columns are numbered as the rows: 1 High (°F), 2 Rain (in), 3 Footfall, 4 Transactions, 5 Sales, 6 Iced share, 7 Peak barista hours, 8 Peak wait. Every cell uses all 612 days.

Three features make a correlation matrix quick to read. The diagonal is all 1s, because every variable correlates perfectly with itself, so tables often leave it out. The matrix is symmetric, because the correlation of footfall with sales is the correlation of sales with footfall, so half of it is enough: here the lower triangle. And every cell is on the same scale, −1 to +1, whatever the variables’ units, so cells can be compared directly in a way that covariances can’t.

Reading the matrix

Read a matrix by looking for its structure before its single numbers. This one has three groups. The first is how busy the day was: footfall, transactions, sales, peak barista hours and peak wait correlate with each other at 0.53 to 0.98. The second is the weather’s effect on the menu: the high temperature and the iced share correlate at 0.94, the S-shaped curve of the nonlinear-and-restricted lesson. The third is rain, which correlates weakly and negatively with footfall, transactions, sales and peak wait (−0.12 to −0.15), and hardly at all with peak barista hours (0.03) or the high temperature (0.05).

Between the groups, the high temperature goes with the busy measures only modestly, at 0.21 to 0.29, and with the peak wait at only 0.08. A matrix like this is a summary of linear association; it says nothing about why. Some of the links the case was built with (footfall, transactions and sales form a chain; warm days bring more iced drinks), and some are side effects of how the cafés are run, as the next sections show.

Redundant measures

Footfall and transactions correlate at 0.98: on the year’s data, knowing one tells you almost everything about the other (r² = 0.96). Two such measures are redundant measures for most questions. Their correlations with everything else are nearly identical, so they are not two independent pieces of evidence that busy days have longer waits; they are one piece counted twice. When you choose which measures to carry into later analysis, keep the one closer to the question (transactions, if the question is about the till) and say why.

Peak barista hours correlate with footfall at 0.92 and with transactions at 0.93. That is not because baristas bring customers in: the case’s managers roster more baristas for the peak on days they expect to be busy, so the roster follows the expected crowd. In regression (B06) measures this closely related cause a problem called multicollinearity: their separate effects can’t be estimated well. Here it is enough to notice that the matrix’s strongest cells often reflect how a business is run rather than how one measure affects another.

A heatmap

A heatmap colours each cell by its value, usually with one colour for positive correlations, another for negative ones, and pale cells near 0. It makes blocks visible at a glance: the busy block shows as a dark square in the middle of the Explore widget’s map. Good heatmaps print the numbers too, because colour alone is hard to read precisely and impossible for some readers, and they put related variables next to each other so that blocks are not broken up by an arbitrary order.

Key idea: Choose a cell, look at its scatterplot

Every cell is a single number summarising a scatterplot. Before you report a cell, look at its plot: an r of 0.08 (high temperature and peak wait) can hide nothing at all or a curve that a straight line misses, and the scatterplots lesson’s warning about 612 overlapping points applies to every cell. The Explore widget draws the scatterplot of any cell you choose.

One table, many tests

Each cell can carry the testing-correlation lesson’s test of H₀: ρ = 0. Twenty-eight cells mean 28 tests. B04’s ANOVA lesson showed the problem: if every null hypothesis were true and the tests were independent, the chance of at least one false alarm among 28 tests at α = 0.05 would be 1−0.9528≈0.761 - 0.95^{28} \approx 0.761−0.9528≈0.76, and on average 1.4 cells would be “significant” by chance alone. Tests in a matrix share variables, so they are not independent and the exact figure differs, but the warning stands: a matrix scanned for stars will find some.

With the whole year, the many-tests problem is not the main danger. With 612 days, any correlation larger than 0.08 in size is significant at 5%, so 24 of the 28 cells pass, and 22 to 24 still pass after the adjustments in the In depth section. Here the question is not whether a correlation is zero but whether it is large enough to matter, the practical significance of the testing-correlation lesson. The adjustments matter when samples are small: in Harbourside’s 30 June days, 18 cells are significant at 5% unadjusted, but only 10 survive the Bonferroni correction, 13 Holm’s method and 16 the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. The challenging example works through them.

In depth

What a correlation matrix is

For m variables, the correlation matrix is the m × m table whose cell in row i and column j is rijr_{ij}rij​, the correlation of variable i with variable j. It has 1s on the diagonal, rij=rjir_{ij} = r_{ji}rij​=rji​, and m(m−1)/2m(m-1)/2m(m−1)/2 distinct off-diagonal cells. A Spearman matrix holds Spearman’s ρ in each cell instead; the rank-correlation lesson explains when to prefer it. For the log the two tell the same story: high temperature and iced share are 0.94 by Pearson and 0.96 by Spearman, because the S-shaped curve is monotonic but not straight.

Not every symmetric table of numbers between −1 and 1 can be a correlation matrix. If x and y correlate at 0.9 and y and z at 0.9, then x and z must correlate at least 0.9×0.9−(1−0.92)(1−0.92)=0.620.9 \times 0.9 - \sqrt{(1 - 0.9^2)(1 - 0.9^2)} = 0.620.9×0.9−(1−0.92)(1−0.92)​=0.62: variables that both follow y closely must follow each other. The general bound for any three variables is in the Formulas section. A valid matrix is what mathematicians call positive semidefinite; you rarely need the term, but you will meet its failure when a matrix is assembled from different sources or different rows.

Pairwise deletion and the n behind each cell

When some values are missing, the engine used in this course works out each cell from the rows where both of its variables are present, which is pairwise deletion. Each cell can then rest on a different set of rows and a different n, so cells are less comparable, and in bad cases the table as a whole can break the bound above. Report the n of each cell (the Explore widget shows it when you choose one), or use only rows complete on every variable, listwise deletion, if few are lost. Ana’s log has no missing values, so every cell of her matrix uses all 612 days.

Pooled groups and curves

A matrix pools whatever rows you give it, and a matrix of pooled data can mislead. Footfall and sales correlate at 0.89 over both cafés but at 0.98 inside Harbourside and 0.97 inside Millbrook: Millbrook has more customers with smaller tickets, so pooling the cafés blurs a nearly straight line into two. Peak barista hours and peak wait correlate at 0.53 pooled and 0.42 inside each café. The groups-and-simpsons-paradox lesson shows a pair whose sign flips. Before trusting a pooled matrix, compute it within each group too.

A matrix also holds only linear association (or monotonic association, for Spearman). Peak waits rise steeply once the load passes about seven transactions per barista hour, and rain is zero on 209 of the 306 days; a single number in a cell can’t show either shape. A near-zero cell means “no straight-line link”, not “no link”.

Building one in Excel

The Analysis ToolPak’s Correlation tool (Data > Data Analysis > Correlation) takes a block of columns with labels and writes the lower triangle of Pearson correlations, with 1s on the diagonal. Microsoft notes that it returns CORREL’s value for each pair. Its output is values, not formulas, so rerun it when the data change, and it gives no p-values. The Covariance tool beside it fills the same layout with population covariances, COVARIANCE.P.

With formulas, each cell is a CORREL. With the data in B2:E31 and the variable names along the top of a grid, the cell for row variable B and column variable C is =CORREL($B$2:$B$31,C$2:C$31): the dollar signs fix the row variable’s range while the column variable’s range moves as you fill right. One more column of tests is =T.DIST.2T(ABS(r)*SQRT(n-2)/SQRT(1-r^2),n-2) with r and n replaced by their cells, the p-value of the testing-correlation lesson’s t test. Excel has no function for the adjustments below, but each is one or two columns of formulas.

Controlling the family-wise error rate: Bonferroni and Holm

The family-wise error rate is the chance of at least one false alarm among the m tests. The Bonferroni correction keeps it at or below α by testing each hypothesis at α/m\alpha / mα/m: for 28 pairs, 0.05÷28=0.001790.05 \div 28 = 0.001790.05÷28=0.00179. Equivalently, multiply each p-value by m (capped at 1) and compare the adjusted p-value with α. It needs no assumption about how the tests depend on each other, which suits a matrix, whose tests share variables. Its cost is power: with many tests, real but moderate correlations fail the strict threshold.

Holm’s method (Holm, 1979) keeps the same guarantee and never rejects fewer hypotheses. Sort the p-values from smallest to largest, p(1)≤p(2)≤⋯≤p(m)p_{(1)} \le p_{(2)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}p(1)​≤p(2)​≤⋯≤p(m)​. Compare the smallest with α/m\alpha/mα/m, the next with α/(m−1)\alpha/(m-1)α/(m−1), and so on, the k-th with α/(m−k+1)\alpha/(m-k+1)α/(m−k+1). Reject while each p-value is below its threshold and stop at the first that isn’t: it and every larger p-value stand. The first comparison is Bonferroni’s; after that each threshold is a little less strict, because once a hypothesis is rejected there is one fewer that could be a false alarm. Holm proved that the guarantee holds whatever the dependence between the tests.

Controlling the false discovery rate: Benjamini–Hochberg

When a matrix is used to screen for leads rather than to confirm findings, guarding against even one false alarm is often too strict. Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) proposed controlling a different quantity, the false discovery rate: the expected proportion of false alarms among the hypotheses rejected (0 if none is rejected). At q = 0.05, about one in twenty of the pairs you report as discoveries may be false, on average.

The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure sorts the p-values in the same way and compares the k-th smallest with k q/mk\,q/mkq/m, a threshold that rises in a straight line. Find the largest k whose p-value is at or below its threshold, and reject that hypothesis and every one with a smaller p-value, even if some of those were above their own thresholds. It works from the largest p-value down, so it is called a step-up procedure; Holm’s works from the smallest up and is a step-down. Benjamini and Hochberg proved the control for independent tests. The statsmodels documentation, whose procedures this course’s engine follows, notes that most of these procedures are robust when tests are positively correlated, which is the usual situation in a matrix; with strong negative dependence the guarantee can fail.

Which to use

  • Confirming a few planned relationships. When a report will act on each finding, control the family-wise error rate with Holm’s method; it is never worse than Bonferroni.
  • Screening many pairs for leads. When a matrix is a first look and every lead will be checked again, control the false discovery rate with Benjamini–Hochberg, and say that a share of the leads may be false.
  • Defining the family. The adjustment depends on m, so decide before looking which tests form the family: the 28 cells of one matrix, not only the cells that looked interesting. Running a Pearson matrix, then a Spearman matrix, then one for each café and each month multiplies the tests again; an adjustment within each matrix doesn’t correct for that p-hacking.
  • Large samples. With hundreds of rows almost every cell is significant with or without adjustment, so judge the size of each correlation and its interval instead.

Common mistakes

  • “Twenty-four cells are significant, so I have 24 findings.”

    With 612 days, any correlation larger than 0.08 in size is significant. Significance only says the correlation is unlikely to be exactly zero; whether 0.08 matters to anyone is a separate question. Report the size of each correlation and its interval, and pick out the few that would change a decision.

  • “I’ll choose the adjustment after I see which one keeps my favourite pair.”

    Choosing Bonferroni, Holm or Benjamini–Hochberg after seeing the results is another way of trying analyses until one works. Decide before looking: Holm when every rejection will be acted on as a finding, Benjamini–Hochberg when the matrix is a screen for leads. Define the family (which tests) in advance too.

  • “Footfall and transactions both correlate with waiting, so that’s two pieces of evidence.”

    Two measures that correlate at 0.98 carry nearly the same information. Their correlations with a third variable will be nearly identical, so counting both double-counts one piece of evidence. Choose one, or combine them, before you count links.

  • “The cell is near zero, so the two are unrelated.”

    A cell holds linear (or, for Spearman, monotonic) association over the rows you gave it. A curve, a U-shape, a variable that is zero on most days, or two groups pulling in opposite directions can all give a cell near zero. Look at the scatterplot and compute the matrix within each group.

  • “A matrix of pairwise correlations is always valid.”

    With pairwise deletion each cell may use different rows, and the table as a whole can then be impossible: for instance, 0.9 and 0.9 with a third cell of 0.2, below the 0.62 that the bound requires. Check the n behind each cell and, if many values are missing, consider listwise deletion or report the missing data.

In practice

A correlation matrix is one of the first things an analyst builds when a dataset arrives with many measures: a retailer’s weekly store KPIs, a hotel’s nightly operating figures, a fund’s daily returns on many assets, or the dozen questions of a customer survey. It answers three practical questions quickly. Which measures are near-duplicates, so that a dashboard or model needs only one of them? Which pairs are strong enough to deserve a closer look? And which expected links are missing, which may point to a data problem (a broken feed, a column in the wrong units) rather than a business insight?

Fictional case

Ana’s January note reads: “The daily log’s correlation matrix (eight measures, both cafés, 612 days) has three blocks: how busy the day was (footfall, transactions, sales, peak barista hours and peak wait), the weather and the iced share, and rain. Footfall and transactions are near-duplicates (r = 0.98), so I will use transactions. With this many days almost every pair is statistically significant, so I have ranked pairs by size instead. Three questions follow: whether baristas shorten waits at a given level of trade, why Harbourside’s sales follow the weather (r = 0.40) more than Millbrook’s (0.15), and what a wet day costs each café. None of these correlations shows what causes what.”

Her note follows the habits that make a matrix useful rather than misleading. It states the rows used (both cafés, all days) and the n. It treats the matrix as a map that suggests questions, not as a list of results. It removes duplicates before counting links. It checks pooled results within each café. And it avoids causal words: the cells say what goes with what, and the last lesson of this module is about what more it would take to say what causes what.

When a matrix is used to confirm findings, the adjustment for many tests belongs in the report: say how many pairs were tested, which procedure was used and why, and give both the adjusted and unadjusted p-values for the pairs you discuss. When it is used for screening, say so, and treat every lead as a hypothesis to test again on new data, such as the next quarter’s log. A lead that holds up on data it wasn’t found in is worth far more than one that survives an adjustment on the data that suggested it.

Summary

  • A correlation matrix shows every pair of m variables in one symmetric table with 1s on the diagonal; it has m(m − 1)/2 distinct pairs (28 for 8 variables). A heatmap colours the cells, and should print the numbers too.
  • Read the structure first: blocks of related measures, redundant measures (footfall and transactions, r = 0.98, carry one piece of evidence, not two) and near-zero pairs. Then look at the scatterplot behind any cell you report.
  • A matrix pools the rows you give it and holds only linear (or monotonic) association: compute it within groups, and remember that a near-zero cell can hide a curve.
  • Some combinations of correlations are impossible: if r_xy = r_yz = 0.9, r_xz must be at least 0.62. Pairwise deletion can produce such impossible matrices.
  • Testing every cell makes false alarms likely. Bonferroni (α ÷ m) and Holm’s step-down method control the family-wise error rate whatever the dependence; Holm is never less powerful. Benjamini–Hochberg controls the false discovery rate and finds more leads.
  • With hundreds of rows almost every cell is significant, so judge practical size; with a few dozen rows the adjustment decides what you can claim (June at Harbourside: 18 unadjusted, 10 Bonferroni, 13 Holm, 16 Benjamini–Hochberg).
  • Use a matrix as a map for questions, decide the family and the adjustment before looking, and check leads on new data. A matrix says what goes with what, not why.

Terms introduced

Correlation matrix
A square, symmetric table of the correlations between every pair of a set of variables, with 1s on the diagonal; m variables give m(m − 1)/2 distinct pairs.
Heatmap
A table or matrix drawn as a grid of coloured cells, the colour showing each cell’s value; for a correlation matrix, one colour for positive and another for negative correlations, paler near zero.
Redundant measures
Two or more variables so closely correlated that they carry nearly the same information, so that using both adds little and can double-count evidence.
Holm’s method
A step-down procedure that controls the family-wise error rate: sort the m p-values, compare the k-th smallest with α ÷ (m − k + 1), and reject until the first that is not below its threshold. Never less powerful than Bonferroni.
False discovery rate
The expected proportion of false alarms among the hypotheses rejected (taken as 0 when none is rejected).
Benjamini–Hochberg procedure
A step-up procedure that controls the false discovery rate at q: sort the m p-values and reject every hypothesis up to the largest rank k whose p-value is at or below k × q ÷ m.
