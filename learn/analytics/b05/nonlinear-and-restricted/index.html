Curves, thresholds and restricted ranges

Pearson’s r measures how close points come to a straight line. Learn what it says, and fails to say, about curves, thresholds and U-shapes, why looking at only part of the range shrinks it, and why noisy measurements do too.

About 85 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Recognise from a scatterplot when a relationship is curved, has a threshold or is U-shaped, and explain what Pearson’s r then does and does not describe.
  • Calculate correlations within ranges of x and with a transformed x (a log or a hinge at a threshold) to show how the strength of a link changes along its range.
  • Explain why restricting the range of x, for example to summer days or to one café’s weekdays, usually shrinks r, and estimate the effect with the range-restriction formula.
  • Estimate how unreliable measurements attenuate a correlation, and say what the correction for attenuation assumes.
  • Choose between Pearson’s r, Spearman’s ρ and a plot with a written description for a curved relationship.

Review first

  • Scatterplots — Reading the form of a relationship (linear, curved, clustered) from a scatterplot.
  • Rank correlation — Spearman’s ρ and the difference between a monotonic and a linear relationship.
  • Outliers and influence — How a few points can move r; this lesson looks at what the shape of the whole cloud does.
  • Skewed data and transformations — Log transformations and why they need positive values.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana plans the iced-drink stock for next summer from the café log. Across the 612 café-days, the day’s high temperature and the share of drinks served iced have r = 0.940, about as strong as correlations in business data get. Her summer manager, looking only at June to August, finds r = 0.841, and only 0.657 for July. “Temperature hardly matters in high summer,” he says. Who is right?

r measures closeness to a line

Pearson’s r answers one precise question: how closely do the points cluster around a straight line? It does not measure how strongly y depends on x in general. When the relationship is a straight line with even scatter, the two ideas coincide. When it is curved, has a threshold, or rises and then falls, r can mislead in either direction: it can be high while hiding the shape, or close to zero while the link is strong.

The iced-drink share is a curve. On cold days it barely moves: 7.2% of drinks are iced on days below 50°F and 10.4% between 50 and 55°F. Between about 60 and 75°F it climbs fast, by 13 to 16 percentage points for every 5°F. Above 80°F it levels off again near 80%, because some customers want hot coffee whatever the weather. The shape is an S. Because the curve rises all the way, it is a monotonic relationship, and a straight line still fits it fairly well: r = 0.940, and Spearman’s ρ, which needs only a consistent direction, is a little higher at 0.957.

The number hides what matters for stock planning: the same 5°F rise changes the iced share by about 3 percentage points on the coldest days and by 13 to 16 on mild ones. A single r can’t say that. A scatterplot, or a table of the iced share by temperature band like the one in the In depth section, can.

Thresholds: when r understates the link

The morning peak tells a sharper story. Ana divides the number of peak transactions by the barista hours rostered for the peak, giving the peak load: the transactions each barista hour has to serve. Load and the average peak wait have r = 0.830. But the plot is not a cloud around a line. It is a threshold effect: flat at about 1.6 to 1.8 minutes for loads up to 7, then rising ever more steeply, to an average of 5.8 minutes when the load passes 10.

Here r understates the link. On the 169 café-days with a load of 7 or less, load and wait are unrelated (r = −0.10); on the 443 days above 7, r = 0.93. Measured against the shape the data actually follow, the squared distance above 7, the correlation is 0.980. The relationship is close to a law: keep the load under about seven transactions per barista hour and waits stay short. An r of 0.83, read as “strong but loose”, would miss that.

U-shapes: when r says nothing

A relationship that rises and then falls, or falls and then rises, can have r close to zero however strong it is. The hotel in the figure gets complaints when rooms are too cold and when they are too warm. Across its eleven settings r = 0.00, yet complaints are almost perfectly predictable from the distance between the setting and 19.5°C. A correlation of zero means no straight-line relationship, not no relationship.

Key idea: Describe the shape, then choose the number

Before reporting r, look at the scatterplot and say what shape the points make. If it is a line, r summarises it. If it is a monotonic curve, report Spearman’s ρ beside r and describe the curve. If it has a threshold or a turning point, describe it in words and numbers (where it turns, how steep it is on each side), because no single coefficient will.

Looking at part of the range

Back to the summer manager. Restricting the data to June to August cuts out the cold days, so temperatures run only from 67 to 94°F, with a standard deviation of 6.4°F instead of 15.0°F. With less variation in x, the same scatter around the curve is a larger share of what is left, and r shrinks: from 0.940 for the year to 0.841 for summer and 0.657 for July. July also sits on the flat top of the S, where warmer days add little. Temperature still matters in July, just less than in May, and over a narrower range. Both of them are describing the data correctly; they are describing different data.

Correlations within ranges confirm what the bands show: r = −0.10 for loads of 7 or less and 0.93 above 7. A hinge transformation makes the shape explicit: h=max⁡(0,x−c)h = \max(0, x - c)h=max(0,x−c) is zero below the threshold c and rises one for one above it. With c = 7, the hinge has r = 0.917 with the wait; squaring it, to allow for the wait rising ever faster, gives 0.980. That 0.98 is closer to the truth about this link than 0.83.

Two cautions. First, the threshold of 7 came from looking at these data, so a correlation with a hinge chosen to fit them flatters the fit; it describes this year’s log well and should be checked on new data. Second, Spearman’s ρ is 0.867 here, a little above r, because ρ rewards any steady rise, but it is no better at describing a flat stretch followed by a steep one. For a threshold, report where it is and how the slope changes, not only a coefficient.

Note: The case states the cause here

In this fictional log, waits are built to depend on the load, so describing the threshold as the load lengthening waits is a planted fact, not something the correlation shows. In real data, a threshold in a scatterplot is a description; the correlation-and-causation lesson discusses what it would take to show the load causes the waits.

U-shapes and turning points

For a symmetrical U, the positive products of z-scores on one side cancel the negative ones on the other, so r comes out near zero. In the hotel’s data, r is −0.97 on the cold side (settings up to 19.5°C) and +0.96 on the warm side; together, 0.00. Spearman’s ρ, which needs a consistent direction, fails too (−0.03). The fix is to measure against the shape: complaints have r = 0.97 with the distance from 19.5°C and 0.999 with its square. As with the threshold, the turning point was read from the data, so state it as a finding to confirm.

Transformations in outline

Some curves straighten under a transformation. A log transformation turns equal ratios into equal differences, which suits relationships in which y grows by a percentage when x grows by a percentage. In twelve fictional towns, the number of cafés rises with population but more and more slowly: r = 0.950 on the raw values, 0.994 on the logs of both. Logs need positive values, so they can’t be used directly on Lantern’s rainfall, which is zero on most days.

Spearman’s ρ is 0.993 for the towns, on raw values or logs alike, because a log keeps every value in the same order. That is a useful property: a rank correlation doesn’t depend on which monotonic transformation you choose, while Pearson’s r does. Fitting a curve after a transformation, and interpreting its slope, belongs to B06.

Restricted range

Range restriction means the data cover only part of the values the variable takes. If the relationship is a straight line with even scatter, and the restriction is on x alone, then shrinking the standard deviation of x from S to s changes r in a predictable way. For the summer days, k = 15.0 ÷ 6.4 ≈ 2.3 and the formula turns the summer r of 0.841 into 0.964, against 0.940 actually observed. It overshoots because the relationship is an S, not a line, and the scatter is not even. The formula is a guide to how much restriction matters, not a way to recover the “true” r.

Range restriction is everywhere in business data. Customers who were approved for credit, staff who were hired, products that stayed on the menu, stores that survived: each is a selected group with a narrower range than the population it came from, and correlations inside it are usually weaker than across everyone. The weaker correlation is accurate for the group; it is wrong only if read as a statement about the whole population.

Restriction does not always weaken r. If you select the part of a curve where it is steepest, r can rise: for loads above 7, r = 0.93, more than 0.83 for all days. And restricting to the steep part of a curve shrinks r far less than restricting to a flat part: days between 60 and 74°F still give 0.906 for the iced share, against 0.657 for July. The general rule is that r depends on the range you look at, so always say what range it describes.

Unreliable measurements attenuate r

Every measurement has some error. The door counter misses people in a crowd; a manager timing the queue with a stopwatch on five customers gets a noisy average. Random error adds scatter that has nothing to do with the relationship, so the observed correlation is weaker than the correlation between the true values. Spearman (1904) called this attenuation and gave a correction: if the reliability of each measure is known (the share of its variance that is true variation, often estimated by measuring the same thing twice and correlating the two sets of results), then robs≈rtruerelx relyr_{\text{obs}} \approx r_{\text{true}}\sqrt{\text{rel}_x \, \text{rel}_y}robs​≈rtrue​relx​rely​​.

For example, with fictional figures: if the true correlation is 0.80, the load is measured with reliability 0.90 and the stopwatch waits with reliability 0.60, the observed r would be about 0.80 × √(0.90 × 0.60) = 0.59. Dividing an observed r by √(rel_x × rel_y) estimates the true one, but only as well as the reliabilities are known, and it can produce values above 1 when they are underestimated or when sampling error in r is large. Use the correction to judge whether a weak correlation might come from noisy measurement; better still, measure more reliably.

Common mistakes

  • “r = 0, so the two variables are unrelated.”

    r = 0 means no straight-line relationship. A U-shape (complaints against the thermostat setting) or a pattern that rises then falls can have r near 0 and a near-perfect link. Plot the data before concluding anything.

  • “r = 0.94, so the relationship is a straight line.”

    A monotonic curve can give a high r: the iced share is an S, flat at both ends, with r = 0.94. A high r says the points are near some line, not that a line describes them well. Look at the plot or at averages by band.

  • “r = 0.83 for load and waits: a strong but loose link.”

    When the relationship has a threshold, r averages a flat stretch and a steep one. Here waits are unrelated to load below 7 and closely related above it (r = 0.93). Describe the threshold; a single r understates the link.

  • “Temperature matters less in summer, because r is lower in summer.”

    Summer days cover a narrower range of temperatures (SD 6.4°F against 15.0°F), and a narrower range shrinks r even when the relationship is unchanged. Compare slopes or band averages, not correlations from different ranges.

  • “A weak correlation means a weak relationship.”

    Measurement error attenuates r: with reliabilities of 0.9 and 0.6, a true correlation of 0.80 shows up as about 0.59. Before dismissing a link, ask how reliably each variable was measured.

In practice

Fictional case

Ana’s note for the summer stock plan: “Iced share rises with temperature in an S: about 3 points per 5°F on the coldest days, 13 to 16 points per 5°F between 60 and 75°F, and levelling off near 80% above 80°F (2027 log, both cafés). In July the share averages about 75%, so July orders should follow footfall more than temperature. The lower correlation in summer (0.84, against 0.94 for the year) reflects the narrower range of summer temperatures, not a weaker link.”

Her note follows the habits of analysts who deal well with curves. She describes the shape in the units of the decision (percentage points per 5°F) instead of reporting a single coefficient, says over what range each statement holds, and explains a lower correlation before anyone can misread it. The same habits apply wherever relationships bend: price and demand near a competitor’s price, workload and errors near capacity, discounts and orders near a threshold for free delivery.

Thresholds deserve special attention in operations because they are where decisions bite. A service desk, a call centre or a café counter works well until utilisation passes a point, beyond which queues tend to lengthen quickly. A correlation across all days blends the calm days and the overloaded ones; a table of averages by band, or correlations within ranges, shows where the threshold is, and that is what a manager needs in order to plan capacity.

When comparing correlations across teams, sites or periods, check the ranges first. A branch with very similar days will show weaker correlations than one with very varied days, even if the two behave the same way. Report the range each correlation covers, and prefer comparisons of band averages or slopes, which B06 develops.

Summary

  • Pearson’s r measures how closely points cluster around a straight line, not how strongly y depends on x in general.
  • A monotonic curve can give a high r (the iced-share S: r = 0.940, ρ = 0.957) while hiding where the relationship is steep and where it is flat; averages by band show the shape.
  • A threshold makes r understate a link: peak waits are unrelated to load up to about 7 (r = −0.10) and closely related above it (r = 0.93); a squared hinge at 7 gives r = 0.980 against 0.830.
  • A U-shape can give r ≈ 0 with a near-perfect link; measure against the shape (distance from the turning point) and say the turning point was read from the data.
  • Logs straighten multiplicative curves (r = 0.950 to 0.994 for the fictional towns); Spearman’s ρ doesn’t change under any monotonic transformation.
  • Restricting the range of x usually shrinks r (summer 0.841, July 0.657, against 0.940 for the year; Millbrook’s weekdays 0.52 against 0.88); always say what range a correlation describes.
  • Unreliable measurements attenuate r: r_obs ≈ r_true × √(rel_x × rel_y). The correction for attenuation is only as good as the reliabilities.

Terms introduced

Threshold effect
A relationship in which y barely changes with x up to a certain value of x and changes markedly beyond it.
Hinge transformation
Replacing x with max(0, x − c), which is zero up to a threshold c and rises one for one above it, so that a correlation or a fitted line can follow a relationship that starts at the threshold.
Range restriction
Analysing only part of the range a variable can take, such as summer days or hired applicants. It usually shrinks a correlation, though it can raise one when the part selected is where a curve is steepest.
Attenuation
The weakening of an observed correlation by random measurement error in either variable; Spearman’s correction divides the observed r by the square root of the product of the two reliabilities.
