Testing a correlation

Decide whether a sample correlation is evidence of a real association, put a confidence interval round it with Fisher’s z, and plan how many pairs a study needs. Test rain against footfall at both cafés, see why r = 0.14 is significant with 612 pairs while r = 0.64 is not with 8, and compare the two cafés’ correlations without confusing significance with size.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • State the null and alternative hypotheses for a correlation, and calculate the t statistic r√(n − 2) ÷ √(1 − r²) and its p-value, one- or two-sided, by hand and in Excel.
  • Calculate the critical value of r for a given n and α, and explain why the same r can be significant with many pairs and not with few.
  • Construct a confidence interval for a population correlation with Fisher’s z transformation, using FISHER and FISHERINV.
  • Calculate the power of a correlation test and the number of pairs needed to detect a given correlation.
  • Compare correlations from two independent samples, and judge whether a significant correlation is large enough to matter.

Review first

  • Hypothesis testing — Null and alternative hypotheses, p-values, one- and two-sided tests, α, power and Type I and II errors are used here without re-teaching.
  • Confidence intervals — An interval as estimate ± critical value × standard error, and what 95% confidence means.
  • Rank correlation — Spearman’s ρ and Kendall’s τ-b, whose tests are mentioned here.
  • Covariance and Pearson’s r — Pearson’s r and r², the statistic this lesson tests.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana suspects rain keeps customers away. At Harbourside, the waterfront café, the daily log for 1 March to 31 December 2027 gives a correlation of −0.23 between the day’s rain and the number of people through the door. At Millbrook, in the office district, the same 306 days give −0.10. Both are negative, both are small. Is either more than the scatter that 306 days of chance could produce if rain made no difference at all?

The question a test answers

Ana’s log is a sample: 306 days of one year, from all the days the cafés might trade under similar conditions. The correlation in that population is written ρ\rhoρ (rho); the sample’s rrr estimates it. Even if ρ\rhoρ were exactly 0, a sample of days would almost never give r = 0 exactly: random variation pushes it a little above or below. The test asks whether Ana’s r is further from 0 than that variation would plausibly take it.

In the language of the hypothesis-testing lesson (B04):

H0:ρ=0H1:ρ≠0H_0: \rho = 0 \qquad H_1: \rho \ne 0H0​:ρ=0H1​:ρ=0

The null hypothesis says there is no linear association in the population; the two-sided alternative says there is one, in either direction. This is the test OpenStax’s statistics texts give for the “significance of the correlation coefficient”, and the one software reports beside r.

The t statistic

If H0H_0H0​ is true and the conditions below hold, the statistic

t=rn−21−r2t = \dfrac{r\sqrt{n - 2}}{\sqrt{1 - r^2}}t=1−r2​rn−2​​

follows the t distribution with n−2n - 2n−2 degrees of freedom. Two degrees of freedom are used up because a correlation is measured round two estimated means. For Harbourside, t=−0.2276×304÷1−0.22762=−4.08t = -0.2276 \times \sqrt{304} \div \sqrt{1 - 0.2276^2} = -4.08t=−0.2276×304​÷1−0.22762​=−4.08 with 304 degrees of freedom, and the two-sided p-value, =T.DIST.2T(ABS(-4.075),304), is below 0.001. If rain made no difference to Harbourside’s footfall, a correlation as far from 0 as −0.23 would turn up in fewer than 1 sample of 306 days in 1,000. Ana rejects H0H_0H0​: rainier days go with fewer people at Harbourside.

At Millbrook, r = −0.102 gives t = −1.79 and a two-sided p-value of 0.074. At α = 0.05 Ana fails to reject H0H_0H0​: the log is compatible with no linear association between rain and footfall at Millbrook, though, as B04 stressed, that is not evidence that there is none.

Why n matters so much

The formula rewards sample size. Turned round, it gives the smallest |r| that is significant for a given n, the critical value of r. At α = 0.05, two-sided, it is 0.707 for 8 pairs, 0.361 for 30, 0.197 for 100 and 0.079 for 612.

Two consequences follow. With 612 café-days, rain’s pooled correlation with footfall, r = −0.14, is significant (p = 0.001) although r² = 0.019: rain accounts for under 2% of the day-to-day variation in footfall. And eight days in April, 5–12 April at Harbourside, give r = 0.64 between the day’s high and footfall, which looks strong, but p = 0.088: with 8 pairs a correlation that size is well within what chance produces. A p-value answers “could this be chance?”, not “is this large?”.

Key idea: What the test does and doesn’t say

Rejecting H₀: ρ = 0 says the sample correlation is unlikely to have arisen by chance if the population correlation were 0. It doesn’t say the association is strong, that it is linear, that one variable causes the other, or that it holds outside the period sampled. Report r with its confidence interval, not the p-value alone.

In depth

Conditions

The t test of r rests on assumptions. OpenStax lists them in regression terms: a linear relationship; for each x, y values roughly normal around the line with the same spread; independent errors; and data from a random sample or a randomised experiment. In practice: plot the data first (a curve or a slip can make r, and its test, misleading), and ask whether the pairs are independent.

Independence is the condition most often broken by business data. Ana’s days come in sequence, and neighbouring days are alike: a wet spell lasts several days, and footfall drifts with the season. Days that resemble their neighbours carry less independent information than their number suggests, so the test treats 306 days as more evidence than they are and its p-values come out too small. Pooling the cafés adds a second problem: each day’s rain appears twice, once for each café, so the 612 rows hold only 306 different weather days. Treat a p-value from a time series as a rough guide, and be most cautious when it is near α.

One-sided and two-sided tests

If the question, fixed before looking at the data, is whether rain goes with fewer customers, the alternative is one-sided, H1:ρ<0H_1: \rho < 0H1​:ρ<0, and the p-value is the lower tail, =T.DIST(t,n-2,TRUE). For Millbrook that is 0.037, half the two-sided 0.074, and below 0.05. This is exactly the temptation the hypothesis-testing lesson warned about: switching to one-sided after seeing the two-sided p-value doubles the real false-alarm rate. Ana’s question when she started the log was “does rain make any difference?”, so she keeps the two-sided test.

A confidence interval for ρ

A confidence interval says how large the population correlation plausibly is, which a p-value doesn’t. The sampling distribution of r is skewed when ρ\rhoρ is far from 0 (r can’t go above 1), so the usual estimate ± margin doesn’t work. Fisher’s z transformation fixes this: z=atanh⁡(r)=12ln⁡1+r1−rz = \operatorname{atanh}(r) = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1+r}{1-r}z=atanh(r)=21​ln1−r1+r​ is close to normal with standard error 1/n−31/\sqrt{n-3}1/n−3​, whatever ρ\rhoρ is. Build the interval for z, then transform its ends back with tanh⁡\tanhtanh.

For Harbourside’s rain: z=atanh⁡(−0.2276)=−0.2316z = \operatorname{atanh}(-0.2276) = -0.2316z=atanh(−0.2276)=−0.2316, SE=1/303=0.0574SE = 1/\sqrt{303} = 0.0574SE=1/303​=0.0574, so the 95% interval for z is −0.2316±1.96×0.0574-0.2316 \pm 1.96 \times 0.0574−0.2316±1.96×0.0574, from −0.3442 to −0.1190. Back-transformed, the interval for ρ\rhoρ runs from −0.331 to −0.118. In Excel: =FISHERINV(FISHER(B1)-NORM.S.INV(0.975)/SQRT(B2-3)) and =FISHERINV(FISHER(B1)+NORM.S.INV(0.975)/SQRT(B2-3)), with r in B1 and n in B2.

The interval is not symmetric about r: it stretches further on the side away from ±1. It also agrees with the test: it excludes 0, as a two-sided test at 5% that rejects ρ = 0 requires (approximately, since the test uses t and the interval Fisher’s z). Millbrook’s interval, −0.212 to 0.010, includes 0. For the eight April days it runs from −0.120 to 0.926: anything from a weak negative association to a near-perfect positive one. That width is the honest summary of what eight days can say.

Testing a value other than 0

Sometimes the question is not whether ρ\rhoρ is 0 but whether it exceeds some value that matters, such as 0.3. The t test above works only for ρ0=0\rho_0 = 0ρ0​=0; Fisher’s z handles any value: z=(atanh⁡r−atanh⁡ρ0)n−3z = (\operatorname{atanh} r - \operatorname{atanh}\rho_0)\sqrt{n-3}z=(atanhr−atanhρ0​)n−3​, compared with the standard normal. For Harbourside’s day’s high and footfall, r = 0.436 over 306 days; against ρ0=0.3\rho_0 = 0.3ρ0​=0.3, z = 2.75 and the two-sided p-value is 0.006.

Tests of rank correlations

Spearman’s ρ is usually tested with the same t statistic, applied to the ranks, and Kendall’s τ with a normal approximation to its distribution. Both are approximations, good for moderate samples; for small samples without ties, exact tables or permutation tests are better. For rain and footfall at Harbourside, Spearman’s ρ = −0.226 and τ-b = −0.173, both with p below 0.001: the same conclusion as r, without relying on normality.

Power and sample size

Power, from the hypothesis-testing lesson, is the chance of rejecting H0H_0H0​ when a particular alternative is true. Fisher’s z gives a simple approximation: with true correlation ρ\rhoρ and n pairs, the test of ρ=0\rho = 0ρ=0 rejects (two-sided, α = 0.05) with probability about Φ(atanh⁡∣ρ∣n−3−1.96)\Phi(\operatorname{atanh}|\rho|\sqrt{n-3} - 1.96)Φ(atanh∣ρ∣n−3​−1.96). Halving the correlation to detect roughly quadruples the pairs needed. Millbrook’s 306 days had about a 42% chance of detecting a true correlation of −0.1 between rain and footfall: its non-significant result is weak evidence of anything. And eight days had only an 18% chance of detecting a correlation as large as the year’s 0.44 between Harbourside’s high and its footfall.

Comparing two correlations

Harbourside’s rain correlation is significant and Millbrook’s is not. It is tempting to conclude that rain matters more at the waterfront, but “significant here, not significant there” is not a test of a difference. Compare the correlations directly. For two independent samples, transform each with Fisher’s z and use

z=atanh⁡r1−atanh⁡r21n1−3+1n2−3z = \dfrac{\operatorname{atanh} r_1 - \operatorname{atanh} r_2}{\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{n_1 - 3} + \dfrac{1}{n_2 - 3}}}z=n1​−31​+n2​−31​​atanhr1​−atanhr2​​

For rain and footfall, −0.228 at Harbourside against −0.102 at Millbrook gives z = −1.59 and a two-sided p-value of 0.11: no convincing evidence that the two correlations differ. For the day’s high and footfall the picture is different: 0.436 at Harbourside against 0.157 at Millbrook gives z = 3.81, p below 0.001. Warm weather goes with more customers at the waterfront than in the office district, which fits what each café is.

One caution. This formula assumes the samples are independent, and the cafés’ logs are not quite: they cover the same 306 days, share the same rain and temperatures, and their daily footfalls are themselves correlated (−0.39: weekends are busy at Harbourside and quiet at Millbrook). Tests that allow for overlapping samples exist but are beyond this course; treat the z here as approximate.

Statistical and practical significance

A significant correlation can be too small to matter, and an important one can fail to reach significance in a small sample. Judge size by r, its interval and r², in the context of the decision. Rain’s pooled r of −0.14 is significant but explains under 2% of the variation in footfall; Ana would not reschedule staff on rain forecasts on that evidence. How many customers an inch of rain costs is a slope, a question for regression in B06, not for r.

Finally, every test here is one test. Ana could test all 28 pairs of eight columns in her log, and at α = 0.05 more than one would be significant by chance even if nothing were related. The correlation-matrices lesson shows how to adjust for that.

Common mistakes

  • “p is below 0.001, so the correlation is strong.”

    The p-value measures how surprising r would be if ρ were 0; with hundreds of pairs even a trivial r passes. Rain’s pooled r = −0.14 is significant with 612 pairs and explains under 2% of the variation in footfall. Report r, its interval and r².

  • “r = 0.64 isn’t significant, so temperature and footfall are unrelated.”

    With 8 pairs the critical value is 0.707 and the test had about 18% power for the true correlation. Failing to reject is a lack of evidence, not evidence of no link; the interval, −0.12 to 0.93, shows how little 8 days can say.

  • “Harbourside is significant and Millbrook isn’t, so rain matters more at Harbourside.”

    A difference in significance is not a significant difference. Test the difference directly with Fisher’s z: for rain, z = −1.59 and p = 0.11. Here too the two samples share their days, so even that test is approximate.

  • Building the interval as r ± 1.96 × SE

    The sampling distribution of r is skewed and bounded by ±1, so a symmetric interval can be wrong or even run past 1. Transform with FISHER, add and subtract 1.96 ÷ √(n − 3), and transform back with FISHERINV.

  • Treating 612 rows as 612 independent observations

    Pooling the cafés repeats each day’s rain twice, and neighbouring days are alike. The test assumes independent pairs; when that fails its p-values are too small. Analyse each café separately and treat p-values from daily series as approximate.

  • Choosing the one-sided test after seeing the result

    Millbrook’s two-sided p = 0.074 becomes 0.037 one-sided. Unless the one-sided question was fixed before the data were seen, halving the p-value doubles the real chance of a false alarm.

In practice

Fictional case

Ana’s note on weather for the year-end review reads: “Rain: at Harbourside, r = −0.23 between rain and footfall (306 days; 95% CI −0.33 to −0.12; p < 0.001); at Millbrook r = −0.10 (CI −0.21 to 0.01; p = 0.07). The difference between the cafés is not significant (p = 0.11). Rain accounts for about 5% of the day-to-day variation in footfall at Harbourside and 1% at Millbrook. Temperature matters more at the waterfront: r = 0.44 at Harbourside against 0.16 at Millbrook (difference p < 0.001). Days are not independent, so treat p-values as approximate.”

The note gives every correlation with its interval and its n, says which test was used, and separates three questions that are easily blurred: is there an association (the test), how large is it (r, r² and the interval), and does it differ between groups (the comparison). It also states the limitation that matters most for daily data. A manager reading it can see that the rain effect is real but small, and that temperature, not rain, is the weather variable worth planning around at Harbourside.

The same pattern appears wherever correlations are reported. A retailer with millions of loyalty-card transactions will find significant correlations everywhere, and must ask which are large enough to act on. A hotel testing whether guest satisfaction tracks room price at its 12 properties can’t expect significance for anything but a very strong link, and should plan more data (or report the interval) rather than conclude “no relationship”. A battery-storage installer comparing the correlation between site temperature and system efficiency in two regions needs the Fisher-z comparison, not two separate p-values.

Two habits carry over. Plan the sample from the smallest correlation that would change a decision: 85 pairs for ρ = 0.3 at 80% power, 783 for ρ = 0.1. And when a correlation is significant, remember it is still only an association: whether warm days bring customers to the waterfront or something else drives both is the correlation-and-causation lesson’s question.

Summary

  • Test H₀: ρ = 0 with t = r√(n − 2) ÷ √(1 − r²) and n − 2 degrees of freedom: =T.DIST.2T(ABS(t),n-2) two-sided. Choose one- or two-sided before seeing the data.

Terms introduced

Population correlation
The correlation between two variables across the whole population the sample comes from, written ρ (rho). A sample correlation r estimates it.
Critical value of r
The smallest absolute value of a sample correlation that is statistically significant at a chosen level for a given number of pairs: t_crit ÷ √(n − 2 + t_crit²).
Fisher’s z transformation
z = atanh(r) = ½ ln((1 + r) ÷ (1 − r)), which turns a correlation into a quantity that is close to normally distributed with standard error 1 ÷ √(n − 3). Used for confidence intervals for ρ and for comparing correlations. Excel’s FISHER; FISHERINV reverses it.
Independent samples
Samples in which the observations in one have no connection with the observations in the other, such as different customers or different stores. Measurements of the same days, people or items are not independent samples.
