Charts and conditional formatting

A chart or a highlighted cell should answer one question at a glance. Choose the chart from the question, build it honestly in Excel (a title that states the finding, bars from zero, colour that never carries the meaning alone), and use conditional formatting, including formula rules with mixed references, to make the cells that matter stand out.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Choose a chart type from the question it must answer: a comparison, a change over time, a composition, a distribution or a relationship.
  • Build an Excel chart whose title states the finding, with labelled axes, units and direct labels, and calculate the figures it needs, such as shares of a total.
  • Evaluate a chart’s scales and colours: bars from zero, when a line chart may zoom, the risks of truncated and secondary axes, and colour that is never the only cue.
  • Apply Excel’s conditional formatting rules (highlight cells, top/bottom, above average, duplicates, data bars, colour scales and icon sets) and explain what each one compares.
  • Write formula-based conditional formatting rules with mixed references that highlight whole rows, and manage rule order and Stop If True.

Review first

  • PivotTables — The monthly and regional summaries charted here are the ones the PivotTable lesson builds.
  • Cells, references and formulas — Formula rules for conditional formatting depend on relative, absolute and mixed references.
  • Conditional sums, counts and averages — Chart data are often small summary tables built with SUMIFS and COUNTIFS.
  • Summarising data: changes, averages and spread — Shares of a total, percentage change and the shape of a distribution are what most charts show.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana has her spring summaries: order value and orders by month, region and channel, built with PivotTables from the 231 cleaned orders. Before the July relaunch she wants a two-page spring review (fictional) that her accountant and her part-time packer can read in a minute. Each chart on it has to answer one question, and her order sheet has to show, without scrolling and sorting, which orders need a second look.

A chart is an argument made with shapes. Before you choose a type, write down the question the reader should be able to answer, then pick the chart that makes that answer easiest to see. Excel will draw almost anything you select; it can’t know what you want the reader to notice.

Start from the question

Business charts usually answer one of five kinds of question. The Financial Times’ Visual Vocabulary, presented in the EU’s data.europa.eu visualisation guide, sorts charts by the pattern they highlight in the same spirit: magnitude, change over time, part-to-whole, distribution, correlation, and also ranking and deviation from a reference.

The question also decides what goes on the axes. For a comparison, the categories go on one axis and the measure on the other; sort the bars so that the order itself says something. For change over time, time runs left to right at equal intervals. For a relationship, the variable you think drives the other goes on the horizontal axis.

The parts of a chart

Every Excel chart is built from data series: sets of related values, such as order value for each region, each drawn in its own colour. A column, bar or line chart has a category axis, which places its labels (regions, months) in order along it, and a value axis, which has a numeric scale. A scatter chart has two value axes. Around them sit the chart title, axis titles, a legend that names the series, data labels on the bars or points, and gridlines.

Two of these do most of the work. The title should state the finding, not the topic: “Central led spring order value only because of one $1,512 office order” tells the reader what to see, while “Order value by region” leaves them to work it out. Labels should sit next to what they name; with one or two series, label the lines or bars directly and delete the legend, which forces the eye to travel back and forth.

Making a chart in Excel

Arrange the data as a small table: labels in the first column, one column per series, a header in the first row. Select it and choose Insert > Recommended Charts, which previews the charts Excel suggests for that data; the All Charts tab lists every type. On Windows, Alt+F1 inserts a chart of the default type beside the data at once, and F11 puts one on a separate chart sheet; neither asks what the chart is for. The chart stays linked to its cells, so it redraws when they change; a chart built on a PivotTable is a PivotChart, which the PivotTable lesson introduces.

Once the chart exists, add or remove parts with the Chart Elements button beside it (titles, axis titles, data labels, legend, trendline), change its type with Change Chart Type, and change which cells it plots with Select Data. Right-click a part and choose its Format command, such as Format Axis, to set its options, including the minimum and maximum of a value axis.

Honest scales

A bar shows its value by its length, so a bar chart must start at zero. If the axis starts at 45, a bar for 63 orders is 18 units long and a bar for 49 orders is 4 units long, and the first looks four and a half times the second although it is only 29% larger. A line shows its values by position and its changes by slope, so a line chart may zoom in to show variation, as long as the axis is labelled and the reader is not asked to compare heights with zero.

Key idea: Bars start at zero; lines may zoom

Length is the message in a bar chart, so cutting the axis distorts it. A line chart can use a narrower axis to show movement, because the reader reads position and slope, not length. Say so on the axis, and never mix the two in one comparison.

Colour and clutter

Use colour for meaning, not decoration: one strong colour for the series or bar you want noticed and a quiet grey for context. Remove what carries no information, such as 3-D effects, shadows, heavy gridlines and a legend for a single series. Every element left should help answer the question in the title.

Colour must never be the only cue. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG 2.2), written for web content but a sound standard for any chart, ask that colour is not the only visual means of conveying information (success criterion 1.4.1, level A), that bars, lines and other graphical objects needed to understand a chart have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against what is next to them (1.4.11, level AA), and that ordinary text has at least 4.5:1 (1.4.3, level AA). In a chart that means labels or markers as well as colours, and no pale series on a white background.

Conditional formatting

Conditional formatting applies a format to a cell only while a rule about its value is true, and re-applies it whenever the values change. It turns a sheet into a chart of itself: the large orders stand out, the duplicates are marked, a colour gradient shows high and low. You’ll find it at Home > Conditional Formatting.

The built-in rules compare each cell with a number or with the rest of the range: greater than 100, the top 10 items, above average, duplicates. When the condition depends on another column, you write a formula rule for the first cell of the range. In this lesson’s order sheet (order_id in A, customer_id B, product C, order_total D, channel E, subscription F), shading every column of a subscription order’s row takes a rule on A2:F16 written as =$F2="yes": the $ keeps every cell looking at column F, and the unlocked row lets each row test its own subscription flag.

In depth

Why bars beat pies

Cleveland and McGill (1984) measured how accurately people read quantities from different graphical encodings. They ranked the elementary tasks from most to least accurate: position along a common scale; position along non-aligned scales; length, direction and angle; area; volume and curvature; shading and colour saturation. In their experiment, judgements of position, as in a bar chart, were more accurate than judgements of angle, as in a pie chart.

That ranking explains most chart advice. Sorted bars on a common baseline use the most accurate task. In a stacked column only the bottom segment sits on the baseline; the others float, so comparing them across columns is a judgement of length on non-aligned scales. A pie asks for angles and areas. Microsoft suggests a pie chart only when there is one data series, no negative values, almost no zeros, and no more than seven categories that together make the whole.

Choosing between similar charts

Column or bar. Both compare categories. Microsoft suggests a bar chart (horizontal) when the labels are long, and bars also suit a ranking of many items, because the labels read left to right. Columns suit a handful of periods.

Line or scatter. In a line chart the horizontal axis is a category axis: text labels go at equal spacing whatever they say, a date axis puts dates in time order, and numbers are treated as labels. A scatter chart has two value axes and places every point at its numeric x. So if the x values are numbers that aren’t evenly spaced, such as the number of guests at each catering event, a line chart misplaces them; Microsoft’s rule of thumb is a line chart for non-numeric x values and a scatter chart for numeric ones.

Stacked and 100% stacked. A stacked column emphasises the total and shows the parts inside it; a 100% stacked column makes every column the same height and shows shares, which suits comparing a mix across months or regions. Use either with two or three parts at most, and put the part the reader must compare on the baseline.

Histogram and box and whisker. Both show a distribution, and Excel 2016 and later have both (Insert > Insert Statistic Chart). A histogram groups a numeric column into bins and draws a bar for the count in each: by default Excel sets the bin width by Scott’s normal reference rule, and you can set a bin width, a number of bins, and overflow and underflow bins instead. A box and whisker chart draws the quartiles as a box, the median inside it and whiskers beyond, with outlier points past the whiskers. The next lesson builds the frequency table behind a histogram.

Combo charts and secondary axes. A combo chart combines chart types, typically columns with a line, and Microsoft suggests a secondary axis when series vary widely or are of different kinds, such as price and volume. Use it with care: the two scales are independent, so where the line crosses the columns, and how steep it looks, depend on axis limits that someone chose. Label both axes with units, colour each axis like its series, and ask whether a ratio of the two (order value per order, say) or two charts one above the other would say it more plainly.

Sparklines. A sparkline is a tiny chart inside one worksheet cell (Insert > Sparklines: Line, Column or Win/Loss). Put one at the end of each row of a table, for example each product’s weekly sales, to show many trends side by side without a chart for each. Markers can pick out individual points, and an axis can be shown when values change sign.

Conditional formatting rules in Excel

  • Highlight Cells Rules: Greater Than, Less Than, Between, Equal To, Text that Contains, A Date Occurring and Duplicate Values. Each compares a cell with a value you type (or a cell you point to).
  • Top/Bottom Rules: Top 10 Items, Top 10%, Bottom 10 Items, Bottom 10%, Above Average and Below Average. The 10 is only the default: you can choose 1 to 1,000 items or 1% to 100% of the range. Average rules compare with the mean of the selected range.
  • Data Bars: a bar inside each cell, longer for larger values.
  • Color Scales: a two- or three-colour gradient between the minimum, a midpoint and the maximum.
  • Icon Sets: three to five icons, each for a band of values set by thresholds. Microsoft says the default bands are equal in size; with three arrows that is roughly the top, middle and bottom third of the way from the lowest value to the highest (the sheets in this lesson split at 67% and 33%).
  • New Rule > Use a formula to determine which cells to format: any formula that returns TRUE or FALSE.

By default (Automatic) a data bar’s length is proportional to its value, so the longest bar belongs to the largest value; a colour scale runs from the lowest value to the highest. You can change either by setting the minimum and maximum types (a number, a percent, a percentile or a formula). That matters when one value dwarfs the rest. Lantern’s office order of $1,512 would stretch the data bars so far that a $58 gift box got a sliver of a bar; setting the maximum to a number, such as 200, or to a percentile, restores the comparison among ordinary orders.

Formula rules and mixed references

A formula rule is written for the top-left cell of the “Applies to” range, and Excel adjusts it for every other cell as if the formula had been filled across and down: relative references move, absolute ones don’t. That is why whole-row highlighting needs a mixed reference such as $F2, with the column locked and the row free. The formula must return TRUE or FALSE (or 1 or 0), and it must start with an equals sign.

Useful patterns, each written for row 2 of a range that starts in column A: =$D2>=100 (orders of $100 or more); =AND($E2="app",$D2>=30) (app orders of at least $30); =COUNTIF($B$2:$B$232,$B2)>1 (customers with more than one order: the whole range locked, the row free); =$D2>=LARGE($D$2:$D$232,10) (the ten largest orders, with ties; LARGE(range, k) returns the kth largest value). One trap: when you click cells while typing a rule, Excel inserts absolute references such as $D$2, which would make every row test row 2. Delete the $ before the row number.

Test a rule before trusting it. Type the same formula in a spare cell of row 2, fill it across and down, and check that it says TRUE exactly where you expect: conditional formatting will move it in the same way. If the formula returns an error in some cells, those cells are not formatted, so a rule can fail silently.

Rule order and Stop If True

Home > Conditional Formatting > Manage Rules lists the rules for a range in order of precedence, top to bottom; a new rule goes to the top, and Move Up and Move Down change the order. When two true rules set different things (one bold, one a red fill), both apply. When they conflict (two fills), the rule higher in the list wins. A true rule also overrides any manual formatting of the cell.

Stop If True, a tick box beside each rule, stops Excel evaluating the rules below it once that rule is true. Microsoft describes it as a backward-compatibility setting, and it isn’t available for data bars, colour scales or icon sets. Because conflicting formats are already settled by precedence, Stop If True matters when a lower rule sets something that doesn’t conflict, such as bold, a data bar or an icon set, which a formula rule with Stop If True hides for the rows it matches.

Caution: Pitfalls

Copying, pasting and filling cells that carry a rule create new rules for the destination, so a busy sheet can collect dozens of near-duplicates: check Manage Rules (show rules for “This Worksheet”) now and then. Keep to a few rules with clear meanings, give every colour a meaning stated in a note or a status column, and remember that a rule that returns an error formats nothing.

Common mistakes

  • “Excel picked the chart, so it must suit the data.”

    Recommended Charts suggests charts for the shape of the selection, and Alt+F1 inserts the default chart type, whatever your question. Decide the question first (comparison, trend, composition, distribution, relationship), then the chart.

  • “The title is ‘Order value by region’.”

    That is a label, not a message. State the finding, such as “Central led only because of one $1,512 office order”, so the reader knows what to look for and you are forced to check it is true.

  • “Starting the axis at 45 makes the differences easier to see.”

    In a bar chart it makes them wrong: 63 against 49 looked like 4.5 to 1. Start bars at zero; zoom only line charts, and label the axis.

  • “Red for bad, green for good is clear enough.”

    Many readers can’t tell some pairs of colours apart, and printouts are often black and white. WCAG 1.4.1 asks that colour is never the only cue: add a status word, an icon or a direct label.

  • “My rule highlights only the first column.”

    A whole-row rule written as =F2="yes" moves to =G2="yes", =H2="yes" … in the other columns. Lock the column with a mixed reference, =$F2="yes", and write the rule for the top-left cell of the range.

  • “I set two rules; the second one is ignored.”

    When two true rules set conflicting formats, the one higher in Manage Rules wins, and Stop If True on a rule stops the ones below it. Check the order, and remember a rule that returns an error formats nothing.

In practice

Monthly management packs often mix a few charts with a table full of conditional formats. The test to apply is the same in both: can the reader say what to do after ten seconds? A finance team’s variance table, for instance, might shade only the lines more than 10% from budget, with the variance written beside the shading, and put one chart above it whose title says which line drove the month.

Fictional case

Ana’s spring review ends up with four visuals and one rule set: a sorted bar chart of order value by region, titled with the office-order caveat; a line of weekly orders, steady at 9 to 16 a week; a single 100% bar of web against app (59.8% to 40.2%); and a histogram of order values, which the next lesson builds. On the order sheet, a formula rule shades subscription rows, data bars show order totals with the maximum set to $200 so the office order doesn’t flatten the others, and a status column repeats every colour in words.

The same habits carry into the dashboards lesson at the end of this course: a question for every visual, a title that states the finding, honest scales, and colour that is never alone. Conditional formatting also becomes a quality check. On Lantern’s full order sheet (quantity in G, unit_price in H, discount in I, order_total in J), a rule such as =ABS($J2-($G2*$H2-$I2))>0.005 would shade any order whose total doesn’t equal quantity × price − discount, the check that B02’s cleaning relied on.

Summary

  • Start from the question: bars for comparisons, lines for change over time, stacked or 100% bars (or a pie with few parts) for composition, histograms and box plots for distributions, scatter charts for relationships.
  • Give every chart a title that states the finding, label axes with units, label series directly, and remove clutter.
  • Bars start at zero, because length carries the value; a line chart may zoom. On a truncated axis two bars look (v₁ − b) ÷ (v₂ − b) times different instead of v₁ ÷ v₂.
  • Use a line chart for categories at equal intervals and a scatter chart when x is numeric; be wary of secondary axes, and consider a ratio or two aligned charts instead.
  • Colour is never the only cue (WCAG 1.4.1); chart elements need 3:1 contrast and text 4.5:1.
  • Conditional formatting rules: highlight cells, top/bottom, above/below average, duplicates, data bars, colour scales, icon sets and formula rules that return TRUE or FALSE.
  • A formula rule is written for the top-left cell of the range and moved like a filled formula; lock the column, =$F2="yes", to format whole rows.
  • Rules higher in Manage Rules win conflicts; Stop If True stops the rules below; copying cells multiplies rules, and an error formats nothing.

Terms introduced

Data series
A set of related values plotted together in a chart, such as order value for each month. Each series has its own colour or marker and its own entry in the legend.
Category axis
The axis of a column, bar or line chart that places text labels at equal spacing whatever they say, or dates in time order (a date axis); numbers placed on it are treated as labels.
Value axis
A chart axis with a numeric scale. Column, bar and line charts have one; a scatter chart has two.
Scatter chart
A chart with two value axes that plots each observation as a point at its x and y values, used to show the relationship between two numeric variables. Also called an XY chart.
Box and whisker chart
A chart of a distribution that draws the first and third quartiles as a box with the median inside it, whiskers for the spread beyond the box, a marker for the mean, and separate points for outliers. Also called a box plot.
Combo chart
A chart that combines two or more chart types, such as columns and a line, often with a secondary axis.
Secondary axis
A second value axis, usually on the right of a chart, with its own scale for one or more series.
Sparkline
A tiny chart inside a single worksheet cell that shows the trend of a row or column of numbers.
Truncated axis
A value axis that starts above zero. It distorts bar lengths, and so the comparisons a bar chart makes.
Contrast ratio
A measure of the difference in brightness between two colours, (L₁ + 0.05) ÷ (L₂ + 0.05), where L₁ and L₂ are the relative luminances of the lighter and darker colour; it runs from 1:1 to 21:1.
Conditional formatting
Formatting that a spreadsheet applies to a cell only while a rule about its value, or a formula, is true, and that updates when the values change.
Data bar
A conditional format that draws a bar inside each cell whose length, by default, is proportional to the cell’s value.
Colour scale
A conditional format that shades cells along a gradient of two or three colours between the lowest and highest values.
Icon set
A conditional format that puts one of three to five icons in each cell according to the band of values, set by thresholds, that the cell falls in.
