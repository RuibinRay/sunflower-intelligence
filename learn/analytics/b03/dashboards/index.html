Dashboards that answer questions

A dashboard is one page that tells its readers where things stand. Learn to start from the audience’s questions, give every number a comparison, lay the page out so the most important measure comes first, build it on tables, formulas and PivotTables so it refreshes, and add the documentation and checks that make it trustworthy.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Identify a dashboard’s audience and questions, and choose the measures that answer them, each with a definition, a comparison and an owner.
  • Design a one-page layout that puts the most important measure first, with consistent scales, formats and labels and a fitting display for each question.
  • Build dashboard figures with SUMIFS, COUNTIFS, AVERAGEIFS and XLOOKUP driven by a drop-down cell, and with PivotTables, slicers and timelines on one table of data.
  • Document a dashboard’s sources, definitions and refresh date, and add reconciliation checks that prove it agrees with its data.
  • Evaluate a dashboard for common failures and accessibility problems, and plan how it is shared and kept under version control.

Review first

  • KPIs: measuring what matters — A dashboard shows KPIs, each with a formula, a target, a direction and an owner, written down in a KPI dictionary.
  • Conditional sums, counts and averages — The tiles are SUMIFS, COUNTIFS and AVERAGEIFS with date windows.
  • PivotTables — The charts are built on PivotTables, which must be refreshed when the data change.
  • Charts and conditional formatting — Each question on the page needs the chart that answers it, drawn honestly.
  • Excel tables and cleaning data — An Excel table that grows with the data, and data validation lists, are the dashboard’s foundations.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

It is the first working day of January 2027. Lantern’s catering business held 76 events in 2026 and invoiced $62,320 for them, from 18 clients. Ana Lim reviews catering every month, and Lantern’s accountant looks at it every quarter. Until now each review has begun with an hour of filtering the events list. Ana wants one page that answers her standing questions as soon as she opens it, refreshes from the events list in minutes, and is trustworthy enough to send to the accountant.

A dashboard answers someone’s questions

B01 defined a dashboard as a single screen or page showing a small set of KPIs, each with its current value, target, comparison and trend, for the people who act on them. Stephen Few, a consultant who has written widely on dashboard design, described it as a “single-screen display of the most important information needed to do a job”. The words that matter are most important and a job: a dashboard is designed for particular readers and the decisions they make, not to show everything the data contain.

So design starts with the audience and their questions, not with the data. Ana decides staffing, pricing and which clients to chase; the accountant checks that catering is on plan and that the figures agree with the books. Their standing questions are:

  1. Are we on plan this month, and for the year so far?
  2. Is the business growing from month to month?
  3. What kinds of event bring in the revenue?
  4. Is the typical job holding its size?
  5. How much do we depend on a few clients?

From questions to measures

Each question becomes a measure, and each measure is written down with its exact definition, the comparison that gives it meaning and its owner, in the key performance indicator dictionary that B01 introduced. Writing the definitions forces decisions: is revenue counted when an event happens or when it is invoiced? Which month does Meridian Partners’ two-day conference belong to?

The last measure but one deserves a word. Client concentration is the share of revenue that comes from the largest few clients. In 2026 Lantern’s top four clients brought in $29,420 of the $62,320, 47.2%: the loss of one of them would hurt. Ana sets a ceiling of 50%, a guardrail in B01’s sense, which the dashboard watches.

A number needs a comparison

Few’s blunt test for any number on a dashboard is “compared to what?”. A tile reading “Revenue $10,120” can’t be judged on its own. Three comparisons are standard: a target or plan, the previous period, and the same period last year, which removes the seasons. Each is a baseline in B01’s sense. Lantern began catering in March 2026, so it has no last year yet; its plan and the previous month have to do.

With them, December’s figure tells a story: $10,120 was the best month of the year, $1,330 more than November, yet $880 below the month’s plan of $11,000 (92.0%). Show the comparison as a gap or a share, as B01’s KPI lesson did, and say which way is good in words, not only in colour.

Layout: the most important first

Readers of left-to-right languages start at the top left, and many stop once they have what they came for. Put the headline numbers there, the explanations below, and the detail last. The UK Government Analysis Function’s guidance calls this an inverted pyramid, “with your most important headline content up front”, and asks dashboards to minimise scrolling. Ordering a page by position, size and emphasis in this way is its visual hierarchy.

Read the wireframe in its own order. The tiles answer “are we on plan?” in a second; the column chart answers “is it growing?” and shows the quiet summer; the type bars and the client table answer the questions about mix and dependence. The notes say what the numbers mean, where they came from and whether they add up. Nothing on the page is there for decoration.

Key idea: One page, few measures, every number with a comparison

Choose the handful of measures the readers act on, give each a comparison, put the most important first, and leave everything else in the workbook behind the page.

In depth

A display for each question

The charts lesson matched charts to questions; a dashboard applies that rule to a whole page.

  • Headline measures are tiles: the number, its comparison, and a word or symbol for the direction (“below plan”, ▼). Use the same layout and format for every tile.
  • Trends are columns or a line along a time axis. Monthly revenue against plan suits columns with the plan as a marker, so the bars stay the main message; columns start at zero.
  • Composition and ranking are sorted bars. Five event types compare far better as bars than as slices; keep pies, if at all, for two or three parts of a whole.
  • A few items with details, such as top clients with their revenue and events, are a small sorted table, with the rest grouped as “others” if they are shown at all.

Avoid what spends space without informing. Gauges and dials take room for one number and add no comparison of their own; 3-D effects distort sizes; a second vertical axis invites false comparisons; and decoration such as shading, pictures and borders competes with the data. Few’s review of failed dashboards singles out gauges and decoration in the same way.

Keep everything consistent so that differences mean something. One money format throughout (whole dollars on a summary page), one colour for revenue in every chart, the event types always in the same order, and the same axis range for charts that are meant to be compared side by side. A reader who has to work out each chart afresh will misread one of them.

Building it so it refreshes

A dashboard that takes an hour to update will be out of date. Build it in three layers: the data, untouched, in one Excel table (Events) that grows as rows are added; the calculations, as formulas and PivotTables that read the table; and the page, which only displays results. When January’s events are added, formulas that refer to the table’s columns pick them up at once, and PivotTables pick them up when they are refreshed (the PivotTables lesson shows how).

The tiles are formulas driven by one input cell, the month the reader chooses. Each formula reads the month from that cell and builds a date window from it, so a single change updates the whole page:

In the full workbook the ranges are the table’s columns, such as Events[invoice], so they grow with the data. The third widget under Explore is this sheet.

The average invoice is the month’s revenue divided by its events, not an average of other averages; the challenging example shows why. The charts sit on PivotTables built from the same table, so every figure on the page comes from one source.

Interactivity: drop-downs, slicers and timelines

A drop-down list lets the reader choose what the formulas show without typing. In Excel it is data validation: select the cell, then Data › Data Validation, set Allow to List and give the Source. If the list itself is an Excel table, the drop-down updates as items are added or removed. The Error Alert tab’s Stop style refuses anything typed that isn’t on the list, so a reader can’t type a month the formulas can’t read (pasted values still get through, as the tables lesson warns).

A slicer is a panel of buttons that filters a table or a PivotTable and shows which items are selected. Click inside the table or PivotTable and choose Insert › Slicer; select one item or several, and use Clear Filter to show them all again. One slicer can drive several PivotTables through Report Connections, but only PivotTables that share the same data source, which is another reason to build them all on one table. A timeline does the same job for dates in PivotTables: a slider that selects years, quarters, months or days (PivotTable Analyze › Insert Timeline).

Caution: Filters and formulas don’t talk to each other

A slicer on a table hides the rows it filters out. SUBTOTAL, which a table’s total row uses, ignores those rows; SUMIFS, COUNTIFS and the rest do not, and go on adding every row that meets their own criteria. A page that mixes slicer-filtered charts with SUMIFS tiles can show two answers to the same question. Choose one mechanism for each part of the page, or put the slicer’s choice into the formulas as a criterion.

Documentation and checks

A dashboard is trusted only if its readers know what the numbers mean and can see that they add up. A short notes panel does the first job: the source and how it was extracted; the period covered (the Analysis Function’s guidance is to make the dates always clear); the date the data were refreshed, typed in when it happens rather than calculated with TODAY(), which would show the date the file was opened; the definitions, or a link to the KPI dictionary; and the owner. When something changes, a dated change note says what and why.

A reconciliation check does the second. It compares a total on the page with the same total computed another way and flags any difference: the ten monthly revenues (March to December, in J2:J11) should add up to the total of the Events table, so should the five event types, and both should match the $62,320 of catering revenue in Lantern’s books (F02); the events should number 76. A check cell such as =IF(ROUND(SUM(J2:J11)-SUM(Events[invoice]),2)=0,"OK","CHECK") sits beside the figures it protects. Rounding to the cent stops the tiny binary differences of the cells-and-formulas lesson from raising false alarms.

Common failures

  • Too much. Eleven charts make the important three hard to find. Each measure must answer a question someone acts on.
  • No comparison. Numbers without a plan, last period or last year can’t be judged.
  • Decoration. Gauges, 3-D and pictures spend space and attention without informing.
  • Stale data. A page with no refresh date invites decisions on old figures; timeliness is part of data quality (B01).
  • Inconsistent scales and formats. Cents in one chart and thousands in another, or axes that differ without warning.
  • Colour alone. Red and green with no words or symbols fail readers who can’t tell them apart.
  • Drifting definitions. A measure quietly redefined breaks its own history; the KPI dictionary and a change note prevent it.

Accessibility

Microsoft’s guidance for accessible spreadsheets applies directly. Give every chart alt text that states what it shows and its main message, and clear titles and axis labels. Name each sheet descriptively and remove blank sheets. Avoid merged cells and blank rows and columns, which confuse screen readers. Turn on header rows in tables.

Keep a high contrast ratio between text and background, and never let colour carry meaning alone. Run Review › Check Accessibility before sharing.

Even so, dashboards are hard to make fully accessible. The Analysis Function notes that they rarely meet every accessibility requirement and advises publishing the data in another format as well, and in the UK public-sector dashboards must meet WCAG 2.2 at level AA. For Ana that means sending the accountant the underlying table alongside the page.

Sharing and version control

Readers should not be able to break the page. Send a PDF or a read-only copy, or protect the formula and data areas and leave only the drop-down cells open. Keep one master workbook with one owner. If it is stored in OneDrive or SharePoint, Version history lets you open an earlier version and restore it (a personal Microsoft account keeps the last 25 versions); record what changed in a change log sheet, dated, so that a number that moves between two versions can be explained.

Common mistakes

  • “Put everything on it, in case someone asks.”

    Each extra chart makes the important ones harder to find. Keep the measures the readers act on; leave the rest in the workbook, one click away.

  • “Red is bad and green is good; everyone can see that.”

    Not everyone can tell red from green, and a printed page in grey loses both. Say “below plan” or use a symbol as well as the colour.

  • “The slicer filtered the table, so the SUMIFS tiles changed.”

    They didn’t. SUMIFS and COUNTIFS add every row that meets their criteria, hidden or not; only SUBTOTAL ignores rows a filter excludes. Drive formula tiles from a drop-down cell, or use PivotTables with the slicer.

  • “The average of the monthly averages is the year’s average.”

    Only if every month has the same number of events. Divide total revenue by total events: $62,320 ÷ 76 = $820, not $872.23.

  • “=TODAY() shows readers the data are current.”

    TODAY() shows the date the file is opened, so it makes old figures look new. Type the date the data were refreshed, and state the period covered.

  • “A gauge makes it look like a real dashboard.”

    A dial takes the space of several numbers to show one, and its scale ends are arbitrary. The number with its comparison says more in less space.

In practice

Dashboards are among the most common things analysts build, and the same design decisions recur everywhere. Three fictional examples show the range.

A hotel group’s weekly page. The operations director of three hotels reviews occupancy, average room rate and revenue per available room every Monday, each against budget and the same week last year. The page also shows the next four weeks’ bookings already made, a leading indicator, and a single exceptions list of hotels more than five points below budget.

A charity’s fundraising page. The trustees see income year to date against the plan, the share from regular donors (their guardrail against relying on one-off appeals) and the cost of raising each dollar. A notes panel explains that legacy gifts are counted when they are confirmed, not when they are promised, after a year in which a promised gift that never arrived flattered the figures.

A gym’s membership page. The manager tracks members, joiners and leavers by month, with a drop-down for the site. A reconciliation check compares the membership system’s count with the payments system’s every month; when the two disagreed, the dashboard found a batch of failed card payments before the accountant did.

Fictional case

At her January review Ana reads the page in a minute. The year beat its plan, but the fourth quarter came in at $27,900 against a plan of $28,000, and December missed by $880. The type bars show meetings bringing the most revenue and launches the least. The client table worries her most: four clients bring 47.2% of the revenue, close to her 50% ceiling. She sets two actions for the first quarter, winning three new clients and applying the new hourly rule from the what-if lesson to long events, and adds both to the change log so that March’s page can show whether they worked.

A dashboard is the end of an analysis, not the start: it is where the cleaned data, the formulas, the PivotTables and the charts of this course meet the people who decide. Its quality is measured by the decisions it makes easier, which is why every choice on the page, from the measures to the date in the corner, should be traceable to a question someone asked.

Summary

  • A dashboard is one page of the few measures its audience acts on; design starts from the readers and their questions, not from the data.
  • Write each measure down with its definition, comparison and owner; client concentration (the top clients’ share of revenue) is a measure of risk worth watching.
  • Every number needs a comparison: a plan or target, the previous period, or the same period last year.
  • Put the most important measure first (top left, an inverted pyramid), choose the display from the question, and keep formats, colours and scales consistent; avoid gauges, 3-D and decoration.
  • Build on one Excel table: formula tiles driven by a drop-down cell and date windows, PivotTables refreshed from the table, slicers and timelines connected to PivotTables with the same data source.
  • Slicers and filters don’t change SUMIFS results; SUBTOTAL ignores filtered-out rows. Use one mechanism per part of the page.
  • Document the source, period, refresh date and definitions, and add reconciliation checks that prove the page agrees with its data.
  • Make it accessible (alt text, names, contrast, no colour-only meaning), share a protected or read-only copy, and keep versions and a change log.

Terms introduced

Client concentration
The share of revenue that comes from a business’s largest few clients; a high share means the loss of one client would hurt badly.
Visual hierarchy
The ordering of a page by position, size and emphasis so that readers see the most important information first.
Drop-down list
A cell limited by data validation to the values in a list, which the user picks from an arrow beside the cell; in a dashboard it lets the reader choose what the formulas show.
Timeline
An Excel filter for the date fields of PivotTables: a slider that selects years, quarters, months or days.
Reconciliation check
A formula that compares a total shown in a report with the same total calculated another way, such as from the source data or the accounts, and flags any difference.
Year to date
The period from the start of the year, or of the series, to the end of the latest complete period. Abbreviated YTD.
