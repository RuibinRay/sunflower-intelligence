Questionnaire design

A survey can only be as good as its questions. Turn research objectives into questions that each earn their place, choose the right question types and rating scales, write options and wording that don’t push answers, guard against response styles and order effects, and pretest the result before anyone sees it, and check that multi-item scales are reliable and valid.

About 85 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Write survey questions from research objectives, justifying each by the analysis and decision it serves, and plan a questionnaire’s flow, screening and routing.
  • Choose among open, closed, dichotomous, multiple-choice, ranking and rating questions, and among Likert, semantic differential, numeric, purchase-intent and Net Promoter scales, stating the level of measurement and the analysis each allows.
  • Write response options that are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, and identify and fix leading, loaded, double-barrelled, ambiguous, double-negative, jargon-laden, recall and sensitive questions.
  • Explain acquiescence, social desirability, extreme and midpoint responding, question-order effects and primacy and recency, and reduce them by design.
  • Plan a pretest with cognitive interviews, and compare web, telephone and face-to-face modes in outline.
  • Assess a multi-item scale’s reliability (test–retest, and internal consistency with Cronbach’s alpha) and the evidence for its validity (content, construct and criterion).

Review first

  • Collecting data: sources, samples and bias — Pew’s basic rules for survey questions (one thing at a time, no leading wording, balanced scales, pretesting) and the table of weak and better questions are taken as read here.
  • Types of data — Levels of measurement (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) decide what you can calculate from each answer.
  • The research process — Questions are written to answer research objectives, so you need the objectives first.

The idea

Fictional case

In January 2027, while she wrote her research brief, Ana drafted the questionnaire for the customer survey that B04’s project planned. The brief (see The research process) names two decisions, whether and how to launch Cold Brew and where to focus Lantern’s 2027 marketing, and gives the survey three objectives: the share of the list who would buy Cold Brew, how likely people are to recommend Lantern, and how they buy coffee, found Lantern and think it could improve. Her first draft ran to twelve questions. The questionnaire that went out on 2 February 2027 had six, and most of this lesson is in the difference.

From objectives to questions

A questionnaire is a fixed set of questions asked in the same words and order of everyone, with answers recorded in a fixed form, so that one person’s answers can be counted alongside another’s. Writing one starts from the research objectives, not from a blank page. For every question, name the objective it serves, the table or figure you will make from the answers and the decision that figure could change. If you can’t, the question doesn’t earn its place.

Questions that nobody will analyse are not harmless. They lengthen the questionnaire, which tires respondents and lowers the quality of the answers that matter. Personal questions that serve no purpose also break the principle of data minimisation. And every extra question is one more chance for a badly worded item to mislead.

Kinds of question

An open question lets people answer in their own words, as Q6 does; a closed question asks them to choose from a list. Closed questions come in four common forms. A dichotomous question offers two answers (yes or no). A multiple-choice question offers a list and asks for one answer, or for all that apply. A ranking question asks people to put options in order, and a rating scale asks them to place a judgement on a set of ordered points.

The list in a closed question is not neutral: it reminds people of some answers and hides others. Pew Research Center asked one sample in 2008 which issue mattered most in their presidential vote, with five options listed, and another sample the same question with no list. With the list, 58% chose the economy and only 8% gave an answer outside the five. Without it, 35% named the economy and 43% gave an answer that wasn’t on the list. Pew notes that researchers sometimes pilot an open version and build the closed list from the commonest answers.

Open questions have two other uses. They suit amounts: Krosnick and Presser’s review of the research finds open questions usually preferable to closed ones for measuring quantities, because the ranges in a closed list can themselves bias answers, which is why Q3 asks for a number. And they collect reasons and ideas nobody thought to list, at the price of coding the answers into themes before they can be counted.

Rating scales

Most marketing questionnaires measure attitudes with a handful of standard formats, shown in the figure.

Likert item
A statement with five (sometimes seven) ordered answers from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”, named after the psychologist Rensis Likert.
Likert scale
Several Likert items about one attitude, scored and averaged (or summed) into one number. Some items are worded in the opposite direction and reverse-coded before averaging.
Semantic differential
A row of points, usually seven, between two opposite adjectives (ordinary … distinctive), on which people place a brand or product.
Numeric rating scale
A question answered with a number on a stated range, such as 0 to 10, with only the ends labelled.
Purchase-intent scale
A question about how likely people are to buy a described product, on verbal points from “definitely would buy” to “definitely would not buy”. Lantern’s Q2 is one.
Net Promoter question
Reichheld’s (2003) question, “How likely is it that you would recommend [company X] to a friend or colleague?”, on a 0 to 10 scale. Bain’s Net Promoter site now gives it as “How likely are you to recommend us to a friend or colleague?”, the form Lantern’s Q1 follows.

The Net Promoter question is worth knowing exactly, because so many firms track it. Reichheld (2003) calls people who answer 9 or 10 promoters, 7 or 8 “passively satisfied” (usually shortened to passives) and 0 to 6 detractors. The Net Promoter Score is the percentage of promoters minus the percentage of detractors, so it runs from −100 to +100. Calculating it, and its uncertainty, is the subject of module 4; here the point is that the score throws away the difference between a 0 and a 6, so keep to the standard wording if you want to compare with anyone else’s: Reichheld’s, or the close variant Bain gives today, which Lantern’s Q1 follows. Bain defines the three groups in the same way.

How many points, and which labels? Krosnick and Presser conclude that seven points are probably best for many rating scales, that answers are more reliable when every point has a word label rather than only the ends, and that the labels should divide the range into roughly equal steps. Pew advises keeping most closed questions to about four or five options, especially on the telephone, where people must hold every option in their heads. Both rules are about what people can answer well, and which applies depends on the mode.

What each answer lets you calculate

B01 sorted variables by their level of measurement: nominal variables can only be counted, ordinal variables can also be put in order, and interval variables and ratio variables support differences (and, for ratio, ratios and true zeros). A questionnaire fixes the level of every variable before any data exists, so decide the analysis first and write the question to match it.

In depth

Response options

The options to a closed question must be mutually exclusive, so that no answer fits two of them, and exhaustive, so that every answer fits one. Ana’s draft broke both rules. Its age bands, 18–35, 35–55 and 55+, gave someone aged 35 or 55 two boxes to tick, and its list of where people had heard of Lantern (social media, a friend, a market stall) had no box for anyone who came through catering, an event or a workshop. Exhaustive lists come from pilots and from what you already know about the population, not from guesswork.

An “Other (please specify)” box looks like a cure for a short list, but Krosnick and Presser report that it is generally ineffective: people tend to choose among the options shown rather than write in their own. “Don’t know” needs judgement too. Their review finds considerable evidence that “don’t know” filters do not improve the measurement of opinions, because many who take that exit do hold a view, so offer it where not knowing is a genuine answer (whether someone has seen a product, say) rather than on every attitude question. “Prefer not to say” belongs on personal questions: it respects people’s right not to answer and records a refusal as a refusal instead of a blank.

Leading and loaded questions are close relatives. A leading question tells people which answer is expected; a loaded one makes them accept something (that they are excited, that the House Blend is much loved) before they can answer at all. Sensitive questions are sometimes necessary: when they are, ask them late, in a self-administered mode, with bands rather than exact figures and a “prefer not to say” option, and only if an objective needs the answer.

Response styles

A response style is a tendency to answer in a particular way whatever the question says. Four matter most in marketing surveys, and all of them are forms of measurement bias.

  • Acquiescence: agreeing with statements regardless of their content. Krosnick and Presser’s review puts the effect at about 10% on average across studies; Pew finds it stronger among less educated and less informed respondents and when an interviewer is present. Agree/disagree, true/false and yes/no formats invite it; Pew’s remedy is to offer a choice between alternative statements, or to ask about the thing itself.
  • Social desirability bias: answering in the way that makes you look good. Pew’s examples are people understating how much they drink and overstating how often they vote or go to church; the effect is larger when an interviewer is present. In marketing surveys the same pressure can inflate reported recycling, healthy eating or loyalty to local and ethical brands.
  • Extreme responding: choosing the end points of scales more than one’s views warrant.
  • Midpoint responding: retreating to the middle option, sometimes as an easy way out.

Behind several of these sits satisficing, Krosnick and Presser’s term for taking short cuts instead of doing the work an answer needs: understanding the question, recalling what is relevant, forming a judgement and mapping it onto the options. Satisficing grows with the difficulty of the task and falls with the respondent’s ability and motivation, so long, dull and difficult questionnaires get worse answers. The remedies are design choices. Ask about the thing itself (“How easy or difficult was it to order online?”) instead of asking for agreement with a statement; keep questionnaires short; put sensitive questions in self-administered modes; and word them so that the less flattering answer is easy to give.

Reliability and validity

Two questions decide whether a measure is worth having. Reliability is its consistency: whether it gives the same result when nothing has changed. Measurement validity is whether it measures what it is meant to. (B01’s data-quality sense of validity, values that conform to their definition, is a different idea: an answer of 4 on a 1-to-5 scale is valid in that sense whether or not the scale measures the right thing.) A measure can be reliable and still invalid, but, in Tavakol and Dennick’s words, “an instrument cannot be valid unless it is reliable”.

Two checks of reliability are common. Test–retest reliability asks the same people the same questions twice, a short time apart, and correlates the two sets of scores; Price, Jhangiani and Chiang treat a correlation of +.80 or more as good, for things expected to stay stable (an attitude to a brand, not this morning’s mood). Internal consistency asks whether the items of a multi-item scale, such as a Likert scale, move together. It is usually summarised by Cronbach’s alpha (Cronbach, 1951): the variances of the items compared with the variance of their total.

If the items move together, the total varies far more than the items do separately, and α approaches 1; if they are unrelated, α is near 0, and it goes negative when items pull against each other, as a reverse-worded item left unreversed does. Tavakol and Dennick note that the acceptable values reported range from 0.70 to 0.95, and Price and colleagues treat +.80 as good. Higher is not always better: Tavakol and Dennick note a recommended maximum of 0.90, because a very high α can mean items that ask the same question in different words, lengthening the questionnaire without adding information. Alpha also assumes the items measure one thing without showing that they do, and it rises with the number of items, so a long scale can reach .70 with weakly related items.

Validity is argued from several kinds of evidence. Content validity asks whether the items cover every part of the idea as defined, judged by comparing the questions with the definition; face validity, whether a measure looks right on the surface, is weaker evidence, because appearances mislead. Construct validity asks whether scores relate to other measures as they should: strongly to other measures of the same idea (convergent validity) and weakly to measures of different ideas (discriminant validity). Criterion validity asks whether scores agree with an outcome measured at the same time (concurrent validity) or later (predictive validity). Texts group this evidence differently: Price, Jhangiani and Chiang count agreement with another measure of the same idea as criterion evidence and don’t use the term construct validity, while marketing-research texts usually treat convergent and discriminant evidence as construct validity, as this lesson does.

Purchase intent is the marketing case of predictive validity. Lantern’s Q2 could give much the same answers if asked twice and still be a poor guide to buying, which is the stated-intent problem Survey error takes up. Its test comes after launch, when Ana can set what list members said in February against what they go on to buy through Lantern’s own channel.

Order and context effects

Pew notes that “earlier questions can unintentionally provide context for the questions that follow”. In one of its December 2008 surveys, 88% said they were dissatisfied with the way things were going in the country when the question came straight after one about the president’s job, against 78% when it came first: ten points from the order alone. Such order effects are why a questionnaire usually moves from general questions to specific ones, and why a question tracked over time keeps its place in the questionnaire.

Lantern’s questionnaire follows that funnel. Q1 asks about Lantern in general before Q2 asks about one new product, so the product question can’t colour the overall judgement; the factual Q3 to Q5 follow; and the open Q6 comes last, when people have already thought about Lantern. Ana kept this order for every respondent so that next year’s answers can be compared with this year’s.

The order of the options matters as well. In a primacy effect people choose options near the top of a list more often; in a recency effect they choose those heard last. Pew links the first to self-administered surveys, where people read the list, and the second to telephone surveys, where they hear it. Krosnick and Presser add that rating scales show primacy in both modes. Pew randomises the order of unordered lists such as Lantern’s Q4, so no option is always first (a catch-all such as “Search or other” stays at the end), but keeps ordered scales in order and reverses them for half the sample, which spreads the effect rather than removing it.

Flow: screening, routing and length

A questionnaire has a shape. It opens by saying who is asking, why, how long it will take and how answers will be used, so that people can give informed consent. The first questions are easy, engaging and clearly about the topic promised; the main topics follow, each from general to specific; sensitive and demographic questions come near the end, unless one is needed earlier to decide who should answer what. Krosnick and Presser give the same advice and add that related questions belong together.

A screening question decides whether someone belongs in the survey at all, and so comes first. A survey of a city’s ready-to-drink coffee buyers would open by asking whether the person has bought a can in the last three months and thank everyone else for their time. Lantern needed no screening question, because everyone on its list was in scope; the longer screener used to recruit focus groups (see Qualitative research) does the same job before a study begins.

Routing (skip logic) then sends each person only to the questions that apply to them. A web survey tool does this automatically; a paper or telephone questionnaire needs written instructions. Test every path before fieldwork, because a routing error silently removes people from a question.

Length is a cost paid by the respondent. Krosnick and Presser note that fatigue can lower the quality of answers later in a self-administered questionnaire, and one symptom is straight-lining: the same answer to every item in a grid. Cut every question that fails the earn-its-place test, put the essential questions first, and tell people honestly how long the survey takes.

Pretesting

Pew calls pretesting an essential step in questionnaire design. The U.S. Census Bureau’s standard for its own surveys requires at least one method in which real respondents take part, and lists several. In a cognitive interview, one person works through a draft questionnaire and describes their thoughts, either aloud as they answer (thinking aloud) or afterwards; the interviewer probes (“What did ‘ready-to-drink’ mean to you?”) and asks the person to repeat questions in their own words. Behaviour coding records, question by question, where interviewers and respondents run into trouble; respondent debriefing asks follow-up questions after a field test.

The Bureau says that a review by survey experts, useful as it is, does not in general satisfy the requirement, because no respondent is involved; it may stand in for respondent pretesting only as a last resort, when time is extremely short. A small field test (a pretest or pilot) then checks the whole process: the invitation, the routing, the timing and the data file the tool produces.

Fictional case

Ana’s review cut the draft from twelve questions to seven: she dropped three that no objective needed and merged two pairs. On 26 and 27 January 2027 she ran cognitive interviews with five customers in the shop. Two of the five weren’t sure whether “ready-to-drink” meant a can or a drink made in a café, so Q2 now names the can and its size. Nobody could say which can size they would prefer without seeing the cans, so that question went to the focus groups, leaving six.

Mode, in outline

The survey mode is how questions reach people and answers come back. OpenStax’s Principles of Marketing compares four (mailed, telephone, in-person and electronic surveys) and notes, for example, that electronic surveys are cheaper and quicker but tend to get low response rates. The mode also changes the answers, as the sections above showed.

Common mistakes

  • “One more question costs nothing.”

    Every question costs the respondent time and attention. Longer questionnaires invite satisficing and straight-lining, and lower the quality of the answers that matter. If you can’t name the analysis a question feeds and the decision it could change, cut it.

  • “More scale points always measure better.”

    Krosnick and Presser’s review finds reliability lower on scales of two or three points, but the gain levels off after about seven, and especially long scales compromise validity: seven-point scales are probably optimal in many instances, though their fit to the telephone is less certain. Pew advises about four or five options for most closed questions, especially on the phone, where people must hold every option in mind. Choose the number of points for the mode, and label every one.

  • “Offering ‘don’t know’ and ‘other’ always improves the data.”

    Evidence reviewed by Krosnick and Presser suggests “don’t know” filters do not improve opinion measurement and that “other” boxes are rarely used. Offer “don’t know” where not knowing is a real answer, and build an exhaustive list from a pilot rather than relying on “other”.

  • “We can work out the averages later.”

    The level of measurement is fixed when the question is written. Ask age or spending in bands and no later analysis can recover a mean; ask a 1–5 agreement item and its mean is a convention, not an amount. Decide the analysis, then write the question.

  • “Randomise every list.”

    Randomise unordered lists, such as reasons or routes, so that no option is always first. Keep ordered scales in order: shuffling “very satisfied … very dissatisfied” makes them unreadable. To spread primacy or recency on a scale, reverse it for half the sample.

  • “A pretest just checks that the link works.”

    Checking the link and the routing is part of it, but the main job is finding out what people think each question means. Cognitive interviews with a handful of real respondents catch ambiguous words, unanswerable questions and missing options that nobody inside the business will notice.

In practice

In a research agency the questionnaire is a contract between the researcher and the client. Before fieldwork the two agree an analysis plan that lists, for every question, the objective it answers and the tables it will produce; questions with no line in the plan are cut. Standard questions such as the Net Promoter question are used in their published wording, or in one fixed close variant, so that results can be compared over time and with other studies, and anything tracked keeps its wording, place and mode from one wave to the next.

Fictional case

Ana’s sign-off sheet for the February questionnaire had five lines: every question is tied to an objective in the brief and to a planned table; Q1 is a close variant of Reichheld’s recommend question (“How likely are you…” for “How likely is it that you would…”) and will be asked in exactly these words, in the same place, next year; Q2 describes the product and its price neutrally on a balanced scale; the options to Q3, Q4 and Q5 are exhaustive and don’t overlap, and Q4’s first four routes are shown in a random order, with “Search or other” always last; and five customers have taken it in cognitive interviews. The web version has also been tested on a phone and a laptop.

The questionnaire also decides what the analysis can never show. Q2 measures what people say they would do, not what they will do, and the survey says nothing about people who are not on Lantern’s list. Those limits belong to sampling and survey error, the next two lessons. What good design can promise is narrower and still valuable: that each answer means the same thing to every respondent and to the person who reads the results.

The same discipline applies well beyond customer surveys. A software firm writing an onboarding survey, a council consulting residents and a hospital asking patients about their care all face the same choices: which questions earn their place, which formats give the measures they need, and which wording, order and mode let people answer honestly. Pew’s guidance and Krosnick and Presser’s review are written for social research, but the evidence they summarise is about how people answer questions, and that doesn’t change with the client.

Summary

  • Start from the research objectives: every question needs an objective, a planned analysis and a decision it could change. Ask only what you can’t learn another way.
  • Open questions collect reasons, ideas and amounts in people’s own words; closed questions (dichotomous, multiple choice, ranking, rating) are quicker to answer and analyse, but their lists shape the answers.
  • Standard scales: Likert items and scales (reverse-code opposite items), semantic differentials, numeric ratings, purchase-intent scales and the Net Promoter question (0–10; promoters 9–10, passives 7–8, detractors 0–6).
  • The question fixes the level of measurement: ask for a number if you need a mean, and decide the analysis before the wording.
  • Options must be mutually exclusive and exhaustive. Avoid leading, loaded, double-barrelled, ambiguous, double-negative and jargon-laden wording, long recall periods and sensitive questions nobody needs.
  • Acquiescence, social desirability, extreme and midpoint responding and satisficing bias answers; question order and option order (primacy on screens, recency on the phone) do too. Design against them.
  • Reliability is consistency (test–retest; internal consistency, by Cronbach’s alpha = k ÷ (k − 1) × (1 − Σ item variances ÷ total variance), usually 0.70 to 0.90). Measurement validity is measuring the right thing (content, construct, criterion). Reliable is not the same as valid.
  • Shape the flow (easy opener, general to specific, screening first, routing, sensitive and demographic questions last), pretest with cognitive interviews, and keep tracked questions in the same mode: web shows primacy; interviewers bring acquiescence and social desirability.

Terms introduced

Questionnaire
A fixed set of questions asked in the same words and order of every respondent, with answers recorded in a fixed form so that they can be counted and compared.
Open question
A survey question that lets respondents answer in their own words or with their own number, rather than choosing from a list.
Closed question
A survey question that asks respondents to choose from a fixed list of answers.
Dichotomous question
A closed question with exactly two possible answers, such as yes or no.
Multiple-choice question
A closed question that offers a list of answers and asks for one of them, or for all that apply.
Ranking question
A question that asks respondents to put a set of options in order, such as from most to least important. The ranks are ordinal and say nothing about how far apart the options are.
Rating scale
A question format in which respondents place a judgement on a set of ordered points, such as five points from very dissatisfied to very satisfied, or 0 to 10.
Likert item
A statement answered on ordered points of agreement, usually five from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree”.
Likert scale
A score built by averaging or summing several Likert items that measure one attitude, after reverse-coding any items worded in the opposite direction.
Semantic differential
A rating format with two opposite adjectives at the ends of a row of points, usually seven, on which respondents place a brand, product or idea.
Purchase-intent scale
A question about how likely respondents are to buy a described product, on verbal points such as “definitely would buy”, “probably would buy”, “might or might not”, “probably would not” and “definitely would not”.
Net Promoter Score
A loyalty measure from answers to “How likely is it that you would recommend [company] to a friend or colleague?” on a 0 to 10 scale: the percentage of promoters (9–10) minus the percentage of detractors (0–6), from −100 to +100 (Reichheld, 2003).
Leading question
A question whose wording signals the answer the asker expects or wants, such as “Don’t you agree that…?”.
Loaded question
A question that contains an assumption, or emotive wording, that the respondent must accept in order to answer, such as “How excited are you to try…?”.
Double-barrelled question
A question that asks about two things at once, so that someone who feels differently about each can’t answer it accurately.
Double negative
Wording with two negatives (“not unreasonable”, “oppose not allowing”) that respondents must untangle before they can answer, which leads to mistakes.
Response style
A tendency to answer survey questions in a particular way regardless of their content, such as agreeing, choosing the end points or choosing the middle.
Acquiescence
The tendency to agree with statements, or to say yes, regardless of their content. It biases agree/disagree, true/false and yes/no questions.
Social desirability bias
Measurement bias that arises when respondents give the answer that makes them look good rather than the true one, such as overstating recycling or understating drinking. It is stronger when an interviewer is present.
Satisficing
Answering a survey question with mental short cuts instead of fully understanding it, recalling what is relevant and forming a judgement; it grows with the difficulty and length of a questionnaire (Krosnick and Presser).
Order effect
A change in the answers to a question caused by the questions asked before it, which provide a context for it.
Primacy effect
The tendency to choose options near the start of a list, most common when respondents read the list themselves.
Recency effect
The tendency to choose options near the end of a list, most common when respondents hear the list read aloud.
Screening question
A question at the start of a survey that decides whether a person is in the population the survey is about, and so whether they continue.
Routing
Instructions or programmed rules (skip logic) that send each respondent only to the questions that apply to them, based on their earlier answers.
Pretest
A trial of a questionnaire with people like the intended respondents before fieldwork, to find questions they misunderstand or can’t answer and to check the routing, timing and data.
Cognitive interview
A one-to-one pretest in which a respondent works through a draft questionnaire and describes their thinking, aloud as they answer or afterwards, while the interviewer probes what each question meant to them.
Reliability
The consistency of a measure: whether it gives the same result when what it measures hasn’t changed, across repeated measurements, across the items of a scale or across observers.
Measurement validity
The extent to which a measure captures what it is meant to measure, supported by content, construct and criterion evidence. Distinct from validity in the data-quality sense.
Test–retest reliability
The consistency of a measure over time, assessed by giving the same measure to the same people twice, a short time apart, and correlating the two sets of scores.
Internal consistency
The extent to which the items of a multi-item scale move together, so that they can be treated as measuring one thing; usually summarised by Cronbach’s alpha.
Cronbach’s alpha
A measure of internal consistency: k ÷ (k − 1) × (1 − the sum of the k item variances ÷ the variance of the total score). It runs up to 1; values from about 0.70 to 0.90 are the usual target, and it assumes the items measure one thing (Cronbach, 1951).
Content validity
The extent to which a measure’s items cover every part of the idea it is meant to measure, as defined; judged by comparing the items with the definition. Face validity, whether a measure looks right on the surface, is weaker evidence.
Construct validity
Evidence that a measure’s scores relate to other measures as they should: strongly to other measures of the same idea (convergent validity) and weakly to measures of different ideas (discriminant validity).
Criterion validity
The extent to which a measure’s scores agree with a relevant outcome measured at the same time (concurrent validity) or later (predictive validity), such as stated purchase intent against later purchases.
Survey mode
The way questions reach respondents and answers come back, such as a web questionnaire, a telephone interview or a face-to-face interview. The mode can change the answers.
