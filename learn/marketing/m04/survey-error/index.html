Survey error

A margin of error measures one kind of error. Learn the others (coverage, non-response, measurement, processing) as one system, calculate response rates by AAPOR’s definitions, see why a response rate is not a measure of bias, weight responses back to the population and count what weighting costs, and read stated intentions for what they are.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Distinguish the components of total survey error (coverage, sampling, non-response, adjustment, measurement and processing) and identify the step at which each enters a survey.
  • Classify survey cases by AAPOR’s final dispositions and calculate the response rates RR1 and RR2.
  • Calculate non-response bias from the response rate and the difference between respondents and non-respondents, and explain why a response rate alone doesn’t measure bias.
  • Calculate post-stratification weights, a weighted estimate, the Kish effective sample size and the design effect of unequal weights, and explain what weighting can’t fix.
  • Evaluate stated purchase intentions against behaviour, and identify measurement and processing errors from wording, mode, memory and data handling.

Review first

  • Collecting data: sources, samples and bias — The four biases (sampling, non-response, survivorship, measurement), the response rate and the bounds non-response allows are built on here.
  • Sampling methods — Frames, coverage, the stratified estimate and the design effect are used throughout.
  • Questionnaire design — Wording, response styles, order and mode are the sources of measurement error.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana’s survey closed on 16 February 2027. Of the 2,004 people invited, 516 completed the questionnaire: 25.7%, not the 30% she had planned for, so the margin of error for a share near 50% is ±4.3 points rather than ±4. Response was far from even: about half the subscribers replied, against a fifth of the newsletter-only group. The 516 completes give a Net Promoter Score of +27.5. Ana’s question is the one this lesson answers: how far can she trust it?

Total survey error

Total survey error is the idea that a survey estimate can be wrong for many reasons, and that they should be designed against and judged together rather than one at a time. Groves and Lyberg (2010), reviewing its history, trace it back to Deming, who in 1944 listed thirteen factors affecting survey results, from non-response, sampling and interviewer effects to processing errors and timeliness, and note that the term is not tightly defined: different authors include different components. A widely used version, from Groves and colleagues’ textbook Survey Methodology (2004), follows the two paths an estimate travels.

On the representation side, coverage error arises from the gap between the target population and the frame (undercoverage and overcoverage, from Sampling methods); sampling error from studying a sample rather than everyone; non-response error from the difference between those sampled and those who answer; and adjustment error from weighting that corrects the wrong things. On the measurement side, a question may not capture the construct at all (validity), answers may depart from the truth (measurement error), and recording, coding and editing may introduce mistakes (processing error).

B01’s four biases fit inside this map. Sampling bias and survivorship bias are coverage and selection problems on the representation side; non-response bias is the systematic part of non-response error; measurement bias is the systematic part of measurement error. What the map adds is that the errors compound, and that fixing one (a bigger sample) does nothing for another (a biased frame).

Each error has a systematic part, bias, and a random part, variance. Groves and Lyberg recall that Hansen and colleagues wrote the total as the mean squared error: MSE=bias2+variance\text{MSE} = \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance}MSE=bias2+variance, plus terms for how errors interact. They also note that most surveys routinely measure only one component, the sampling variance. That is the margin of error, and it is why a margin of ±4.3 points says nothing about whether the estimate is 10 points too high.

Response rates by the book

The American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) publishes Standard Definitions, now in its tenth edition (2023), so that response rates mean the same thing from one survey to the next. Every case drawn into the sample is given a final disposition in one of four groups: interviews (complete, or partial); eligible cases that weren’t interviewed (refusals and break-offs, non-contacts and others); cases of unknown eligibility; and cases that turn out not to be eligible, which are left out of the rates altogether.

Researchers must define in advance what counts as complete and partial. AAPOR gives examples rather than one rule: in one, more than 80% of the questions answered is a complete and 50% to 80% a partial. Ana set her rule before fieldwork (fictional): all five closed questions (Q1 to Q5) answered is a complete, whether or not the open Q6 was filled in; three or four, a partial; fewer than three, a break-off, counted with refusals.

Fewer completes than planned means a wider margin: with 516 instead of 601, ±4.31 points instead of ±4.00. That loss is visible and modest. The serious question is about the 1,488 people who didn’t complete.

Why a response rate is not a measure of bias

B01 combined respondents and non-respondents as a weighted average. Rearranged, the same identity gives the bias of the respondents’ figure: yˉr−yˉ=(1−RR)(yˉr−yˉnr)\bar{y}_r - \bar{y} = (1 - \text{RR})(\bar{y}_r - \bar{y}_{nr})yˉ​r​−yˉ​=(1−RR)(yˉ​r​−yˉ​nr​), the share who didn’t respond times the difference between respondents and non-respondents. Bias needs both: a low response rate with no difference does no harm, and a high one with a large difference still misleads.

This is why AAPOR states that response rate information alone is insufficient for determining how much non-response error exists. The U.S. National Research Council’s 2013 review of non-response cites Groves and Peytcheva’s compilation of 59 studies, which found very little correlation between non-response rates and their measures of bias, and concludes that a falling response rate means only that the potential for bias has increased. Bias belongs to each estimate, not to the survey: the same non-respondents can distort one question badly and another hardly at all.

Ana can’t see her non-respondents’ answers, but she can see who they are. Subscribers were 8.7% of the list and 17.2% of the completes; newsletter-only readers were 72.2% of the list and 57.6% of the completes. The segments also answered very differently: Net Promoter Scores of +62, +53, +41, +7 for subscribers, shop customers, workshop participants and newsletter-only readers. A respondent pool tilted towards subscribers will flatter Lantern, and that tilt is the one thing she can correct.

What weighting costs

Unequal weights make an estimate less precise, because a few heavily weighted respondents move it more. Kish’s rule of thumb measures the loss: the effective sample size is (∑wi)2÷∑wi2(\sum w_i)^2 \div \sum w_i^2(∑wi​)2÷∑wi2​, and the design effect of the weights is n÷neff=1+CV2(w)n \div n_{\text{eff}} = 1 + \text{CV}^2(w)n÷neff​=1+CV2(w), one plus the squared coefficient of variation of the weights. For Lantern’s 516 completes, neff=469.3n_{\text{eff}} = 469.3neff​=469.3 and the design effect 1.099: the weighting costs about 47 completes’ worth of precision.

Zipf and Valliant’s guide to design effects warns that Kish’s formula rests on restrictive assumptions (equal variances in every stratum) and can mislead when strata differ, or when response rates differ across groups. Both hold here. Computed directly with the stratified standard error from Sampling methods, the weighted share of promoters has a standard error of 2.15 points before the finite population correction (2.07 with it), against 2.27 from Kish’s effective sample size: because the segments differ so much in their share of promoters, stratifying wins back most of what the unequal weights cost. Use n_eff as a quick warning; use the design’s own standard error when it matters.

What weighting can’t fix

Post-stratification makes the respondents match the population on the weighting variable, and nothing else. It removes bias only to the extent that, within each weighting group, respondents answer like the non-respondents in that group: in B02’s terms, that the missing answers are MAR given the stratum. If, within every stratum, the people who liked Lantern were keener to reply, weighting by stratum reproduces their enthusiasm at the right scale.

Note: What Ana couldn’t see

In this teaching case every person on the list has a known answer to Q1, so the truth is available, though never to Ana. Among the 2,004 invited, 34.3% are promoters: 45.9% of those who completed and 30.3% of those who didn’t. The bias formula gives (1 − 0.2575) × (45.9 − 30.3) = 11.6 points, exactly the gap between 45.9% and 34.3%. Within every stratum, respondents were likelier to be promoters than non-respondents (subscribers 73% against 55%; other shop customers 62% against 43%; workshop participants 59% against 57%; newsletter only 31% against 25%). So the weighted score, +20.4, still sits far above the list’s true Net Promoter Score of about +8.

The same respondents distort different questions by different amounts. For Q2, weighting moves the share who would definitely or probably buy Cold Brew from 42.2% to 38.2%, within about a point of the list’s true 39.3%: willingness to try Cold Brew had little to do with who replied, once the strata were balanced. Recommending Lantern had a great deal to do with it. Run the two samplers below with non-response and weighting switched on and compare.

Weighting has the same limits on a larger scale. Pew Research Center weighted large online opt-in samples on demographic and political variables by several methods: average estimated bias across 24 benchmarks fell from 8.4 points unweighted to no lower than about 6 points, and in Pew’s words, “even the most effective adjustment procedures were unable to remove most of the bias.” Which variables went into the weights mattered more than the method used.

What helps is evidence about the non-respondents and better variables to weight on. Follow up a random sample of non-respondents by another mode, as B01’s hotel did, and use their answers to estimate the gap directly. Compare respondents with non-respondents on everything the frame already records: for Lantern, order history, subscription length and sign-up channel. Weight on the variables that predict both replying and the answer. And raise response where it is lowest, with reminders, a shorter questionnaire or a small incentive, while remembering that a higher rate helps only if it brings in people unlike those already responding.

Measurement error: wording, mode and memory

On the other side of the map, answers can be wrong however representative the respondents. Questionnaire design covered the main sources: wording that leads, loads or confuses; response styles such as acquiescence and social desirability; order effects; and the mode, which changes how social pressures and option orders work. Memory adds two more. People forget, and they misplace events in time, pulling them into or out of the period asked about, which is why Q3 asks about last week, not last month.

Item non-response, a respondent skipping some questions, sits between the two sides: the person answered the survey but not the question. It is distinct from unit non-response, not answering at all, and is handled in the analysis (module 4) by reporting bases and coding missing answers rather than treating blanks as zeros.

Stated intentions are not purchases

Purchase-intent questions are measurement with a special problem: they ask people to predict their own future behaviour. Morwitz, Steckel and Gupta’s (2007) analysis of studies relating intentions to later purchases found intentions more strongly related to purchases for existing products than for new ones, for durable goods than for non-durables, over short horizons than long ones, and for specific brands or models than for whole categories. Sun and Morwitz (2010) describe respondents who exaggerate their demand, producing a positive intention bias that is likely in new-product surveys; in one of their data sets, 15.3% of respondents said they would buy a personal computer within a year, but only 5.7% did.

Lantern’s Q2 is about a new, non-durable product, asked with a three-month horizon and a named brand: two conditions that weaken the link and two that help. The weighted 38.2% who say they would definitely or probably buy Cold Brew is a measure of interest among Lantern’s contacts, not a forecast of buyers. Module 3’s market sizing turns intent into an estimate of purchases only with an explicit conversion assumption, and tests how much the answer depends on it.

Processing error

Errors also enter after the answers arrive. A partial is counted as a complete; a stratum code is attached to the wrong respondent when the list is merged with the survey export; a scale is keyed in reverse; weights are applied to the wrong rows; an open answer is coded by one person differently from another. Most of these are systematic: they push estimates one way rather than averaging out. The defences are routine and dull: a written data plan, checks that weighted totals reproduce the frame’s, a second coder for open answers with inter-coder agreement checked, and keeping the raw export untouched alongside every edited version.

Common mistakes

  • “Our 26% response rate means the results are unreliable; at 60% they would be fine.”

    A response rate measures room for bias, not bias. AAPOR states that response rates alone are insufficient to determine non-response error, and Groves and Peytcheva’s 59 studies found little correlation between the two. What matters is whether non-respondents differ on what you are measuring.

  • “Weighting removes non-response bias.”

    Weighting corrects the mix of respondents on the weighting variables only. Lantern’s weighted NPS, +20.4, still overstates the truth because within every segment promoters replied more readily. Pew found that even the best adjustments left most of the bias in opt-in samples.

  • “The margin of error is the error.”

    It reflects sampling variance alone. Coverage gaps, non-response, wording, mode and processing mistakes can each be larger, and none of them shrinks as the sample grows.

  • “Response rate = replies ÷ invitations, however you count.”

    Whether partials count (RR2) or not (RR1), and what happens to bounced invitations and ineligible cases, change the figure. Use AAPOR’s definitions, set your complete and partial rules in advance, and say which rate you report.

  • “Weights are free.”

    Unequal weights reduce precision: Lantern’s 516 completes carry about the information of 469 equally weighted ones by Kish’s rule. Very large weights on a few respondents are a warning sign; collapse small groups or trim extreme weights, and say so.

  • “38% said they would buy, so 38% will buy.”

    Stated intentions overstate purchases, especially for new products (Sun and Morwitz), and predict less well for new, non-durable products and long horizons (Morwitz, Steckel and Gupta). Treat intent as a signal of interest and apply an explicit, tested conversion assumption when sizing a market.

In practice

Professional surveys publish their quality, not just their results. AAPOR’s definitions exist so that a reader can compare one survey’s response rate with another’s; many statistics offices publish quality reports listing response rates, weighting variables and known coverage gaps; and good market research reports carry a method statement with the frame, the dates, the mode, the dispositions, the weighting and the margin, so that a reader can judge the errors the numbers can’t show.

The most useful habit is a non-response analysis. Before reporting, compare respondents with non-respondents on everything the frame records. A retailer surveying loyalty-card holders can compare the two groups’ spending, visit frequency and tenure from its own transaction data; if respondents spend 40% more, every satisfaction figure is suspect, and the transaction variables become candidates for weighting. When a decision is large, budget for a follow-up of a random sample of non-respondents by a different mode.

Fictional case

Ana’s survey note to herself, filed with the results: “Invited 2,004; RR1 25.7%, RR2 28.5%; response ranged from 21% (newsletter only) to 54% (workshop participants). Results weighted to the list’s four segments; effective sample 469. Weighted NPS +20.4, probably an upper estimate because keener customers are likelier to reply. Cold Brew intent is interest, not a forecast. Next time: compare respondents’ and non-respondents’ order histories, weight on them if they differ, and phone a random 50 non-respondents.”

None of this makes a survey worthless. It makes its claims match its evidence. A weighted score with an honest warning, a response rate stated by a standard definition and an intent figure treated as interest will survive scrutiny; a raw +27.5 presented as “what our customers think” will not.

Summary

  • Total survey error: coverage, sampling, non-response and adjustment errors on the representation side; validity, measurement and processing errors on the measurement side. The margin of error measures only sampling variance; MSE = bias² + variance.
  • AAPOR’s final dispositions: completes and partials; refusals and break-offs, non-contacts and others; unknown eligibility; not eligible (excluded). RR1 = I ÷ all eligible and unknown cases; RR2 adds partials to the numerator. Say which rate you report.
  • Non-response bias = (1 − RR) × (respondents − non-respondents). A low rate allows bias; it doesn’t prove it, and a high rate doesn’t prevent it. Bias differs from question to question.
  • Post-stratification weights w_h = N_h ÷ n_h make the respondents match the population on the weighting variables; the weighted estimate equals the stratified estimate.
  • Weighting costs precision: Kish’s n_eff = (Σw)² ÷ Σw², deff = 1 + CV²(w), a rule of thumb. It can’t fix differences between respondents and non-respondents within a weighting group.
  • Stated purchase intentions tend to overstate buying, especially for new products, and relate less closely to purchases for new products, non-durables, long horizons and whole categories. Treat them as interest, not forecasts.
  • Processing errors (coding, merging, keying, weighting the wrong rows) are prevented by a data plan, checks against the frame and second coders.

Terms introduced

Total survey error
The combined error in a survey estimate from every source (coverage, sampling, non-response, adjustment, measurement and processing), considered together when designing and judging a survey.
Coverage error
Error in a survey estimate that arises because the sampling frame misses part of the target population (undercoverage) or includes units outside it (overcoverage), and those units differ on what is measured.
Measurement error
The difference between the answer a respondent gives and the true value, from wording, response styles, order, mode or memory. Its systematic part is measurement bias.
Processing error
Error introduced after answers are collected, in recording, coding, editing, merging or weighting the data.
Mean squared error
The average squared distance between an estimate and the true value over repetitions of a survey: bias squared plus variance.
Final disposition
The outcome code given to every case in a survey sample at the end of fieldwork, in AAPOR’s four groups: interview (complete or partial), eligible non-interview, unknown eligibility, and not eligible.
Break-off
A respondent who starts a questionnaire but stops before answering enough questions to count as a partial; AAPOR counts break-offs with refusals.
RR1
AAPOR’s Response Rate 1: complete interviews divided by all eligible cases and cases of unknown eligibility, I ÷ ((I + P) + (R + NC + O) + U).
RR2
AAPOR’s Response Rate 2: complete and partial interviews divided by all eligible cases and cases of unknown eligibility, (I + P) ÷ ((I + P) + (R + NC + O) + U).
Unit non-response
A sampled person or unit giving no usable answers at all, so that it is missing from the respondents.
Item non-response
A respondent leaving particular questions unanswered while answering the rest of the survey.
Survey weight
The number of population members a respondent stands for in an estimate, used so that weighted respondents reproduce the population’s make-up.
Post-stratification
Weighting respondents after a survey so that each group’s weighted total matches its known population count: each respondent in group h gets the weight N_h ÷ n_h.
