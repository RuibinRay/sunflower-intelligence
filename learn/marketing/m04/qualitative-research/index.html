Qualitative research

Surveys count; qualitative research explains. Learn when depth interviews, focus groups and observation fit, how to recruit with a screener and write a discussion guide, how many interviews are enough, and how to code transcripts into themes with a codebook, check that two coders agree and report what people said without turning it into numbers it can’t support.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain when qualitative research fits a research objective and what it can’t show, such as the share of a market that holds a view.
  • Compare depth interviews, focus groups and observation, including group dynamics such as dominant voices and the wish to look good.
  • Design a screener and a funnel-shaped discussion guide with open questions and neutral probes.
  • Use evidence on saturation to judge how many interviews or groups a study needs.
  • Apply a codebook to transcripts, calculate percent agreement and Cohen’s kappa between two coders, and report themes and quotations honestly.

Review first

  • The research process — Qualitative research is the main tool of exploratory designs, and the research codes govern consent and recording.
  • Situation analysis — Lantern’s twelve client interviews, and their survivorship problem, were first analysed there.
  • Conditional sums, counts and averages — The codebook worksheet counts codes with COUNTIFS.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

By March 2027 the February survey has closed, and Ana can count how many people on her list say they would buy Cold Brew. The numbers can’t tell her why. Do subscribers like the idea because they like Lantern’s coffee, or because a can is convenient? Would someone who buys ready-to-drink coffee on the way to work notice a new brand at all? For these questions she plans two 90-minute focus groups with a hired moderator: 8 subscribers on 10 March 2027, and 7 people who buy ready-to-drink coffee at least once a month but have never bought from Lantern on 12 March 2027.

Qualitative research collects words, actions and images from a small number of deliberately chosen people, to understand what they think, how they talk about it and why. Its output is themes, explanations and the language customers use. Quantitative research, such as a survey of a planned sample or an analysis of sales records, measures how many, how much and how often, and, from a probability sample, can estimate a population’s figures with a margin of error. The two answer different questions, and good projects use both.

When qualitative research fits

  • Exploring a problem you don’t yet understand well enough to ask precise questions about.
  • Explaining a result: why a survey score is low, why customers left, why a launch disappointed.
  • Generating hypotheses for a survey or an experiment to test, and the words to use in its questions.
  • Testing early reactions to a concept, a name, a pack or an advertisement before money is spent on it.
  • Understanding a process, such as how a household chooses a supplier or how a school chooses a caterer.

It doesn’t fit questions about how many. Fifteen people chosen to hold particular views can’t say what share of a city’s shoppers would buy a can, however vivid their answers. When the objective says “estimate”, the design is descriptive; qualitative work can only prepare for it or explain its result.

Key idea: Themes, not shares

Qualitative findings say what views exist, how people express them and why they hold them. They don’t say how common those views are. “Several participants said the price was high” is a finding; “40% think the price is too high” from a focus group is not.

Depth interviews and focus groups

A depth interview is a one-to-one conversation, often lasting 30 to 90 minutes, in which an interviewer follows a guide but is free to follow the participant wherever the answers lead. A focus group is a discussion of a topic by a small group of people who meet the study’s requirements, led by a moderator; OpenStax puts the usual size at 8 to 12 people. Lantern’s groups, of 8 and 7, are at the small end of that range or just below it, which gives each participant more time.

The group is a focus group’s strength and its risk. One person’s remark reminds another of something; disagreement sharpens reasons. But OpenStax warns that a group can be overwhelmed by one or two aggressive personalities and that the format can discourage reserved people from speaking up. Two further risks are well known: quieter members may go along with what has been said, and people tend to give the answers that make them look good in front of strangers, a tendency the questionnaire-design lesson meets again as a response style. A skilled moderator asks everyone for a first reaction on paper before the discussion, goes round the room, and invites the quiet participants by name.

Observation, ethnography and online communities

People can’t always say what they do, so sometimes it is better to watch. OpenStax defines observational research as collecting data based on the actions of those observed: shoppers at a chilled-drinks shelf, say, timed as they look, pick up and read cans, with the store’s permission. Ethnography goes further: the researcher spends time in people’s own settings, such as a commuter’s morning or a family’s breakfast, to understand behaviour in its context. Online research communities, in which a recruited group of customers takes part in discussions, diaries and small tasks over weeks or months, carry the same ideas onto a private website.

In depth

Recruiting the right people

Qualitative participants are chosen on purpose, not at random: the study needs the kinds of people whose views matter to the decision. Lantern needs two kinds, its own subscribers and the much larger group of ready-to-drink buyers who have never bought from it, and puts each in its own group so that neither shapes the other’s discussion. Ana can invite subscribers from her list; the second group needs an agency, which finds people with a screener: a short questionnaire that decides whether a person qualifies for a study, and for which group, before they are invited.

The screener for group B (fictional) shows what a screener does:

  1. Age: 18 or over (adults only, so no parental consent is needed).
  2. Bought ready-to-drink coffee at least once in the past month: must say yes.
  3. Which of these coffee brands have you bought in the past year? (Lantern appears in a list of eight, so the screener doesn’t reveal who is asking.) Anyone who has bought Lantern is excluded.
  4. Works, or has a close family member who works, in marketing, market research or the coffee or drinks trade: excluded.
  5. Took part in a group discussion or paid interview in the past six months: excluded, to avoid people who take part for a living.
  6. Where they usually buy ready-to-drink coffee (supermarket, convenience store or petrol station, specialty grocer): the agency recruits a mix.

Recruits are told at recruitment, and again at the start of the session, that it will be recorded and that Lantern’s team may watch; the MRS Code of Conduct (rule 29) requires participants to be informed about any recording, monitoring or observation at recruitment and at the beginning of data collection. They are paid a thank-you for their time, and the agency recruits one or two more than needed, because some people won’t come.

The discussion guide

A discussion guide is the moderator’s plan for an interview or group: the topics in order, the main questions, any tasks, and the time for each. Most guides follow a funnel, from the general to the specific, so that participants describe their own habits and words before they see anything the client has made. In Lantern’s guide the can appears only after 40 minutes, once everyone has described how and where they buy coffee and which canned brands they know.

The questions are open (“tell me about…”, “how do you decide…”) and the follow-ups are probes: neutral prompts that ask for more without suggesting an answer, such as “What makes you say that?”, “Can you give me an example?” and “What else?” A leading question (“Wouldn’t a stylish can make you more likely to buy it?”) tells participants what the client hopes to hear, and in a group one agreeable answer can carry the room.

How many interviews or groups?

Qualitative samples are sized by saturation: the point at which further interviews or groups add no new themes. Two studies give it numbers. Guest, Bunce and Johnson (2006) coded 60 in-depth interviews with women in Ghana and Nigeria in rounds of six and found that saturation occurred within the first twelve interviews, with the basic elements of the main themes present as early as six. They warned that twelve will likely not be enough if the group is relatively heterogeneous or the topic diffuse.

Hennink and Kaiser (2022) reviewed 23 articles that tested saturation, 17 of them with empirical data, and found that those studies reached it within 9 to 17 interviews or 4 to 8 focus group discussions, mainly where the participants were similar to one another and the objectives narrow. The practical rule follows: plan enough interviews or groups for each kind of person, watch how many new codes each one adds, and stop when a run of them adds nothing.

By either standard, Lantern’s design is thin: one group of each kind can’t show that the themes are complete. Its findings are a first map, good for wording the next survey’s questions and for hypotheses, not a full account of what ready-to-drink buyers think. M01’s 12 catering-client interviews match the twelve within which Guest and colleagues found saturation in their fairly similar group, but they have a different weakness: every interviewee was a current client.

From transcripts to themes

Analysis starts from transcripts, word-for-word written records of each recording. Coding labels each passage with a code, a short name for what it is about, so that passages on the same idea can be collected and compared. A codebook lists the codes with a definition, a rule for when to use each and when not, and an example, so that different coders, or the same coder on different days, code alike. The method most researchers follow is thematic analysis, set out by Braun and Clarke (2006) in six phases: familiarising yourself with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and producing the report. The phases loop: reviewing the themes often sends the analyst back to the transcripts, and the codebook changes as the analysis develops. A theme is a pattern of meaning across the data, not just a heading; “price” becomes a theme when the analyst can say what people mean by it, for example that paying a dollar more than for the brand they buy now needs a reason.

Counting codes honestly

Counting codes shows where the talk went. In Lantern’s 20 coded quotations, 4 are about price and 4 about where to buy, and 4 different participants raised price. But the 20 are passages Ana selected and coded from three hours of talk, so counts describe her selection, not a population. The moderator’s guide decided how long each topic was discussed, talkative participants supply more quotations, and the 15 participants were chosen, not sampled, so “4 of 20 quotations” is not “20% of buyers”.

Report counts, if at all, as what they are (“price came up in both groups, raised by four participants”), and keep percentages for descriptive research. The interesting differences are often between groups: in Lantern’s, subscribers talked about matching Lantern’s coffee and its compostable bags, while the non-customers talked about noticing an unknown brand on a shelf and paying more than they pay now.

Do two coders agree?

Inter-coder agreement is the extent to which two people coding the same material independently assign the same codes. The simplest measure is percent agreement: Ana and a freelance researcher, coding separately, agreed on 18 of the 20 quotations, 90%. But two coders would agree on some quotations by chance, and more often when one code dominates.

Each is a sign that the codebook is unclear, so the coders discuss them and add a rule: a free can offered so people can taste it belongs under Taste, and a remark about what the can is made of belongs under Sustainability. Reporting what people said

  • Choose quotations that represent a theme, and say how widely it was shared and by whom (“raised in both groups”).
  • Include the views that cut against the main pattern; a report of only the agreeable quotations misleads.
  • Keep the words as spoken; mark any cut with an ellipsis and never join remarks from different people.
  • Identify participants only by codes such as A3 or B2, and leave out any detail that could identify them.
  • State the design next to the findings: who took part, how they were recruited and what the method can’t show.

These are the codes’ honest-reporting rules applied to words: the ICC/ESOMAR code requires that published results are not misleading, and the MRS code that findings are clearly supported by the data.

Common mistakes

  • “Most of the focus group liked it, so most customers will.”

    A focus group is a handful of people chosen on purpose and talking to one another, so neither its make-up nor its answers stand for a population. Use it to find reasons and hypotheses, and measure how common they are with a descriptive survey.

  • “Four of the twenty quotations were about price: 20%.”

    Code counts describe the transcripts: how long the guide spent on each topic and who talked most. They can show where the discussion went, but turning them into percentages invites readers to treat them as shares of a market.

  • “Two groups were enough; we heard the same things twice.”

    Saturation is judged within each kind of participant, by whether new interviews or groups add new themes. Evidence suggests 9 to 17 interviews or 4 to 8 groups for fairly similar participants and narrow objectives; one group per kind can’t show saturation.

  • “Our coders agree 90% of the time, so the codes are reliable.”

    Percent agreement includes agreement by chance, which is high when one code dominates. Report Cohen’s kappa as well, and read the disagreements: they show where the codebook needs a clearer rule.

  • “Ask them straight out whether they’d buy our cold brew.”

    Opening with the client’s product, or with a leading question, tells participants what you want to hear and stops them describing their own habits in their own words. Funnel from the general to the specific, ask open questions and probe neutrally.

  • “Pick the best quotes for the report.”

    Choosing only the most quotable or the most flattering remarks misleads as surely as a truncated chart. Pick quotations that represent each theme, say how widely it was shared, include the dissenting views, and never join remarks from different people.

In practice

Fictional case

A fictional regional building society is redesigning how first-time buyers apply for a mortgage online. Its insight manager runs the qualitative stage before any survey.

She chooses depth interviews. Borrowing is private, and couples often disagree about it, so she runs twelve one-to-one video interviews with people who started an application in the last three months, six who finished it and six who gave up. A screener, run by a recruitment agency, checks that each person applied for a first home and doesn’t work in financial services; recruits are told at the outset that the interviews will be recorded and that a designer from the society may watch.

Her guide is a funnel. It starts with the search for a home, moves to how they found out about mortgages and only then to the society’s application, which participants share on screen and talk through. The probes are neutral: “What were you expecting to happen there?” “What did you do next?”

She codes as she goes. After each interview a colleague and she code the transcript; by the ninth, no new codes appear, and the last three add nothing, so she stops at twelve. A second coder recodes four transcripts: percent agreement is 88%, but kappa is 0.71, and their disagreements reveal one muddled code (“confusing” covered both jargon and missing information), which they split into two.

She reports themes, not percentages. The strongest theme is that applicants who gave up hit the request for documents they didn’t have to hand, with no way to save the application and return. She reports it with three quotations, one from a participant who didn’t mind the documents at all, and recommends a quick survey of all recent applicants to measure how many abandoned at that step, and an A/B test of a “save and finish later” button to see whether it works. Qualitative work found the problem; quantitative work will size it and test the fix.

Summary

  • Qualitative research explains: it finds the views people hold, the words they use and why. It can’t estimate how common a view is; that needs descriptive research.
  • Depth interviews suit personal, sensitive or complex topics; focus groups suit reactions and shared language but bring dominant voices, conformity and the wish to look good; observation and ethnography show behaviour people can’t report.
  • Participants are chosen on purpose with a screener, which also excludes people from the industry and those who take part too often; they are told about recording when recruited.
  • A discussion guide funnels from the general to the specific, with open questions and neutral probes; the client’s product comes late.
  • Size samples by saturation: studies suggest 9 to 17 interviews or 4 to 8 groups for fairly similar participants and narrow objectives, and more for varied groups.
  • Analyse with a codebook and thematic analysis. Counts of codes describe transcripts, not populations.
  • Check coding with percent agreement and Cohen’s kappa, κ = (p_o − p_e) ÷ (1 − p_e), and use the disagreements to improve the codebook. Report representative quotations, dissent included, with participants identified only by codes.

Terms introduced

Qualitative research
Research that collects words, actions and images from a small number of deliberately chosen people, to understand what they think, how they talk about it and why. Its output is themes and explanations, not estimates for a population.
Quantitative research
Research that measures how many, how much and how often, such as a survey of a planned sample or an analysis of records, and, from a probability sample, can estimate a population’s figures with a margin of error.
Depth interview
A one-to-one research conversation, often 30 to 90 minutes long, in which the interviewer follows a guide but is free to follow the participant’s answers.
Focus group
A discussion of a topic by a small group of people who meet a study’s requirements, led by a moderator who follows a discussion guide.
Ethnography
Research in which the researcher spends time in people’s own settings, observing and talking with them, to understand behaviour in its context.
Screener
A short questionnaire used before a study to decide whether a person qualifies to take part, and in which group.
Discussion guide
The moderator’s or interviewer’s plan for a qualitative session: the topics in order, the main questions, any tasks and the time for each.
Probe
A neutral follow-up prompt in an interview or group, such as “What makes you say that?”, that asks for more without suggesting an answer.
Saturation
In qualitative research, the point at which further interviews or groups add no new themes or codes.
Coding
In qualitative analysis, labelling passages of transcripts with codes, short names for what each passage is about, so that passages on the same idea can be collected and compared.
Codebook
The list of codes used in a qualitative analysis, each with a definition, rules for when to use it and when not, and an example.
Thematic analysis
A method for finding, reviewing and reporting patterns of meaning (themes) in qualitative data, set out by Braun and Clarke (2006) in six phases.
Inter-coder agreement
The extent to which two or more people coding the same material independently assign the same codes, measured by percent agreement or Cohen’s kappa.
Cohen’s kappa
A measure of agreement between two coders that corrects for agreement expected by chance: (observed agreement − chance agreement) ÷ (1 − chance agreement).
