Sampling methods

Who could be in your survey decides what it can tell you. Define the target population, frame and sampling unit; choose among simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster and multistage designs and know what non-probability samples can and can’t support; allocate a stratified sample and estimate from it with a standard error; and size a sample for the subgroups you need to read.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Define the target population, sampling frame and sampling unit of a survey, and identify the undercoverage and overcoverage between them.
  • Compare simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster and multistage designs on cost, precision and the frame each needs, using the design effect.
  • Explain why convenience, judgement, quota and snowball samples, opt-in panels and river samples are not probability samples, and when they are still useful.
  • Calculate proportional and Neyman allocations of a stratified sample, by hand and in Excel, and a stratified estimate of a proportion with its standard error.
  • Plan a sample size from a margin of error, adjusted for the expected response rate and for subgroups that must be read separately.

Review first

  • Collecting data: sources, samples and bias — Populations, samples, sampling frames and the basic random and non-random designs are taken as read.
  • Sampling distributions — The standard error, the finite population correction and the four random designs in statistical terms come from there.
  • Confidence intervals — The margin of error and the sample size n = z²p(1 − p) ÷ E² are used, not re-derived.

The idea

Fictional case

B04’s project gave Ana a sample size: 601 completed questionnaires for a margin of ±4 points at 95%, which at the 30% response she expected meant 2,004 invitations. It didn’t say whom to invite. Her mailing list on 1 February 2027 held 6,000 people in four groups: 520 subscribers, 1,000 other shop customers, 150 workshop participants and 4,330 people who receive only the newsletter. This lesson is about that choice, and about what it allows her to claim.

Who the survey is about, and who it can reach

Start with the target population: the whole group a survey’s conclusions are meant to describe, defined by the research question. The sampling frame (B01) is the list or source from which the sample is actually drawn, and the sampling unit is what the frame lists and the design selects: a person, a household, a store, a sign-up batch. Ideally frame and target population coincide. In practice they overlap, and the two kinds of mismatch push results in different ways.

Undercoverage means members of the target population who are missing from the frame and so can never be chosen, however carefully the sample is drawn. Overcoverage means units on the frame that are outside the target population: they can be chosen, and unless they are screened out, their answers are mixed in with everyone else’s. Both are decided before a single invitation goes out, and neither shows up in a margin of error.

Fictional case

For Ana’s question about how customers see Lantern, the target population is everyone who has bought from Lantern. Her list holds 1,670 of them (subscribers, other shop customers and workshop participants) and misses some: anyone who has unsubscribed, catering clients who deal with Lantern by invoice and never joined the list, and guests who drank Lantern coffee at an event without signing up. It also holds 4,330 newsletter readers who have never bought. For the Cold Brew question the gap is wider still: the people who will decide Cold Brew’s fate are Harbour City’s ready-to-drink coffee buyers, and the list reaches only the small, friendly slice of them who already know Lantern.

None of this makes the list useless. It makes the February survey a survey of Lantern’s contacts, whose results describe Lantern’s contacts. Ana can read the customer strata on their own, and she must not read the Cold Brew answers as a measure of the city’s market; sizing that market is module 3’s job.

Probability and non-probability samples

A probability sample is drawn so that every unit on the frame has a known chance of selection, greater than zero, decided by a random mechanism. OpenStax’s Principles of Marketing puts it as every member having an identified likelihood of being selected. That known chance is what lets the sampling distribution from B04 apply: it is why a standard error and a margin of error mean something.

In a non-probability sample the chance that any given person ends up in the sample is unknown, because people are chosen by the researcher’s judgement, by convenience, or by their own decision to take part. Such samples can be quick, cheap and useful, but the formulas for standard errors don’t apply to them as they stand. The AAPOR task force on non-probability sampling concludes that inference from them can be valid only to the extent that the modelling assumptions behind it hold, and warns that analysing them as if they were simple random samples invalidates the standard errors.

In depth

Probability designs

B01 and B04 introduced the four basic random designs; the question for a researcher is which suits the frame, the budget and the estimates needed. A simple random sample needs only a complete list. A systematic sample takes every kth unit from a random start, which is easy from a list and spreads the sample across it, but can go wrong if the list has a cycle of the same length. Lantern’s list is in sign-up order; every third person from a random start among the first three would give 2,000 invitations.

A stratified sample needs the stratum of every unit on the frame before sampling, which Lantern’s list provides. It guarantees each group its share of the sample and is more precise than a simple random sample of the same size when the strata differ on what is measured. A cluster sample selects whole groups, such as stores, streets or sign-up batches, and takes everyone in them. It is cheaper when travelling to or listing every unit is expensive, and less precise, because members of a cluster tend to resemble each other.

A multistage sample combines these: it samples clusters, then units within them, sometimes in more than two stages. A national shopper survey might choose towns at random, then supermarkets in each chosen town, then shoppers at set times outside each chosen supermarket. It needs a list only of the units at each stage (towns, then those towns’ supermarkets), not of every shopper in the country, which is why face-to-face surveys of large populations are usually multistage.

The design effect

The design effect compares a design with simple random sampling: it is the variance of an estimate under the design divided by its variance under a simple random sample of the same size. A design effect of 2 means the design needs twice the sample to match a simple random sample’s precision; its effective sample size is n÷deffn \div \text{deff}n÷deff. Stratification usually gives a design effect a little below 1; clustering gives one above it.

For clusters of equal size b the design effect is about 1+(b−1)ρ1 + (b - 1)\rho1+(b−1)ρ, where ρ, the intraclass correlation, measures how alike members of the same cluster are (0 if no more alike than strangers, 1 if identical). Because ρ is multiplied by the cluster size, even a small one hurts: Marrowby Buses (fictional) surveying all 25 passengers on each of 40 buses, with ρ = 0.04, has a design effect of 1+24×0.04=1.961 + 24 \times 0.04 = 1.961+24×0.04=1.96, so its 1,000 passengers carry the precision of about 510 independent ones.

Orrinsby Software (fictional), worked in full below, sampled its three plans at very different rates. Pooling its 500 replies gives 29.4% who would pay for a new feature; the stratified estimate is 16.4%, with a standard error of 1.83 points. The pooled figure counts each Enterprise reply as if it stood for as many customers as a Free reply, when it stands for a tenth as many (4 customers against 40). In Excel, with the stratum sizes in B2:B4, sample sizes in C2:C4 and “yes” counts in D2:D4, the estimate is =SUMPRODUCT(B2:B4,D2:D4/C2:C4)/SUM(B2:B4).

Non-probability designs

Five kinds are common in market research, and none gives each unit a known chance of selection.

  • Convenience sample (B01): whoever is easiest to reach, such as shoppers passing a stall.
  • Judgement sample: units the researcher picks as typical or informative, such as the five stores a manager thinks represent the chain. OpenStax names it alongside convenience sampling.
  • Quota sample: interviewers fill set numbers in groups defined by easily observed traits, such as 50 women and 50 men in each age band, choosing whom to approach themselves. The AAPOR task force describes it as a form of sample matching and recalls that the quota-based polls of the 1948 U.S. presidential election went wrong. The quotas match the population on the quota traits only; within each quota, the interviewer’s choices decide who is asked.
  • Snowball sample: participants recruit further participants from among their contacts, useful for groups with no list, such as owners of a rare car. Its make-up depends on who started the chain and whom they know.
  • Online panels and river samples: an opt-in panel is a pool of volunteers who signed up to take surveys, usually for rewards; a river sample, in AAPOR’s words, is a web-based opt-in approach that recruits respondents through invitations on websites (pop-ups, banners and links). Both are self-selected: the AAPOR task force notes that participation rates in surveys of opt-in panels are often in single digits.

Quota sampling is the one most often mistaken for stratification, because both fix numbers per group. The difference is in the last step: a stratified sample draws at random within each stratum from a list, whereas a quota sample lets interviewers fill each cell with whoever they find. People who are at home, approachable or online at the right moment fill the quotas, and nothing guarantees they resemble the rest of their group.

Non-probability samples have honest uses: pretests, qualitative research, exploring hard-to-reach groups, tracking changes over time with a method held constant, and fast reads where a rough answer is enough. What they can’t do is support a margin of error on their own. Report them as what they are (“of 400 panel members who took part”), describe how they were recruited and weighted, and treat their levels with caution.

How big a sample?

B04 gave the size for a proportion: n=z2p(1−p)÷E2n = z^2 p(1 - p) \div E^2n=z2p(1−p)÷E2, with p = 0.5 when nothing is known, so ±4 points at 95% needs 601 completed questionnaires. Three adjustments turn that into a plan. When the sample is a large share of a small population, the finite population correction reduces the need to n=n0÷(1+(n0−1)÷N)n = n_0 \div (1 + (n_0 - 1) \div N)n=n0​÷(1+(n0​−1)÷N). The number of invitations is the completes needed divided by the expected response rate: 601÷0.3=2003.3601 \div 0.3 = 2003.3601÷0.3=2003.3, so 2,004. And if a subgroup must be read separately, the size must be worked out for that subgroup, not for the whole.

The last adjustment is the one most often missed. A proportional design gives each stratum its share of the completes, so small strata get few. At the planned 30% response, Lantern’s 50 workshop invitations would yield about 15 completes, a margin of about ±24 points for that group, too wide to say anything about workshop participants on their own. Reading every stratum to ±10 points needs about 97 completes each before the correction (fewer in the small strata), which means a disproportionate design and weighting afterwards.

Two warnings go with every sample size. It is a number of usable completes, so the invitations depend on a response rate that is itself a guess. And it controls sampling error only: a large sample from the wrong frame, or one in which the people who reply differ from those who don’t, is precisely wrong. Who replies is the subject of the next lesson.

Common mistakes

  • “We sampled at random, so the results describe our market.”

    A random sample describes the frame it was drawn from. Lantern’s list reaches people who already know Lantern, so its Cold Brew answers describe Lantern’s contacts, not Harbour City’s coffee buyers. Define the target population first, then check what the frame misses and what it adds.

  • “A quota sample is a stratified sample.”

    Both fix numbers per group, but a quota sample lets interviewers choose whom to approach within each quota, so selection chances are unknown. A stratified sample draws at random from a list within each stratum. Only the second supports a standard error.

  • “Pool the replies from a disproportionate sample.”

    When strata are sampled at different rates, the pooled share over-represents the oversampled strata (Orrinsby’s 29.4% against 16.4%). Combine stratum estimates with population weights, W_h = N_h ÷ N.

  • “A cluster sample of 1,000 is as good as a random sample of 1,000.”

    Members of a cluster resemble each other, so each adds less new information. With 25 per cluster and an intraclass correlation of 0.04 the design effect is about 2: the 1,000 are worth about 500 independent units.

  • “The sample size for the whole survey covers the subgroups too.”

    A margin of ±4 points overall becomes ±13 for a group with 50 completes and ±24 for one with 15. Work out the size for each group you must read separately, and oversample it if needed.

  • “With 5,000 opt-in panel replies the margin of error is ±1.4 points.”

    The formula assumes known selection chances. For an opt-in panel they are unknown, and a bias from who volunteered doesn’t shrink with size. AAPOR warns that treating such samples as simple random samples invalidates the standard errors.

In practice

Agencies choose designs by working backwards from the estimates a client needs and the money available. A retailer that needs results for each of six regions, with a margin of ±5 points in each, needs about 385 completes per region, about 2,300 in all, whatever the regions’ sizes; a client who only needs the national figure can make do with 385, spread in proportion. The frame comes next: a customer database for customers, an address-based list for households, a panel when no list exists and the budget is small.

When no frame exists, multistage designs build one stage by stage. A survey of shoppers in a chain’s 120 stores might select 20 stores at random, with chances in proportion to their footfall, then interview shoppers at random times within each, recording how many passed so that each interview can be weighted. The result is less precise than a simple random sample of the same number of shoppers, but it is affordable and still a probability design.

Fictional case

For Lantern’s next survey Ana wrote three rules into her research brief. Say whom each question is about before choosing the frame, and screen out anyone outside that population. Size the sample for every group a decision depends on, not just for the total. And never quote a margin of error for anything but the probability sample: the comments people leave on Lantern’s social media pages, however many, are a self-selected sample to be read for ideas, not counted.

A sampling plan is finished only when it states the target population, the frame and its gaps, the design and allocation, the completes needed for each group, the expected response and the invitations that follow from it. Everything after that, as the next lesson shows, depends on who actually replies.

Summary

  • Define the target population from the question. The frame’s undercoverage (people it misses) and overcoverage (units outside the target) are fixed before sampling, and no margin of error shows them.
  • A probability sample gives every unit a known, non-zero chance of selection; that is what makes a standard error meaningful. Non-probability samples (convenience, judgement, quota, snowball, opt-in panels, river) don’t, and their inference rests on modelling assumptions.
  • Simple random and systematic samples need a list; stratified samples need each unit’s stratum and usually gain precision; cluster and multistage samples cut cost and lose precision: deff ≈ 1 + (b − 1)ρ, n_eff = n ÷ deff.
  • Proportional allocation n_h = n N_h ÷ N is self-weighting; round so the parts add up. Disproportionate allocation reads small groups but requires weighting; Neyman allocation n_h ∝ N_h S_h minimises the overall standard error.
  • Stratified estimate: p̂_st = Σ W_h p̂_h; SE = √(Σ W_h²(1 − n_h ÷ N_h) p̂_h(1 − p̂_h) ÷ (n_h − 1)). Never pool a disproportionate sample.
  • Plan sample sizes for every group you must read, apply the finite population correction for small groups, and divide by the expected response rate for invitations.

Terms introduced

Target population
The whole group a survey’s conclusions are meant to describe, defined by the research question, such as all of a business’s customers or all ready-to-drink coffee buyers in a city.
Sampling unit
The thing a sampling frame lists and a design selects, such as a person, a household, a store or a cluster of people.
Undercoverage
Members of the target population who are missing from the sampling frame and so can never be selected.
Overcoverage
Units on the sampling frame that are outside the target population, such as people who have never bought on a list used to survey customers; unless screened out, their answers are mixed in.
Probability sample
A sample drawn by a random mechanism so that every unit on the frame has a known chance of selection greater than zero, which is what makes standard errors and margins of error meaningful.
Non-probability sample
A sample in which the chance that each unit is selected is unknown, because units are chosen by judgement, convenience, quotas, referral or their own decision to take part.
Multistage sample
A probability sample drawn in stages, such as towns, then stores within the chosen towns, then shoppers within the chosen stores, so that a full list is needed only at each stage.
Design effect
The variance of an estimate under a survey’s design divided by its variance under a simple random sample of the same size. Above 1 the design is less precise than simple random sampling; below 1, more.
Effective sample size
The size of a simple random sample that would give the same precision as a survey’s actual sample: the sample size divided by the design effect.
Intraclass correlation
A measure, from 0 to 1, of how alike units in the same cluster are compared with units in different clusters; it drives the design effect of a cluster sample.
Proportional allocation
Dividing a stratified sample among strata in proportion to their sizes, n_h = n × N_h ÷ N, so that every unit has the same chance of selection.
Disproportionate allocation
Sampling some strata at higher rates than others, usually to read small groups separately; estimates for the whole population must then weight the strata by their sizes.
Neyman allocation
The allocation of a stratified sample that minimises the standard error of the overall estimate for a fixed total: n_h in proportion to N_h × S_h, the stratum’s size times its standard deviation.
Stratified estimate
An estimate for the whole population built from stratum results weighted by each stratum’s share of the population, such as p̂ = Σ (N_h ÷ N) × p̂_h for a proportion.
Judgement sample
A non-probability sample of units the researcher picks as typical or informative. Also called a purposive sample.
Quota sample
A non-probability sample in which interviewers fill set numbers of respondents in groups defined by easily observed traits, such as age and sex, choosing whom to approach within each group.
Snowball sample
A non-probability sample in which participants recruit further participants from among their contacts, used for groups that have no list.
Opt-in panel
A pool of volunteers who have signed up to take surveys, usually for rewards; samples drawn from it are self-selected, not probability samples.
River sample
A web-based opt-in sample recruited through invitations placed on websites, such as pop-ups, banners and links, for a single survey or a panel.
