Insights and reporting

Turn findings into insights and recommendations by combining survey, qualitative and secondary evidence; say what research can’t show and when only an experiment will do; and write a research report with honest charts, uncertainty in plain words and respondents kept anonymous.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Distinguish a finding, an insight and a recommendation, and write an insight that explains a finding and implies an action.
  • Combine quantitative, qualitative and secondary evidence by triangulation, and judge what the combination does and doesn’t support.
  • Identify what a survey can’t show, such as causes or behaviour, and recommend a test market or A/B test where only an experiment can answer.
  • Plan a research report from executive summary to appendix, including the method details that professional codes require.
  • Design honest charts and tables, and describe uncertainty in words a manager can act on.
  • Apply confidentiality in reporting: attributions that identify no one, small-cell suppression and care with quotations.

Review first

  • Analysing survey data — Bases, cross-tabs, top-box scores, the NPS and weighted results are the findings this lesson turns into a report.
  • Qualitative research — The focus groups and the rules for reporting quotations honestly.

  • Competitor research — The shelf-price audit of Lantern Cold Brew’s rivals.
  • Interpreting results: charts, comparisons and causes — Correlation isn’t causation, confounding, and comparison groups.
  • Charts and conditional formatting — Chart choice and the truncated axis.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

By the first week of April 2027 Ana has every result her research plan asked for: the February survey, two focus groups on 10 and 12 March, a shelf-price audit at ten stores on 15–19 March, and her top-down and bottom-up sizing of the cold brew market. M02’s launch plan for Lantern Cold Brew sells 250 ml cans at $3.49 through supermarkets, natural and specialty grocers and Lantern’s own channel, with a first-year goal of about 100,000 cans. Ana must now turn four sets of results into one decision, and write it up for the people who will carry it out.

A stack of accurate figures doesn’t make a decision. That, weighted, 38.2% of the list say they would definitely or probably buy, that Summit Brew averages $3.87 on the shelf and that a bottom-up estimate comes to 48,200 cans are all findings, and none of them says what to do. This lesson is about the last step of research: turning findings into insights and recommendations, saying honestly what the research can’t show, and reporting it so that readers can check the work while respondents stay protected.

Findings, insights and recommendations

A finding is a fact the research established, stated with its base and its uncertainty: weighted to the list’s segments, 38.2% say they would definitely or probably buy Cold Brew, with 61.8% of subscribers against 31.0% of newsletter-only contacts. An insight explains why a finding occurs, rests on more than one piece of evidence, and points to something to do that the findings alone don’t. A recommendation is the action itself, with the conditions under which it should be taken and how its result will be checked.

“Subscribers like Lantern more than newsletter readers do” isn’t an insight: it restates the finding. “Cold Brew’s appeal rests on knowing Lantern’s coffee” is one, because it explains the gap between the segments, it is supported by the focus groups as well as the survey, and it implies something specific: the people who don’t know Lantern need a reason to try the can.

Triangulation: four views of Cold Brew

Triangulation checks a conclusion against evidence from different methods, whose weaknesses differ, so that agreement strengthens it and disagreement shows where to look. Ana has four sources on Cold Brew, each with something the others lack: the survey, the focus groups, the shelf-price audit and the sizing.

What each piece of Lantern’s 2027 research says about Cold Brew, and what it can’t say

The sources agree on the shape of the answer. The survey finds intent highest where people know Lantern; subscribers in the focus groups say “If it tastes like the House Blend does when I make it at home, I’d buy it every week.” and “I’d pay more for it than for the big brands because I know who makes it.”. The people on the list who know Lantern least, the newsletter-only contacts, are cooler in the survey, and ready-to-drink buyers who have never bought from Lantern say, in the focus group, “The one I buy is about two fifty. I’d need a reason to pay a dollar more.” and “I don’t know the name, so I’d only try it if somebody handed me a free can.”; the audit confirms that the cheapest cans on the shelf, Daily Grind RTD’s, average $1.16 less. And the bottom-up estimate, built from listings and sales per store rather than from intent, comes to about half the goal. That is the insight: Cold Brew’s first-year volume will come from Lantern’s own customers and from turning strangers into triers, and the second is the risk.

What research can and can’t show

A survey shows associations: subscribers are keener on Cold Brew. It can’t show that subscribing makes people keener, because loyalty to Lantern may lead people both to subscribe and to want its new product: a confounding variable of the kind B01 described. Nor can it show what people will do: intent is a stated preference, what people say they would choose, and Survey error showed that it overstates buying. A revealed preference is read from what people actually choose, in sales records or in a test.

Some questions only an experiment can answer. Whether free cans turn strangers into repeat buyers, or whether $3.49 sells fewer cans than $2.99, are questions about causation, and the reliable way to answer them is to vary the cause on purpose and compare. A test market (M02) launches in a few stores before the rest; an A/B test (B01) randomly assigns visitors to two versions of a web page or offer. B12 Experimental Design teaches how to design both.

Reporting it

The research report carries the findings to people who weren’t in the analysis. It leads with the answer, separates what the data show from what the researcher concludes, shows every figure with its base and uncertainty, and states its limitations as plainly as its results. It also protects the people who took part: no one should be identifiable from a quotation, a table cell or a chart.

Key idea: Three tests for every line of a report

Is it supported? Every claim traces to data, with its base and margin; anything else is labelled as interpretation. Is it useful? Findings lead to insights and insights to actions, with the condition that would change the action. Is it safe? No respondent can be recognised, and no figure is shown on a base too small to bear it.

In depth

Writing an insight

An insight answers “why?” and then “so what?”. A useful test has three parts: does it explain a finding rather than restate it; does more than one piece of evidence support it; and does it suggest an action that a reader of the findings alone wouldn’t have reached? “Delivery speed was raised by 18.0% of subscribers and 8.1% of newsletter-only respondents” is a finding. “Subscribers notice delivery because the monthly box is a promise, and one late box breaks it” is a candidate insight: it explains the gap, it can be checked against the wording of the open answers and Lantern’s delivery records, and it suggests fixing the subscription deliveries first.

Insights are hypotheses with evidence, not facts. Write them so that they could turn out wrong, say what evidence supports them, and say what would show them wrong. An insight built on one striking quotation, or on a difference smaller than its margin of error, is a guess.

Triangulating in practice

Sources can converge (they point the same way), complement each other (one explains what another only measures, as the focus groups explain the survey’s gap between segments) or diverge. Divergence is itself a finding: if the survey had shown newsletter readers as keen as subscribers while the focus group of non-buyers rejected the price, Ana would need to ask which sample was unusual, or whether the survey’s price wording differed from what the focus group was shown. Weigh sources by their quality for the question at hand, not by counting them like votes: two weak sources that share a weakness, such as two surveys of Lantern’s own list, don’t make a strong one.

Qualitative evidence never becomes a share. In Ana’s two focus groups, four of the 20 coded quotations were about price, but that doesn’t mean 20% of buyers care about price: the quotations were chosen to illustrate themes, and the 15 participants were recruited, not sampled. Two coders agreed on 18 of the 20 codes (90%), an inter-coder agreement that says the themes were applied consistently, not how common they are.

Association, causation and experiments

A cross-tab or a chi-square test shows that two answers go together. To read it as a cause you would need to rule out every other explanation, which observational data rarely allow. B01’s comparison groups help, and so does a pattern that holds within subgroups, but the strongest design assigns the cause at random. The questions Ana’s report must leave open are causal ones: whether sampling raises repeat buying, and how much the price changes sales.

A test market answers such questions with real purchases. A design for Cold Brew: list it in some of the planned supermarkets for twelve weeks, offer free cans in half of them chosen at random, and compare cans sold per store per week, in enough stores for a difference worth paying for to show (B12). Random assignment means the two halves differ only by chance and by the sampling, so a clear difference can be put down to the sampling. The stores without free cans show the rate of sale without sampling, which is what Ana’s planning figure assumes; the gap between the two halves shows what sampling adds. Experiments cost time and money, so run one when the decision is large and the answer uncertain, which is the value-of-research logic of The research process.

Stated and revealed preference

Stated preferences are cheap to collect and can cover products that don’t exist yet, but they are hypothetical: answering “probably” costs nothing, and people want to please a researcher, or a brand they like. Revealed preferences are what people did with their own money, but they exist only for choices people have actually faced. Use stated intent to compare groups, rank options and spot problems, and revealed behaviour, from sales data or a test, to forecast volume. When the two disagree, trust behaviour about how much and use the survey to understand who and why.

The research report

OpenStax’s Principles of Marketing makes preparing the report the last of its seven research steps, the point at which recommendations can be acted on. A report is organised around the reader’s decision, not around the order in which the work was done. This course uses the structure below; some organisations put the limitations straight after the method, which is equally good, provided they are not left out.

The parts of a research report

For a survey, the method section should cover what AAPOR’s Code of Professional Ethics and Practices (revised 2021) requires researchers to disclose when they release results: who sponsored and conducted the research, the exact question wording, the population studied, how the sample was drawn and recruited, the mode and dates of data collection, the sample sizes and the precision of the results, how the data were weighted and processed, and a general statement of the design’s limitations. For Ana’s survey that means 2,004 invitations from the mailing list, a proportional stratified sample, a web questionnaire from 2 to 16 February 2027, 516 completes (AAPOR RR1, 25.7%), weighting to segment sizes and the margins that go with it.

The professional codes add a rule of presentation: findings, interpretation and recommendations must be told apart. The MRS Code of Conduct (2023) requires members to ensure that outputs “clearly distinguish between facts, opinion, and interpretation” (rule 60), and the ICC/ESOMAR International Code (2025 revision) asks researchers to distinguish findings from their interpretation and from any recommendations, and to give clients enough technical information to judge the results. A report that blends them leaves the reader unable to tell evidence from advocacy.

Honest charts and tables

A chart is honest when a reader who looks at it quickly takes away what the data support and no more. The rules follow from the earlier lessons:

  • Show the base under every bar or column, and say whether figures are weighted; give weighted figures with their unweighted base.
  • Show uncertainty: intervals on bars, or margins in a note, so that readers don’t read differences smaller than the noise.
  • Start bars at zero. A truncated axis exaggerates differences between shares. With the axis starting at 50%, subscribers’ 61.8% bar is 2.2 times the height of other shop customers’ 55.3% bar, although the true ratio is 1.12 and the two can’t be told apart.
  • Leave out bases below your minimum, or mark them clearly, rather than plotting them like the rest. NCHS’s minimum of 30 (Parker et al., 2017) is one published standard.
  • Keep scales in their natural order (definitely … definitely not), use the same axis for charts that will be compared, and label units: an NPS is in points, not per cent.

Uncertainty in words

Most readers don’t read intervals, so the words must carry them. Give a range where it matters (“between 34% and 43% of the list”), and choose the verb by what the interval allows.

Avoid words that claim more than a sample can: “proves”, “all customers”, “will buy”. Avoid “significant” on its own for readers who will hear “important”; say what the test showed instead (“a gap this large would rarely arise by chance”). And never turn “no evidence of a difference” into “no difference”.

Confidentiality and anonymity in reporting

People take part in research on a promise of confidentiality: the researcher may know who said what but won’t reveal it. Ana’s survey isn’t anonymous in the strict sense, since each answer is linked to a person on her list, so it is the reporting that must keep the promise. The MRS Code of Conduct (2023) requires members to preserve participants’ anonymity in what they share unless they have consented to be identified (rule 49), to take reasonable action to make anonymisation effective (rule 50), and to take particular care not to identify participants inadvertently, especially where samples are very small, as in business and employee projects (rule 51). The ICC/ESOMAR Code asks researchers to ensure that personal data can’t be traced by deductive disclosure: recognising someone by combining details that are harmless on their own.

Three habits keep a report safe. Attribute quotations by role or group code (“participant A3, subscriber group”), never by name, and cut details that point to one person, such as an employer, a job title or a street. Suppress small cells: Ana’s 27 workshop participants include only 5 aged 55 or over, and crossing them with how they first heard of Lantern produces cells of two and three people whom the workshop leader could recognise. This cell suppression has a trap: if a table shows a total and every cell but one, the hidden cell can be worked out, so a second cell must be hidden too or the categories merged. And treat business-to-business work with extra care: the 12 catering clients Lantern interviewed (of its 18) are few enough that a quotation about “a law firm’s board meeting” could identify one of them.

Acting on research

Research is worth what it changes. A recommendation should name who will do what by when, the condition under which the plan changes, and the measure that will be watched. The condition works best as an action standard, agreed before the results arrive so that nobody can move it to suit them. After the decision, compare what happened with what the research predicted: the gap is the best evidence on how far to trust the next survey.

Common mistakes

  • “The insight is that 38% would buy.”

    That is a finding. An insight explains why a finding occurs, rests on more than one source, and implies an action the finding alone doesn’t, such as aiming sampling at people who don’t know Lantern.

  • “Four sources agree, so the case is proven.”

    Sources that share a weakness don’t strengthen each other: a survey of Lantern’s list and focus groups of its subscribers both over-represent people who know Lantern. Triangulation is strong when the methods’ weaknesses differ.

  • “Four of the 20 quotations were about price, so 20% of buyers care about price.”

    Quotations are chosen to illustrate themes from a recruited group of 15. Qualitative work shows what views exist and why, not how common they are.

  • “Subscribers are keener, so turning customers into subscribers will raise demand.”

    The survey shows an association. Loyal customers choose to subscribe and also like Lantern’s coffee: a confounding variable. Only a design that varies the cause, such as an experiment, can show what subscribing does.

  • “Put everything in the findings; readers can judge.”

    The codes require findings, interpretation and recommendations to be told apart. Lead with the answer in the executive summary, keep each finding with its base and margin, and label conclusions as conclusions.

  • “A quotation is anonymous if the name is removed.”

    A job title, an employer, a street or a small group can identify someone just as well. Attribute by group code, cut identifying details, suppress small cells, and take extra care with business-to-business samples.

In practice

Fictional case

Ana’s executive summary, as she presents it to her team on 6 April 2027: “Recommendation: launch Lantern Cold Brew in stages. The people who know Lantern want it: weighted to our list, 38.2% say they would definitely or probably buy (roughly 34% to 43%; 516 completes), with subscribers far keener than newsletter-only contacts (62% against 31%). In our focus group, ready-to-drink buyers who don’t know Lantern compared $3.49 with cans that cost a dollar less and wanted to try before buying. Our bottom-up estimate is about 48,000 cans in year one, half the goal, which this plan can’t reach. So we launch in our own channel and natural grocers, planning on about 33,000 cans, aim free cans at strangers, and test a few supermarkets before listing in the rest. These are stated intentions from our own customers, not forecasts of sales.”

She answers the question behind the question. Her team asks whether 38% means the goal is safe. Ana explains that the figure describes people on Lantern’s own list, who know the brand, and that intent overstates buying; the sizing, built from stores and sales per store, is the better guide to volume. She shows the chart with intervals and leaves the workshop participants out, saying why.

She keeps the respondents out of it. A team member asks who said the can should have a lantern on it. Ana quotes the remark by its code, A2, as in the report, and declines to say more: participants were promised confidentiality. The catering interviews from November 2026 appear as counts of reasons, and any quotation is attributed only to “a catering client”, with details that could point to the firm removed, because 12 clients are few enough to recognise.

She writes down what would change her mind. The decision log records the plan, the owner of each action, and the rule: if the test supermarkets without free cans average fewer than 10 cans a store a week over 12 weeks, Lantern stays out of the remaining supermarkets for 2027 and puts the money into its own channel. When the test ends she will compare its sales with the bottom-up assumptions, and the gap will tell her how far to trust the next round of research.

Summary

  • A finding states what the data show, with base and uncertainty; an insight explains why, rests on more than one source and implies an action; a recommendation is the action, with its conditions and a way to check it.
  • Triangulation combines methods whose weaknesses differ. Convergence strengthens a conclusion, divergence is itself a finding, and qualitative counts never become shares.
  • Surveys show associations and stated preferences. Causes and volumes need revealed behaviour or an experiment: a test market or an A/B test.
  • A report leads with the answer, discloses its method (AAPOR’s list: sponsor, wording, population, sample, mode, dates, sizes and precision, weighting, processing, limitations) and keeps findings apart from interpretation.
  • Honest charts show bases and intervals, start bars at zero, leave out small bases and keep scales in order; words follow the interval: “clearly higher” only when it excludes zero.
  • Keep respondents anonymous: quotations by code, identifying details cut, small cells suppressed (and a second cell when totals would reveal the first), extra care with business samples.
  • Act on research with an owner, a decision rule set in advance and a later comparison of outcome with prediction.

Terms introduced

Finding
A fact a piece of research established, stated with its base and uncertainty, such as the share of respondents who chose an answer.
Insight
An explanation of why a finding occurs, supported by more than one piece of evidence, that suggests an action the findings alone don’t.
Stated preference
What people say they would choose or do, collected in a survey or interview; for a purchase it usually overstates what they go on to do.
Revealed preference
A preference inferred from what people actually chose or bought, as in sales records or a test market.
Executive summary
The opening page of a report, giving the answer and the recommendation first, with the main findings, their uncertainty and the main limitation.
Deductive disclosure
Identifying a research participant by combining pieces of information that don’t identify anyone on their own, such as a role, a location and an age group.
Cell suppression
Withholding a table cell based on too few people to report reliably or anonymously; a second cell is withheld when totals would otherwise reveal the first.
