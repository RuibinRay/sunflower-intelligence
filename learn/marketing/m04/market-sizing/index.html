Market sizing

How big is the market, and how much of it can you win? Define a market by its products, customers, place and period; separate TAM, SAM and SOM; estimate from the top down and the bottom up; use survey intent with an explicit conversion assumption; and report a range with the assumption that drives it, not one hopeful number.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Define a market size by its products, customers, place, period and unit, and convert volume into value at a stated price level.
  • Distinguish the total addressable, serviceable available and serviceable obtainable markets (TAM, SAM and SOM), stating the definitions you use.
  • Calculate a market or sales estimate from the top down (category × shares) and from the bottom up (outlets × rate of sale, or customers × buying × frequency), in Excel.
  • Apply an explicit conversion assumption to stated purchase intent, and explain why intent is not behaviour.
  • Evaluate an estimate by triangulation, sensitivity analysis and sanity checks, and work out what would have to be true for a goal to be reached.

Review first

  • The product life cycle — M02 set Lantern Cold Brew’s first-year goal of about 100,000 cans in a category of 4.8 million; this lesson tests it.
  • Situation analysis — Market share and substitutes, which define what a market includes, were introduced there.
  • Survey error — The February survey’s weighted intent figures and the caution about stated intentions come from that lesson.
  • What-if analysis: data tables, Goal Seek, scenarios and Solver — The sizing model uses Goal Seek, a data table and sensitivity analysis.

The idea

Fictional case

It is March 2027. Lantern Cold Brew is still a plan: 250 ml cans at $3.49 in Harbour City, where about 4.8 million cans of ready-to-drink coffee are sold a year. M02’s plan set a first-year goal of about 100,000 cans. Since then Ana has talked to two store buyers, asked her mailing list about Cold Brew in the February survey and audited competitors’ shelf prices. Before she orders a first production run, her board wants to know how many cans Lantern can really expect to sell, and whether 100,000 is an estimate or a hope.

What a market size is

A market size is the quantity of a product that a defined group of customers buys in a defined place over a defined period. All four parts matter. “The cold brew market” means nothing until you say which products count (canned ready-to-drink coffee only, or bottles and café iced coffee too), whose purchases count, where and over how long. Harbour City’s 4.8 million cans is a yearly figure for canned ready-to-drink coffee, all brands and all channels; change any part of the definition and the number changes, and so does every market share calculated from it.

Size can be measured in volume (units: cans, tubs, memberships, seats) or in value (money), and value is volume times an average price. Say which price. Shoppers pay the shelf price; producers receive the shelf price less what the shops and distributors keep. At $3.49 on a supermarket shelf, Lantern receives about $2.37 a can (M02).

TAM, SAM and SOM

Market sizes are often given as three nested figures. The total addressable market (TAM) is the whole demand for the product category in the place and period, whoever supplies it. The serviceable available market (SAM) is the part of the TAM that the business’s offer and its routes to market could serve. The serviceable obtainable market (SOM) is the part of the SAM that the business can realistically win in the period, given its resources and its competitors.

The labels are most used in start-up and technology practice, and sources define them differently. TechTarget describes TAM as the revenue opportunity at a 100% market share, “as if no competition exists”; Carta describes it as the total demand for your product across all potential customers. For one source SAM is the part of the TAM a company’s products can serve, for another the part it can reasonably expect to reach, and some put a time limit on SOM while others don’t. So say in words what each of your three figures counts. This course uses the definitions in the previous paragraph.

For Lantern Cold Brew the TAM is M02’s 4.8 million cans a year. The SAM is the cans sold through the three kinds of outlet in Lantern’s plan: supermarkets (45% of the category’s cans), natural and specialty grocers (15%) and cafés and online shops (10%), the kind of outlet Lantern runs itself. Together they sell 70% of the category, 3.36 million cans; the convenience stores’ 30% is outside it because the plan leaves them out. The SOM is the figure the board is asking for.

Two routes to an estimate

Top-down market sizing starts from the whole category and narrows it with shares: the cans sold in the planned channels, then the share of those the business might win. Bottom-up market sizing builds the figure from its parts: how many stores will stock the product and how many it sells in each, or how many customers will buy and how often. Top-down is quick and shows the scale. Bottom-up is slower, but every input is something a store buyer, a customer or a trial can confirm or contradict.

Ana’s bottom-up estimate for year one is 48,200 cans: 30 supermarkets at 10 cans a store a week, 25 natural grocers at 14, and 1,200 cans a month through Lantern’s own cafés and online shop. M02’s goal is 100,000. One is 1.4% of the SAM and the other 3.0%. This lesson is about deciding which to believe, and what would have to be true for the larger one.

Key idea: A size is a claim with four parts

Products, customers, place and period, in a stated unit and, for value, at a stated price level. “A $15 million market” isn’t a fact until you can say what was counted, where, over how long and at which price.

In depth

Drawing the boundary

The first decision is which products count. Draw the line too tightly and you miss real competition; draw it too loosely and the market looks huge while your share looks tiny. M01’s substitutes give the test: a product belongs in the market if the customers you want treat it as a real alternative.

The broader definitions aren’t wrong; they answer different questions. A launch plan needs the category the product will be shelved in, because that is where shoppers compare it and where store buyers judge it.

TAM, SAM and SOM in practice

The TAM is narrowed to the SAM by whatever limits what the business could serve: the channels it will sell through, the regions it can deliver to, the customer types its offer suits. Lantern’s SAM is limited by channel. A stricter analyst would count only the stores expected to list Lantern, which gives a much smaller SAM (an exercise asks you to work it out). Neither choice is wrong, but the shares calculated from them differ, so say which you used.

The SOM has to come from evidence about what the business can win: listings, rates of sale, customers who will buy. It is an estimate. A goal is different: a target the business sets itself. When a goal is larger than the SOM, the plan has to change, or the goal does.

Top-down: from the category to a share

A top-down estimate multiplies the category by a chain of shares. Its inputs usually come from secondary data: trade associations, syndicated data from retail panels, industry reports and official statistics (the secondary-research lesson covers where to find them and how to judge them; OpenStax gives an industry report of all related product sales as an example). Lantern’s chain is short: 4.8 million × 70% = 3.36 million cans in the SAM. The last link, the share of the SAM that Lantern will win, is the hard one, and nothing in the category data says what it should be.

Caution: “If we get just 1% of the market…”

The commonest top-down error is to pick a small share of a large market and call it modest. One per cent of Harbour City’s 4.8 million cans is 48,000, almost exactly Ana’s bottom-up estimate, yet for a new brand known to about 12% of category buyers (M02) that share is not modest: every can has to be won from a store buyer and then from a shopper. A share in a top-down estimate needs evidence of its own, such as what comparable new brands achieved in their first year.

Bottom-up: from outlets or from customers

For a product sold in shops, a bottom-up estimate multiplies the number of outlets that will stock it by its rate of sale, the units an outlet sells in a period, usually per store per week. Outlet counts come from store lists, distributors and official business statistics: in the United States, the Census Bureau’s County Business Patterns gives the number of establishments by six-digit industry code for every county, each year. Rates of sale come from store buyers, from comparable products and, best of all, from a trial in a few stores.

For a product sold direct, or to customers the business can count, build from customers instead: the number of people in the target group, the share who will buy, how often they buy and how much each time. The same structure fits a service (members × uptake × visits × price) and a business product (firms × adoption × seats × price per seat).

Volume into value

To turn cans into dollars, multiply by an average price, and choose it with care. The right average is weighted by volume, the price of the average can actually sold, which needs each brand’s sales. Ana’s shelf-price audit (the next lesson) gives 24 shelf prices from ten stores, averaging $3.15, so the category is worth about 4.8 million × $3.15 ≈ $15.1 million a year at shelf prices. The average of the three brands’ own averages is $3.05, which gives $14.6 million. If the cheapest brand, Daily Grind, sells the most cans (it is the best known, at 75% awareness in M02), the true figure is lower still.

Value at shelf prices is what shoppers spend; producers receive less. In M02’s three retail channels the distributors and retailers keep 32% to 40% of the shelf price. Weighting those margins by each channel’s share of the category, and assuming the cafés and online shops keep nothing (true only of a brand selling through its own, as Lantern will), the trade keeps 32.1%, so producers receive at most about $10.3 million. Quote the figure that matches the decision, and say which it is.

Using what customers say they will do

Surveys give another bottom-up route: ask people how likely they are to buy, then count buyers. In February Ana asked her mailing list, on a five-point purchase-intent scale, how likely they would be to buy Lantern Cold Brew at about $3.49 in the next three months. Weighted to the list’s four groups by post-stratification (the survey-error lesson explains the weights), 13.7% said they definitely would and 24.6% probably would: about 819 and 1,475 of the 6,000 people on the list.

Stated intent isn’t behaviour: the survey-error lesson showed that it tends to overstate buying, most of all for new products. Armstrong, Morwitz and Kumar (2000) found that forecasts built on purchase intentions for existing products were more accurate than extrapolating past sales, but they note that intentions-based methods often give biased estimates, overstating or understating actual buying, and that sales data should be used to correct the bias where they exist. A new product has no sales of its own, so the correction is an assumption: a conversion rate for each answer, borrowed where possible from a comparable launch, and varied to see what it does.

The list is exactly the people Lantern’s own cafés and online shop can reach, so it suits the direct channel. Suppose 40% of those who said “definitely” and 10% of those who said “probably” become regular buyers, each taking a four-pack a month (fictional assumptions). That gives 819 × 0.40 + 1,475 × 0.10 ≈ 475 regular buyers and about 1,900 cans a month, against Ana’s 1,200.

Now vary the assumption. At 25% and 5% the estimate is about 1,114 cans a month; at 60% and 20%, about 3,146. The conversion rates move the answer by a factor of nearly three. Notice also what the question measured: intent to try within three months. How many of those who try come back every month is a separate assumption that no intent question answers.

Note: What Ana couldn’t see

Because the case holds every person’s answer, the survey can be compared with a truth Ana had no way of observing. On the whole list 12.7% would say “definitely” and 26.6% “probably”, against her weighted 13.7% and 24.6%. With the same 40% and 10%, the truth gives about 1,854 cans a month instead of 1,900: a difference of about 50 cans, small beside the range of 1,114 to 3,146 that the conversion assumption creates.

Triangulating: what a gap means

Triangulation in sizing means estimating the same quantity by independent routes and comparing the results (the secondary-research lesson does the same with sources). Agreement is reassuring only if the routes really are independent. Disagreement is more useful, because it points at the assumption that differs. Lantern has three figures for year one.

The simulator seems to support the goal until you look at what it assumes. It treats a channel as all or nothing: once Lantern chooses supermarkets, it is on every supermarket’s shelf. Spread over all 120 supermarkets, its 92,139 supermarket cans come to about 14.8 cans a store a week, and its natural-grocer cans to about 37 a store a week, against the buyers’ 10 and 14.

Put Ana’s 55 listings into the simulator’s rates of sale and its figure falls to about 91,000 cans; use her direct estimate as well and it is about 86,000. The rest of the gap is the rates of sale. How many stores will list Lantern, and how fast it sells in each, are the two things to research next.

The gap matters for money as well as volume. M02’s pricing lesson put the default plan’s break-even at about 117,000 cans a year. On Ana’s estimate, the first year wouldn’t cover its fixed costs, and even the goal is below break-even; whether that is acceptable for a launch year is the board’s judgement, but the sizing makes it visible.

Sensitivity, ranges and sanity checks

A sizing model has many inputs, and they are not equally important. A sensitivity analysis moves one input at a time from a low to a high value you can defend, holding the others at their central values, and ranks the inputs by how much they move the answer. Ana’s low and high values, her own judgements after the buyer conversations (fictional), give the table below.

The rate of sale in natural grocers matters most, because Ana is least sure of it and natural grocers supply the largest part of her estimate. The ranking depends on the ranges, so they must be honest, not chosen to make a point. With every input at its low value the model gives 20,928 cans, and with every one at its high value 81,920. Those extremes are wider than any likely outcome, because all five assumptions are unlikely to miss in the same direction, yet even the top of the range is below 100,000.

Report the result as a range around a central figure and name what drives it: “about 48,000 cans, 40,000 to 56,000 if any one assumption is off, and most sensitive to how fast it sells in natural grocers.” Then sanity-check the figure against what you already know.

  • The category. An average Harbour City supermarket sells about 346 ready-to-drink coffee cans a week (2.16 million ÷ 120 ÷ 52), so Ana’s 10 cans would be 2.9% of a listing store’s category, plausible for a new brand. In natural grocers 14 of about 308 is 4.55%.
  • Capacity. Can the co-packer make the volume, and can a small team serve every store that lists the product?
  • Units and periods. Weeks against months, cans against four-packs, a calendar year against a launch year that starts part-way through it.
  • Double counting. A subscriber who buys in the online shop and in a supermarket appears in two routes.

A figure that fails a sanity check is wrong somewhere, even if every multiplication is right.

Common mistakes

  • “Our TAM is the opportunity.”

    The TAM counts every sale in the category, by every competitor, through every channel. A business can win only part of its SAM, and only with listings, customers and time. Lead with the SOM and show how it was built; quote the TAM for context.

  • “One per cent of a big market is a safe assumption.”

    A share chosen to sound modest is still an assumption, and for a new brand 1% can be hard. One per cent of Harbour City’s category is 48,000 cans, which needs about 55 listings at the rates of sale the buyers expect. Build the share from listings and rates of sale, or from customers, rather than picking it.

  • “The goal is our forecast.”

    A goal is a target; an estimate is what the evidence supports. When the two differ, as 100,000 and 48,200 do, work out what would have to be true for the goal, check it, and change either the plan or the goal.

  • “Customers who say they will buy are buyers.”

    Stated intent isn’t behaviour, and intentions-based forecasts can overstate or understate buying. Apply an explicit conversion rate to each answer, vary it, and remember that a question about the next three months measures trial, not repeat purchase.

  • “A single number looks more professional.”

    A point estimate hides how uncertain it is. Give a central figure, a range and the assumption that moves it most: that tells the reader what to watch and what to research next.

  • “Volume times the average shelf price is the producers’ revenue.”

    Shelf prices measure what shoppers spend. Producers receive the price after the trade’s margin, and a simple average of shelf prices isn’t weighted by what sells. State the price level and how the average was formed.

In practice

Three people use market sizes in three different ways. An investor reading a start-up’s pitch looks past the TAM, which is usually large and easy to find, to how the SAM is defined and to the bottom-up build of the SOM: how many customers, at what price, reached through which channel. A pitch that offers only “1% of a huge market” hasn’t done the work.

A retailer’s category buyer, deciding whether to list a new product, thinks in rates of sale: how many units a store a week, against the products already on the shelf, and what the space and the listing fee would earn otherwise. A supplier that can show credible rates of sale from a trial, rather than a share of the national market, gets a more serious hearing.

A product manager uses the range, not the central figure, to plan production and stock. A first run sized to the central estimate, with a contract that allows a quick second run, keeps both too little and too much stock affordable. The assumption with the widest swing tells her what to measure first, usually in a small test market or a trial in a few stores.

Fictional case

Ana’s note to her board, March 2027: “Our bottom-up estimate for Cold Brew’s first year is about 48,000 cans, 1.4% of the cans sold in our planned channels, and 40,000 to 56,000 if any one assumption is off. The 100,000-can goal would need us to sell 2.5 times as fast as the store buyers expect in every store that lists us, or to be listed in more supermarkets than Harbour City has. I propose we replace the goal with this range, size the first production run to the central figure, and measure rates of sale in a few stores before ordering a second. Whether to launch on these numbers is for our April review.”

The same discipline applies to a bakery planning a new loaf, a software firm pricing an add-on or a gym chain opening a site: define the market, build the estimate from parts someone can check, compare routes, and report a range with the assumption that drives it.

Summary

  • A market size is the quantity of a defined product bought by defined customers in a defined place and period, in units (volume) or money (value); change the definition and the size, and every share, change.
  • TAM is the whole category, SAM the part the business’s offer and channels could serve, SOM the part it can realistically win; sources define them differently, so say what each of yours counts.
  • Top-down multiplies the category by shares, and its weak link is the share you assume you will win. Bottom-up builds from outlets × rate of sale × weeks, or customers × share who buy × frequency × units.
  • Value = volume × average price: say which price (shelf or producers’), and weight the average by volume where you can.
  • Stated intent needs an explicit conversion assumption for each answer; vary it, and don’t mistake intent to try for repeat buying.
  • Triangulate: estimate by independent routes and read a gap as a pointer to the assumption that differs.
  • Run a sensitivity analysis, report a central figure with a range and its main driver, and sanity-check against the category, capacity, units and double counting.
  • Ask what would have to be true for a goal: Lantern’s 100,000 cans needs 2.5 times the buyers’ rates of sale, or more supermarkets than Harbour City has.

Terms introduced

Market size
The quantity of a product bought by a defined group of customers in a defined place over a defined period, measured in units (volume) or money (value).
Total addressable market
TAM: the whole demand for a product category in a defined place and period, whoever supplies it. Definitions vary between sources; some describe it as the revenue available at a 100% market share.
Serviceable available market
SAM: the part of the total addressable market that a business’s offer and routes to market could serve, such as the sales in the channels it plans to use. Definitions vary between sources.
Serviceable obtainable market
SOM: the part of the serviceable available market that a business can realistically win in a stated period, given its resources and competitors. An estimate, not a goal.
Top-down market sizing
Estimating a market, or a business’s sales, by starting from the whole category and narrowing it with shares: the channels or segments served, then the share the business expects to win.
Bottom-up market sizing
Estimating a market, or a business’s sales, by building it from its parts: outlets × rate of sale × periods, or customers × the share who buy × how often × how much.
Rate of sale
The number of units of a product an outlet sells in a period, usually per store per week; also called velocity.
