The research process

Research earns its cost only through a decision. Learn to turn a business decision into a research problem and measurable objectives, choose an exploratory, descriptive or causal design, write a brief and read a proposal, put a ceiling on what research is worth, and follow the professional codes that protect the people you study.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Distinguish a decision problem from a research problem, and write research objectives that are specific, measurable and tied to an action.
  • Choose an exploratory, descriptive or causal design for a research objective, and explain what each can and can’t deliver.
  • Describe the steps of a research project and the contents of a research brief and a research proposal.
  • Calculate the expected value of perfect information for a decision, and use it with the “could it change the decision?” test to judge whether research is worth its cost.
  • Apply the main rules of the ICC/ESOMAR, MRS and Insights Association codes: informed consent, the right to withdraw, privacy, no selling under the guise of research, honest reporting and research with children.

Review first

  • Business questions and measurable objectives — A research objective is a decision-focused question made measurable; that lesson introduced hypotheses too.
  • Collecting data: sources, samples and bias — You need primary and secondary data, experiments and the outline of consent and personal data under the UK GDPR.
  • What finance is: money, time and risk — Valuing research uses the expected value of an uncertain outcome.
  • Confidence intervals — Lantern’s survey plan comes from the sample-size calculation in that lesson.

The idea

Fictional case

It is the end of January 2027, and Ana Lim has two questions for Lantern Coffee Co.’s year. The first is Lantern Cold Brew, the 250 ml can planned in M02 at $3.49: should she launch it this summer in supermarkets, natural and specialty grocers and Lantern’s own channel, as the launch plan proposes? The second is how customers see Lantern after its first year: whether they would recommend it, how they found it and what it should do better. She has about $7,000 she could spend on research before the end of April, a mailing list of 6,000 people and no time for studies that won’t change what she does.

Marketing research is the systematic planning, collection, analysis and reporting of information about customers, markets and competitors, to help make a marketing decision. OpenStax’s Principles of Marketing quotes the American Marketing Association’s description of it as the function that links consumers, customers and the public to the marketer through information. The word that matters is decision. Research that informs no decision is an expense, however interesting its findings; research that starts from a decision knows what to measure, how precisely and by when.

From a decision to a research problem

Every project starts by separating two problems that are easy to confuse. The decision problem is the choice a manager faces, stated as the actions on the table: launch Cold Brew in all three channels, sell it only through Lantern’s own channel for a year, or wait. The research problem is what the manager needs to find out to make that choice well: how many people would buy the can at its price, which people, and what would put the others off. A decision problem asks “what should we do?”; a research problem asks “what do we need to know?”

The research problem is then broken into research objectives: specific, measurable statements of what the study will deliver. “Find out what customers think of Cold Brew” is a topic, not an objective. “Estimate, to within about ±4 percentage points, the share of Lantern’s mailing list who would definitely or probably buy Cold Brew at $3.49 in the next three months” is an objective: it names the population, the measure and the precision, and its answer bears on the launch. Where earlier evidence suggests an answer, state it as a hypothesis, such as “subscribers are more likely than newsletter-only contacts to say they would buy”, so that the data can show it to be wrong.

A good test of each objective is to ask what you would do with each possible answer. If Ana would launch whether 20% or 60% of her list said they would buy, the question isn’t worth asking, or it is being asked of the wrong people. It is the test of B01’s decision-focused question: different answers must lead to different actions.

Three kinds of design

The objectives decide the design: the overall plan for collecting and analysing the data. Textbooks group designs into three kinds by what they can deliver; OpenStax adds ethnographic research as a fourth, which this course treats as one of the exploratory methods.

ExploratoryUnderstanding a problem; ideas and hypothesesSecondary data, depth interviews, focus groups, observationThemes, explanations, better questionsThe March focus groups on Cold Brew

Exploratory research helps you understand a problem and generate hypotheses, with flexible, small-scale methods such as depth interviews (one-to-one conversations following a guide) and focus groups (moderated discussions in a small group), which the qualitative-research lesson teaches. Descriptive research measures the characteristics of a population or market, usually with a survey of a planned sample. Causal research tests whether changing one thing causes a change in another, by making the change for a randomly chosen group and comparing the outcome with a control group, as in B01’s A/B test.

Projects often run the three in sequence: explore to learn what to ask, describe to measure how common each answer is, and test cause only for the few changes worth an experiment. Ana’s plan runs partly the other way. The survey goes out first because B04 planned it and the list is ready, and the focus groups follow to explore what the survey can’t: why people would or wouldn’t buy. Either order works if each design is used for what it can do.

Key idea: Research is for decisions

Before anything is collected, write down the decision, the options and the result that would favour each. If no plausible result would change what you do, the research isn’t worth doing, however interesting it sounds.

In depth

The steps of a research project

OpenStax’s Principles of Marketing sets out seven steps, and this course follows them. The figure shows them with the part of M04 that teaches each one.

  1. Define the problem. The decision problem, the research problem and the objectives, and the population the decision is about.
  2. Develop the research plan. The design, the data, the timetable and the budget, agreed through a brief and a proposal.
  3. Select the data collection method. Secondary data first, then primary data by survey, interviews, groups, observation or experiment.
  4. Design the sample. Whom to ask, how many, and how to choose them.
  5. Collect the data. Fieldwork, with a record of who responded and who didn’t.
  6. Analyse and interpret the data. Estimates with their margins of error; themes from what people said.
  7. Prepare the research report. Findings, their limits and what they mean for the decision.

The steps are a checklist, not a one-way street. A pretest can send you back to the objectives, a poor response rate to the sample, and a surprising finding to a new exploratory study.

Primary or secondary data

Step 3 starts with a choice B01 introduced: secondary data, which already exists because it was collected for another purpose, or primary data, collected for the question at hand. OpenStax’s comparison is the usual one. Primary data can be structured to be exactly what is needed but is expensive to collect; secondary data is generally inexpensive or free but can be outdated, and it was defined for someone else’s purpose. So professionals search secondary sources first and collect primary data only for what remains, which is the subject of the next lesson.

Matching the design to the objective

Read the verb in each objective. “Why”, “what would make” and “which ideas” call for exploratory work: the answer is a set of explanations, and a dozen well-chosen conversations beat a thousand ticked boxes. “How many”, “what share” and “how often” call for descriptive work, and the sample must be chosen so that it stands for the population; Module 2 shows how.

“Does X cause Y”, “will changing X raise Y” and “which version works better” call for an experiment. A survey may find that people who like the can are more likely to say they would buy, but that is an association, not proof that a better can would sell more: as B01’s interpreting-results lesson showed, other differences between the two groups could explain it. Only a design in which chance decides who gets the change separates its effect from everything else.

The brief and the proposal

A study is usually agreed through two documents. The research brief is written by the client, the person or team who will act on the results, whether a manager is briefing an agency or a colleague in the business’s own research team. The research proposal is the researcher’s answer: how the study will meet the brief, at what cost and by when. The brief says what is needed and why; the proposal says how.

Two items in the brief do most to keep research useful. The first is the decision with its deadline: a report that arrives after the decision has been taken is worth nothing. The second is an action standard: a result agreed in advance that will lead to a stated decision, such as “go ahead if at least 30% of target buyers say they would definitely buy”. Agreeing it before the data arrive stops everyone from reading the result in the light of what they already wanted to do.

What research is worth

Research costs money and time, and it can add value only through the decision, by making a better choice more likely. That gives two tests. First, could any plausible result change the decision? If not, the research is worth nothing to it. Second, is its cost below what a better decision could gain?

The second test has a ceiling you can calculate. List each option’s payoff in each possible state of the market and the probability you give each state now. The expected value of an option, as F00 defined it, weights each payoff by its probability; without research you choose the option with the highest expected value. With perfect information you would learn the state first and choose the best option for it. The gain from choosing that way is the expected value of perfect information (EVPI).

No real study is perfect: a survey of intentions can say demand looks strong when it isn’t. So the EVPI is a ceiling on what any research can be worth to the decision, not a forecast of what a particular study will return. A study that costs more than the EVPI can’t pay for itself; one that costs less might, if it is good enough to change the choice. The ceiling is highest when the stakes are large and the options’ expected values are close, which is exactly when the decision is hardest.

In-house or agency

Lantern has no research department, so Ana must decide who does each part. An agency brings trained moderators and interviewers, recruiters who can find people the client can’t reach, access to panels, capacity and independence from the client’s hopes. Doing the work in-house is cheaper and quicker to start, and it keeps customer data and plans inside the business, but it needs the skills, the time and the discipline to report results the owner won’t like. Many studies mix the two: the client runs what it can and buys the specialised parts.

Ethics in market research

Market research depends on people being willing to take part and on the public trusting that research is what it says it is. Three professional codes set the rules most researchers work under: the ICC/ESOMAR International Code on Market, Opinion and Social Research and Data Analytics, issued jointly by the International Chamber of Commerce and ESOMAR and revised in 2025; the Market Research Society’s Code of Conduct in the UK (2023); and the Insights Association’s Code of Standards and Ethics for Market Research and Data Analytics in the US. The ICC/ESOMAR code’s first principle sums them up: “All research must be legal, honest, transparent and truthful.”

Informed consent and the right to withdraw. Informed consent is a participant’s voluntary agreement to take part, given after being told who is doing the research, what it involves and how their data will be used. The ICC/ESOMAR code requires researchers to identify themselves promptly, to make participation voluntary and based on clear and accurate information, and to let people withdraw from the research at any time. The MRS code requires participants to be told of their right to withdraw at any time, and the Insights Association’s code says researchers must respect the right to refuse or to stop taking part.

Privacy and data protection. Survey answers linked to a name or an e-mail address are personal data, so the data-protection law outlined in B01 applies, including its rules on consent where that is the legal basis. The ICC/ESOMAR code adds data minimisation: collect only personal data that is reasonable and directly relevant to the research. Researchers also promise confidentiality (they know who answered but don’t reveal it) or anonymity (they collect nothing that identifies anyone), a distinction OpenStax draws; the two promises are not the same, so make only the one you can keep.

No selling under the guise of research. Sugging, selling under the guise of research, is contacting people as if for research in order to sell to them or to collect sales leads. The MRS code gives it as an example of the activities under the guise of research that its rules forbid; the ICC/ESOMAR code requires a clear distinction between research and non-research activities; and the Insights Association’s code requires the two to be kept separate. That code allows incentives only to encourage people to take part: a thank-you for answering is one thing, a sales offer inside the questionnaire another.

Honest reporting. The ICC/ESOMAR code makes the client and the researcher jointly responsible for ensuring that published results are not misleading. The MRS code requires findings to be clearly supported by the data and not presented misleadingly, and the Insights Association’s code requires public releases to be accurate and corrected promptly if wrong. A client may decide not to publish a study at all; publishing only its flattering half, so that the published result misleads, is a different matter.

Children. All three codes require consent from a parent or responsible adult before children take part, but they define a child differently, as the table shows.

Caution: Check the code that applies to you

The codes agree on principles and differ in detail, and national law comes first. Read the code of the association you or your agency belong to and the law where your participants live. Ana’s survey offers no age group below 18, so its invitation should say that it is for adults only.

Common mistakes

  • “The research will tell us what to do.”

    Research reduces uncertainty about a decision that someone still has to make. Write down the decision and its options first, and the result that would favour each; otherwise the study answers questions nobody needed answering, and the decision is taken on instinct anyway.

  • “More information is always worth having.”

    Information has value only if it could change the decision, and it can never be worth more than the expected value of perfect information. A study whose every plausible result leads to the same choice is worth nothing to that choice, however cheap.

  • “The focus groups showed that most people would buy.”

    Exploratory methods use small, deliberately chosen groups: they show what people think and why, not how many think it. Shares come from descriptive research on a planned sample, and cause from experiments. Match the design to the verb in the objective.

  • “Writing the brief is the agency’s job.”

    The brief is the client’s: only the client knows the decision, its deadline and what it can spend. The proposal is the researcher’s answer. An agency asked to “find out what customers think” has to guess the decision, and usually guesses wrong.

  • “A discount code at the end of the survey will lift the response.”

    An offer to buy inside a questionnaire mixes research with selling, which the codes forbid, and MRS’s regulations on incentives rule out price discounts and the client’s own goods as incentives. A thank-you incentive for taking part, such as cash or a voucher for shops other than the client’s, is allowed; sales offers go through marketing, to people who agreed to receive them.

  • “Anonymous and confidential mean the same thing.”

    Confidential answers can be traced by the researcher but are never revealed; anonymous answers can’t be traced at all. A survey that records e-mail addresses is at best confidential, so promising anonymity would mislead the people who answer it.

In practice

Fictional case

Ferrow Buses, a fictional regional bus company, wants to bid for a council grant to run an airport express from the city centre. Its research manager receives a one-line brief from the commercial director: “Prove that passengers want an airport express, for the grant application.”

She reframes the brief. Research can’t be commissioned to prove a conclusion, and an application built on a study designed to agree would break the codes’ rules on honest reporting. She rewrites the decision problem as a choice: whether to bid for the grant, and if so for what service (every 30 or every 60 minutes, at what fare). The research problem is how many trips a day the service would carry at each frequency and fare, and which travellers would use it.

She orders the designs. Secondary data comes first: the airport’s published passenger numbers, the council’s travel survey and the company’s own ticket records. Exploratory interviews with airport staff, frequent flyers and taxi drivers follow, to find out how people travel to the airport now and what would make them switch. A descriptive survey of travellers at the airport, sampled by day and time, then measures how many would use each option. A causal question, whether a lower fare would raise use, can only be answered by a trial, so she proposes testing two fares in the first months of service if the grant is won.

She puts a ceiling on the cost. The commercial director’s rough figures are a $400,000 profit over the grant period if demand is high, a $250,000 loss if it is low, and a 50% chance of each. Bidding has an expected value of $75,000 against $0 for not bidding; with perfect information the company would bid only if demand were high, for $200,000. The EVPI is $125,000, so the $38,000 agency quote for the airport survey passes the ceiling test. They agree an action standard before fieldwork: bid for the 30-minute service if the survey’s estimate implies at least 900 trips a day at the planned fare.

She splits the work. The desk research and the interviews are done in-house; the airport survey goes to an agency, because interviewing in a terminal needs the airport’s permission, trained interviewers and a sampling plan by flight time. And she checks the ethics. Marketing wanted the survey to collect e-mail addresses for fare offers: she moves that to a separate, optional sign-up after the interview. School pupils travel on the airport road, so interviewers screen out anyone under 18 rather than seek a parent’s consent in a terminal; and the grant application will quote the survey’s estimate with its base and margin of error, whatever it turns out to be.

The same habits apply to a two-person café and to a national brand: start from the decision, choose the design from the verb in each objective, spend no more than a better decision could gain, and treat the people who answer as the reason research is possible at all.

Summary

  • Marketing research exists to inform decisions. The decision problem is the choice to be made; the research problem is what must be found out to make it; research objectives make that specific and measurable.
  • Test every objective by asking what you would do with each possible answer; if every answer leads to the same action, drop it.
  • Exploratory research explains and generates hypotheses; descriptive research measures how many and how often from a planned sample; causal research tests cause with experiments.
  • The steps run from defining the problem and planning, through choosing data, sampling and collecting, to analysis and a report, and the result feeds the next question.
  • The client’s brief states the decision, what is known, objectives, timing, budget and action standards; the researcher’s proposal answers with design, sample, analysis, timetable and cost.
  • Research is worth doing only if a result could change the decision, and it is worth at most the expected value of perfect information: the expected value of choosing with perfect knowledge minus that of the best choice without it.
  • The ICC/ESOMAR, MRS and Insights Association codes require informed consent and the right to withdraw, respect for privacy, no selling under the guise of research, honest reporting and a responsible adult’s consent for children, whom they define differently.

Terms introduced

Marketing research
The systematic planning, collection, analysis and reporting of information about customers, markets and competitors, to help make a marketing decision.
Decision problem
The choice a manager faces, stated as the actions being considered: what to do, not what to find out.
Research problem
The information needed to make a decision, stated as what the research must find out.
Research objective
A specific, measurable statement of what a study will deliver, such as an estimate, a comparison or an explanation, chosen because its answer bears on a decision.
Exploratory research
Research that aims to understand a problem, find possible explanations and generate hypotheses, usually with flexible, small-scale methods such as depth interviews, focus groups, observation and secondary data.
Descriptive research
Research that measures the characteristics of a population or market, such as how many, how often, who and how much, usually with a survey of a planned sample or with records.
Causal research
Research that tests whether changing one thing causes a change in another, by experiment: the change is made for a randomly chosen group and the outcome compared with a control group.
Research brief
The document in which the client describes the decision, the background, what it needs to know, whom to study, the timing and the budget, so that researchers can propose a study.
Research proposal
The researcher’s answer to a brief: the objectives as understood, the design and methods, the sample, the analysis, the deliverables, the timetable and the cost.
Action standard
A result agreed before research is carried out that will lead to a stated decision, such as “launch if at least 30% of target buyers say they would definitely buy”.
Expected value of perfect information
The most that information removing all uncertainty could be worth to a decision: the expected value of choosing the best option once the outcome is known, minus the expected value of the best option chosen without that knowledge. Abbreviated EVPI.
Informed consent
In research, a participant’s voluntary agreement to take part, given after being told who is doing the research, what it involves and how their data will be used, with the right to withdraw at any time.
Confidentiality
In research, the promise that the researcher, who knows who gave each answer, will not reveal it or link it to the person in anything shared.
Anonymity
In research, collecting no information that could identify the person who gave an answer, so that not even the researcher knows who it was.
Sugging
Selling under the guise of research: contacting people as if for research in order to sell to them or to collect sales leads. Professional research codes forbid it.
