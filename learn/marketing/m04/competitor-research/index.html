Competitor research

Find out what rivals offer, charge and plan, from sources you can use honestly: websites, price lists, reviews, job advertisements, trade shows, filings, customers and store checks. Run a shelf-price audit and summarise it by brand and channel, know where the professional codes and the law draw the line on mystery shopping and confidential information, and turn the findings into decisions about your own offer.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Identify what to learn about a competitor (offer, price, positioning, distribution, promotion, resources, performance and plans) and the decision each answers.
  • Choose and evaluate sources of competitor information, from websites and job advertisements to filings, customers and store checks.
  • Design a shelf-price audit and summarise it by brand and channel with AVERAGEIFS, MINIFS and MAXIFS, comparing like with like and separating promoted prices.
  • Apply the MRS and ESOMAR guidance on mystery shopping, SCIP’s code and the law on trade secrets to decide which research methods are acceptable.
  • Construct a benchmarking table and turn its findings into implications and questions for your own offer.

Review first

  • Situation analysis — The competitor analysis framework, market share and the competitive-strength scorecard are M01’s; this lesson supplies the research behind them.
  • Conditional sums, counts and averages — The audit is summarised with AVERAGEIFS, MINIFS, MAXIFS and COUNTIFS.
  • Pricing approaches — Competition-based pricing and Lantern’s planned $3.49 are where the audit’s findings are used.
  • The research process — Research ethics (honesty, consent, sugging) apply to competitor research too.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

In the week of 15–19 March 2027, Ana recorded the shelf price of a single 250 ml can of each of M02’s three brands, Summit Brew, Daily Grind RTD and Arcadia Coffee, in ten Harbour City stores, chosen for convenience rather than at random: four supermarkets, three convenience stores and three natural and specialty grocers. She wants to know whether Lantern Cold Brew’s planned $3.49 still makes sense on real shelves. On the catering side, Brewline Catering won all 5 of the bids Lantern lost in 2026, at $7.50 a head against Lantern’s $9.00, and she knows little more about it than that.

What to find out about a competitor

M01’s competitor analysis asked whom customers compare you with and how you compare with them. This lesson is about the research behind it: what to find out, where to find it, and how to do so honestly. OpenStax calls the activity competitive intelligence, the collection of information about competitors from the marketplace on any element of the marketing mix, such as checking competitors’ prices, auditing their promotion or examining their distribution. It is one part of M01’s wider market intelligence.

Collect what could change a decision, and no more. A file of every fact about every rival is expensive and soon out of date. A few facts about the two or three competitors that customers really compare you with, refreshed when they move, are worth far more.

Three rules of honest competitor research

Most of what a business needs to know about its competitors is public, or can be learned from customers who are free to say it. The difficulty is not access but discipline: knowing which facts matter, recording them so they can be compared, and staying on the right side of the professional codes and the law. Three rules cover most cases.

  1. Observe what any customer could see. Published prices, shelves, menus, adverts, job advertisements and filings are fair game; so is buying a rival’s product and testing it.
  2. Ask people what they are free to tell you. Customers, lost prospects and distributors can tell you why they chose a rival; don’t press them for documents they are bound to keep confidential.
  3. Never lie, pay or pressure your way to what a rival keeps secret. Bribery breaches every code; misrepresentation to obtain confidential information breaches their rules on honesty (SCIP’s requires you to say who you are before any interview); and for trade secrets misrepresentation, bribery and inducing a breach of confidence are improper means in law.

The rest of the lesson takes the sources in turn, then the store check, where most of Ana’s evidence comes from, then mystery shopping and the law, and finally how to turn findings into decisions.

Reviews are the easiest source to over-read. The people who post them form a self-selected sample, weighted towards the delighted and the furious, so they show what can go right or wrong, not how often. Count themes in them as you would code open answers, and never report a share of reviewers as a share of customers.

For listed companies, filings are the richest public source: the US Securities and Exchange Commission’s EDGAR system gives free access to the documents public companies file, with full-text search, and the secondary-research lesson shows how to read such records. Most of a small business’s rivals are private, though, and for them the shelf, the trade and the customer matter more.

Customers see a rival’s offer from the side that matters. A win–loss analysis interviews buyers soon after they decide, both those who chose you and those who chose a competitor, about what decided it. Asking only the buyers who chose you invites survivorship bias, the flaw in M01’s client interviews; the prospects who went elsewhere hold the evidence you lack.

Store checks and shelf-price audits

A store check is a visit to a set of outlets to record what is actually on the shelf: which products, at what prices, with how much space, what is on promotion and what is out of stock. A shelf-price audit is a store check focused on prices. Its value is that it measures what shoppers face, not what a price list says. Five design decisions make an audit worth analysing.

  1. Purpose. Start from the decision. Ana’s is whether $3.49 fits, so she needs single cans of the rival brands, in the channels of her plan and, for comparison, convenience stores.
  2. Which stores. Cover each channel, and say how the stores were chosen. Only a probability sample of stores, drawn at random from a list of all of them, supports an estimate for the whole city with a margin of error (the sampling-methods lesson); otherwise the audit describes the stores visited.
  3. When. Visit every store in the same week, so that prices are compared under the same promotions; repeat the audit to learn how often rivals promote.
  4. What to record. The shelf price as shown, and when a promotion is on, the regular price too; the pack size; facings and out-of-stocks; the store and the date.
  5. Like with like. Compare the same pack size, or convert to a price per unit of volume, and compare within a channel before comparing across channels.

Summit Brew is the dearest brand in every channel and Daily Grind RTD the cheapest wherever it is sold. Convenience stores charge most for both brands they stock ($4.06 and $2.52 on average), and natural grocers a little more than supermarkets. Across all stores the ranges are $3.59 to $4.19 for Summit Brew, $1.99 to $2.59 for Daily Grind RTD and $2.79 to $3.09 for Arcadia Coffee.

Be careful with the “All stores” column. Daily Grind RTD isn’t sold in natural grocers and Arcadia Coffee isn’t sold in convenience stores, so each brand’s overall average mixes different channels, and the comparison between them partly measures where they are sold. Compare within a channel first.

Two of the 24 prices were promotions, both at S2: Summit Brew at $3.59 and Daily Grind RTD at $1.99. Against M02’s usual prices ($3.79 and $2.29) those are cuts of 5.3% and 13.1%, the promotion depth. Without them the supermarket averages are $3.76 for Summit Brew and $2.26 for Daily Grind RTD. One week can’t show how often the rivals promote, and the audit missed something it should have recorded: the regular price beside each promoted one.

Finally, what the audit can’t say. Its ten stores were not drawn at random (four of Harbour City’s 120 supermarkets, for instance), so its averages describe those stores in that week, with no margin of error for the city. A simple average of shelf prices also ignores which stores sell most. It is good evidence of where the rivals’ prices sit; it is not evidence of what shoppers will do at $3.49, which is M02’s price-response question.

Mystery shopping and its limits

Mystery shopping means sending a trained researcher, posing as an ordinary customer, to experience and record a service. When a company shops its own outlets to check service standards, MRS’s guidelines require that its staff have been told it may happen. Shopping competitors is different, because their staff can’t be told, and the professional codes allow it only with care.

The MRS Code of Conduct (2023) allows recording only in a company’s own mystery shopping, where staff have been told it may happen, and refers members to separate MRS guidelines. Those guidelines (2011) say that a competitor’s staff must not be identified or recorded in any way, results must be reported in aggregate, the time taken should be as short as possible and no more than a normal customer enquiry, and the staff must suffer no detriment.

ESOMAR’s guideline on mystery shopping studies (2005) adds that such studies should be carried out only where no substantial disadvantage will result, with the time and other demands reasonable and kept to a minimum, since for a self-employed professional an enquiry can literally cost lost income; any booking should be cancelled as quickly as possible. Results must not be used to discipline or otherwise disadvantage individuals.

Competitive-intelligence professionals have a stricter rule for interviews. The code of ethics of the Strategic Consortium of Intelligence Professionals (SCIP) requires members to comply with all applicable laws and to disclose all relevant information, including their identity and organisation, before every interview. A sound working rule follows from all three: observe what any customer would see and buy what any customer would buy; don’t make a rival do work for a sale that will never happen (a detailed quote, a booking you will cancel) beyond what an ordinary enquiry takes; and never use a false identity to obtain information a real customer wouldn’t be given, or to question a rival’s staff.

The legal line: trade secrets

The law draws the outer line at trade secrets. In US federal law a trade secret is business information whose owner has taken reasonable measures to keep it secret and which has economic value because it is not generally known or readily found out by proper means (18 U.S.C. § 1839). Acquiring one by “improper means” is misappropriation under the same section, and improper means include theft, bribery, misrepresentation and inducing a breach of a duty to keep it secret; reverse engineering, independent derivation and other lawful means are expressly excluded.

The EU’s Trade Secrets Directive (2016/943) draws a similar line. In the UK its rules on lawful and unlawful acquisition take effect through the law of breach of confidence, with the Trade Secrets (Enforcement, etc.) Regulations 2018 filling gaps, as their explanatory note explains. Under the directive, observing, studying, taking apart or testing a product that has been made available to the public is lawful acquisition; unauthorised access to or copying of a holder’s documents, and any other conduct contrary to honest commercial practices, is not.

Caution: An outline, not legal advice

The practical message is the same everywhere: buy and test rivals’ products, read what they publish, and ask customers what they are free to tell you. Before anything close to the line, such as hiring a rival’s former employee or receiving a document someone shouldn’t have shared, take legal advice.

From findings to implications

Benchmarking lays out your offer or performance beside competitors’ on the same measures. The table is only half the work: each row should end in an implication or a question for your own offer. A gap matters only if customers care about it, and a difference customers can’t see is no difference to them.

Common mistakes

  • “The list price is the price.”

    Customers pay shelf prices, discounts and promotions. A rival’s published price list is a starting point; a store check or a buyer’s account of what was quoted shows what customers actually pay.

  • “Averages across all stores compare brands fairly.”

    Brands are stocked in different channels, and channels price differently. An all-store average partly measures where a brand is sold. Compare within a channel first, then across channels.

  • “Promotions are just part of the average.”

    Mixing promoted and regular prices hides both the regular price level and the promotion strategy. Record the regular price beside every promoted one, report them separately, and repeat the audit to learn how often rivals promote.

  • “Ten stores tell us the city’s average price.”

    An audit of stores chosen by the researcher describes those stores in that week. An estimate for every store in the city, with a margin of error, needs stores drawn at random from a full list.

  • “Posing as a customer is just good research.”

    Observing an ordinary customer experience is accepted with conditions: no identifying or recording rivals’ staff, no more of their time than a normal enquiry, results in aggregate. A detailed quote for an event that will never happen goes beyond those conditions, SCIP’s code forbids interviewing under a false identity, and for trade secrets misrepresentation is improper means in law.

  • “A benchmark table is the conclusion.”

    A table of differences is evidence, not a decision. Each row needs an implication or a question for your own offer, and a gap matters only if customers care about it.

In practice

In many consumer-goods companies, competitor research is routine rather than occasional. Field sales teams record shelf prices, facings and promotions on their store visits; the brand manager receives a monthly summary by channel and chain and compares each rival’s regular and promoted prices with the brand’s own. The summary feeds two decisions: whether the brand’s price position still matches its positioning, and when to expect a rival’s promotion and plan around it.

A business-to-business firm learns most from its bids. After each win or loss, someone who wasn’t on the sales team phones the buyer and asks what decided it, what the winner offered and what would have changed the outcome. Over a year the pattern of answers says more about a rival’s pricing and service than any website, and it is entirely honest, because the buyer chooses what to share.

Hotels, airlines and online retailers can track rivals’ published prices every day, by hand or, within the sites’ terms of use, with software that reads the rivals’ public websites. The rule of thumb that keeps it honest is the same as in a store: collect what any customer could see, and nothing a rival has kept private.

Fictional case

Ana’s note after the audit, March 2027: “On the shelves we checked, $3.49 puts us between Arcadia Coffee and Summit Brew in both of our planned channels, about 22 to 40 cents under Summit Brew, which fits a single-origin can that undercuts the premium leader. It doesn’t tell us how many cans we’ll sell. I will repeat the audit in April and record regular prices beside any promotions. For catering, I’m booking short calls with the five organisations that chose Brewline, asking what decided it and what they were offered if they are free to say, and I’ll keep an eye on Brewline’s job advertisements for drivers.”

In each case the method is the same: decide what you need to know for a decision, collect it from sources you can use openly, compare like with like, and end with what it means for your own offer.

Summary

  • Competitor research finds out what rivals offer and charge, how they position, distribute and promote, what resources they have and what they plan; collect only what could change a decision.
  • Sources range from websites, price lists, reviews and job advertisements to trade shows, filings and, most valuable, customers and lost prospects (win–loss analysis).
  • A shelf-price audit records what shoppers actually face. Design it around the decision: the same pack, every store in the same week, regular prices beside promoted ones, and an honest statement of how the stores were chosen.
  • Summarise by brand within each channel (AVERAGEIFS, MINIFS, MAXIFS), separate promotions, and convert to a price per unit when pack sizes differ.
  • The MRS and ESOMAR guidance allows mystery shopping of competitors only with care: staff not identified or recorded, minimal time, results in aggregate. SCIP requires identity to be disclosed before interviews.
  • Trade secrets are protected in law: misrepresentation, bribery and inducing a breach of confidence are improper means; buying and testing a product sold to the public is not.
  • Benchmarking puts offers side by side; each row should end in an implication or a question for your own offer.

Terms introduced

Competitive intelligence
The collection and analysis of information about competitors (their offers, prices, distribution, promotion, resources and plans) from the marketplace, by lawful and ethical means, for decisions.
Win–loss analysis
Interviewing buyers soon after a decision, both those who chose you and those who chose a competitor, about what decided it.
Store check
A visit to a set of outlets to record what is actually on the shelf: products, prices, space, promotions and out-of-stocks.
Shelf-price audit
A store check focused on prices: recording the shelf price of defined products in a set of stores over a short period, with promotions marked.
Promotion depth
The size of a temporary price cut as a percentage of the regular price: (regular − promoted) ÷ regular.
Mystery shopping
Research in which a trained researcher, posing as an ordinary customer, experiences and records a service; of a company’s own outlets, or with conditions of competitors’.
Trade secret
Business information that has value because it is not generally known and that its holder takes reasonable steps to keep secret; acquiring it by improper means, such as misrepresentation, bribery or inducing a breach of confidence, is misappropriation in US law, and EU and UK law call it unlawful acquisition.
Benchmarking
Comparing a business’s offer, prices or performance with competitors’ on the same measures, side by side, to find gaps and their implications.
