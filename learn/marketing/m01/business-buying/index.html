How organisations buy

Organisations buy differently from households: their demand is derived from their own customers’, they are fewer and larger, purchasing is a profession, and several people share each decision. Learn the three buying situations, the six roles in a buying centre, the stages of the business buying process and the procurement rules that decide how a seller must sell.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Compare business and consumer markets, and calculate how a change in end demand is amplified in orders for equipment.
  • Classify purchases as straight rebuys, modified rebuys or new tasks, and explain what each means for the current supplier and a challenger.
  • Identify the roles in a buying centre (initiators, users, influencers, buyers, deciders and gatekeepers) from what people do in a purchase.
  • Describe the stages of the business buying process and the procurement rules that govern it.
  • Recommend how a seller should approach an organisational buyer.

Review first

  • What marketing is — The lesson builds on the distinction between business-to-consumer and business-to-business marketing.
  • How consumers make buying decisions — The business buying process is compared with the five-stage consumer process, and supplier choice uses the weighted-attribute model.
  • Percentages: finding 10% of a number and increasing a number by 15%, for the calculations of derived demand.

The idea

Fictional case

Harbor Tech, a 300-person software firm, has been a Lantern catering client since its first month: in March 2026 it paid a $2,400 deposit for three catered events at $800 each. Lantern’s catering now has 18 corporate clients, each booking about 6 events a year at about $820 an event.

Fictional case

When Ana asks how Harbor Tech decides to book, five answers come back. Harbor Tech and its people are fictional.

  • Hana Hallett, the office manager, finds suppliers, books events and signs orders up to $2,000.
  • Tom Okoye, the finance director, approves any supplier contract over $2,000 and sets the budget.
  • Lena Brooks, the head of people, asked for better coffee at staff events after an engagement survey.
  • The staff, about 300 people, drink the coffee and fill in the event feedback form.
  • Marco Diaz, the reception lead, decides which sales calls and samples reach Hana.

None of those five people is “the customer”. Each takes part in the decision in a different way, and a caterer who talks only to the person who places the order may never reach the people who started the idea, who drink the coffee or who sign the larger cheques. This is what makes organisational buying different, and this lesson gives you the tools to analyse it.

Organisations that buy include businesses buying inputs and services to produce what they sell, resellers such as wholesalers and retailers buying goods to sell on, governments, and institutions such as hospitals, universities and charities. Selling to them is business-to-business marketing, and their purchases together make up the business market.

How business markets differ

These are tendencies. A small firm buying one laptop behaves much like a household, and a family buying a home goes through a process as formal as many companies’.

The difference with the biggest consequences is where demand comes from. Derived demand is demand for a product that arises from the demand for what it helps to produce. Harbor Tech doesn’t want coffee for its own sake: it wants it for staff events, which depend on how many people it employs, how well it is doing and how much it cares about staff engagement. If its sales fall and it cuts events, no price Lantern can offer will bring the bookings back. A business seller therefore has to watch its customers’ markets, not just its customers.

Size matters too. Lantern’s online shop serves about 1,500 customers, and its average order value in each month from July to December 2026 was between about $41 and $55. Its catering serves 18 clients, each worth thousands of dollars a year. Losing one online customer is barely noticed; losing one catering client is felt at once. With so few, so valuable customers, the relationship with each one is worth managing deliberately.

Three buying situations

Not every organisational purchase is the same kind of decision. Patrick Robinson and Charles Faris, with Yoram Wind (1967), distinguished three buying situations, or buy classes, by how new the purchase is to the organisation, how much information it needs and how many alternatives it considers.

Straight rebuy
The buyer reorders something it has bought before, from the same supplier, on the same terms, often routinely and by someone low in the organisation. Little information is sought and few people are involved.
Modified rebuy
The buyer wants to change something about a repeat purchase: the specification, the price, the terms or the supplier. More people get involved, more information is sought, and other suppliers get a chance.
New task
The organisation buys something for the first time. Risk and uncertainty are highest, so it seeks the most information, considers the most alternatives and involves the most people.

Each situation favours someone different. In a straight rebuy the current supplier, the in-supplier, holds a strong position and its job is to keep quality and service high enough that nobody thinks of looking elsewhere. An out-supplier, any competitor, usually has to turn the purchase into a modified rebuy, giving the buyer a reason to reconsider, such as a better total cost or a problem it can solve. A new task is open to all, and suppliers who help the buyer understand its own need early often shape the specification in their favour.

The buying centre

The buying centre, also called the decision-making unit, is everyone in an organisation who takes part in a purchase decision. Frederick Webster and Yoram Wind (1972) described five roles in it: users, influencers, buyers, deciders and gatekeepers. Many textbooks, OpenStax among them, add a sixth, the initiator.

Initiator
The person who first suggests the purchase, often because they notice a problem.
User
The people who will use the product or service. They often start the purchase and help define what is needed, and their experience decides whether it is bought again.
Influencer
People whose views or expertise shape the specification or the choice without making the final decision: engineers, specialists, consultants, and users whose feedback is asked for. Not to be confused with a social-media influencer.
Buyer
The person with formal responsibility for dealing with suppliers and arranging the terms, often a purchasing professional or, in a small firm, whoever handles the paperwork. In this sense “buyer” is a role, not the whole organisation.
Decider
The person or group with the power to choose the supplier or approve the purchase. For a large purchase this is often someone senior, or a committee.
Gatekeeper
Anyone who controls what information and which salespeople reach the others: an executive assistant, an IT manager who vets software suppliers, a purchasing department that insists all contact goes through it.

Key idea: Classify people by what they do, not by their title

A job title rarely tells you a role. An IT manager can be a gatekeeper, a plant manager can be the decider for small orders and only an influencer for large ones, and a user can be an initiator. One person can hold several roles and several people can share one. Ask, for this purchase: who started it, who will use it, who shapes it, who deals with suppliers, who can say yes, and who controls access?

Fictional case

Harbor Tech’s five people fill all six roles in its catering purchases between them, and some fill more than one. Working out who plays which role is the first stage of the course project, so this lesson leaves the map to you. When you draw it, use everything the case says about each person, not just their job title.

In depth

Derived demand and the acceleration effect

Derived demand has a consequence that surprises people. Demand for equipment and other durable inputs depends not only on how much the customer’s own customers buy, but on how fast that is changing. A courier with a fleet of vans replaces a share of the fleet each year as vans wear out. If parcel volumes grow, it must also buy extra vans; if they stop growing, it buys replacements only; if they fall, it may buy nothing at all.

So a modest change in end demand produces a large swing in orders for vans. Economists call this the acceleration principle; this lesson calls it the acceleration effect. The challenging example works it through with numbers, and the formula is below.

For a supplier of equipment, the lesson is to forecast its customers’ customers. A van maker that read last year’s orders as its trend would expand just as orders were about to halve. The same logic, less sharply, applies to any business input bought in advance of demand: the catering a firm books for staff it plans to hire, the stock a retailer orders for a season it expects to be good.

The business buying process

Organisational purchases go through more stages than a consumer’s, and the stages are more formal. OpenStax lists eight.

  1. Problem recognition. Someone recognises a need or an opportunity, internally (a machine breaks down, staff complain) or externally (a supplier shows a better way).
  2. General need description. The organisation describes in general terms what it needs: how many, what for, how reliable.
  3. Product specification. It turns the need into technical requirements, often with engineers or specialists. A supplier who helps write the specification has an advantage.
  4. Supplier search. It looks for qualified suppliers: those it already knows, trade directories, recommendations, online search.
  5. Proposal solicitation. It asks the qualified suppliers for proposals: a simple quote for a routine item, or a formal request for proposal for a complex one.
  6. Supplier selection. It evaluates the proposals against its criteria and chooses one supplier or several. Many buyers use a weighted scorecard, the weighted-attribute model of the consumer-decisions lesson applied to suppliers.
  7. Order-routine specification. It sets the terms of the order: quantities, delivery schedules, service levels, payment terms, warranties.
  8. Performance review. It judges how the supplier performed, and decides whether to continue, change the terms or switch.

Not every purchase goes through every stage. Robinson, Faris and Wind set the stages against the three buying situations, in what became known as the buygrid. The table shows the usual pattern.

A typical pattern, not a rule. Textbooks differ in the details; the point is that rebuys skip most of the search and evaluation.

Compared with the five consumer stages, the business process splits the consumer’s evaluation into specification, search, proposals and selection, and turns post-purchase behaviour into a formal performance review. Like the consumer model, it is a checklist rather than a law: stages overlap, loop back and are sometimes skipped even in large purchases.

How organisations choose suppliers

Business buyers weigh more than the price. They care about the total cost of ownership: everything the organisation will pay to acquire, use, maintain and eventually replace the product, which can make a dearer offer cheaper in practice. They care about risk: what happens if a delivery is late, the product fails or the supplier goes out of business. They care about service, reliability and flexibility. And the people in the buying centre care about their own position: Thomas Bonoma (1982) argued that buyers act in their own interest as they see it, and that the person with the title is not always the one who controls the purchase. A purchasing manager, for example, may prefer the supplier least likely to get her blamed if something goes wrong.

This is why business buyers are often described as rational and personal at once. A proposal must make the economic case on paper, for the decider and the procurement process, and also answer each person’s own question: will it work for the users, will it meet the engineer’s specification, will the decider be glad they approved it?

Procurement rules

Most organisations of any size write down how purchases must be made. The rules protect them against waste, error and fraud, and a seller who doesn’t know them can lose a sale it had already won in conversation. Common features include:

  • Approval limits. Each person may commit the organisation only up to an approval limit; above it, someone more senior or a committee must approve. The person who places orders is often not allowed to approve large ones.
  • Competition thresholds. Purchases above a set value need several written quotes, and above a higher value a formal tender or request for proposal, evaluated against published criteria.
  • Approved suppliers. Staff may have to buy from an approved supplier list of vetted suppliers, so a new supplier must first be approved, which can take weeks.
  • Separation of duties. The person who requests a purchase, the one who approves it and the one who pays for it are kept apart, the segregation of duties that F01 introduced as an internal control.
  • Ethics rules. Limits on gifts and hospitality, declarations of conflicts of interest and, during a tender, rules that suppliers may contact only the named procurement officer. Bribery is illegal; in the United States, the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act forbids bribing foreign officials, and OpenStax discusses it in its chapter on business buying.

Public bodies have the strictest rules, because they spend public money. In the United States, the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR) governs purchases by federal agencies. Its thresholds are adjusted for inflation: the current ones took effect on 1 October 2025, up from $10,000 and $250,000, and the version in force is FAC 2026-01, effective 13 March 2026.

Under the FAR, a purchase at or below the micro-purchase threshold of $15,000 may be made without seeking competing quotations if the price is reasonable, and such purchases are to be spread fairly among qualified suppliers as far as practicable. Between that and the simplified acquisition threshold of $350,000, the contracting officer must promote competition as far as practicable and, where the purchase is not advertised publicly, should consider asking at least three sources. A requirement may not be split into several smaller purchases just to get under a threshold. Larger purchases follow more formal procedures still. Other countries’ governments, and many hospitals, universities and companies, have equivalent rules with their own thresholds.

Caution: Rules change and differ

Thresholds are revised from time to time, and every organisation sets its own. Treat the figures here as an illustration of how procurement rules work, and check the current rules of any organisation you sell to before you plan the sale.

Selling to organisations

The ideas of this lesson add up to a plan for selling to an organisation. Find out which buying situation you are in, because it decides whether you are defending, challenging or starting from scratch. Map the buying centre by what people do, and plan what each role needs to hear: users want something that works in their day, influencers want their technical criteria met, deciders want the economic case and a low risk of regret, buyers want a complete, compliant proposal on time, and gatekeepers want a reason to let you through.

Learn the procurement rules early, so that your proposal fits the approval limits and competition thresholds rather than running into them. Lower the buyer’s risk with trials, references, guarantees and clear service terms. And remember that in a business market the sale is often the start of a relationship: performance reviews decide the next purchase, and a straight rebuy is won or lost in the months of service before it.

Common mistakes

  • “The buyer is the person who decides.”

    In a buying centre, the buyer is the role that deals with suppliers and terms. The decider may be someone else, and for a large purchase often is. Find out who can say yes, and up to what amount.

  • “A job title tells you someone’s role.”

    Roles are defined by what people do in a particular purchase. An IT manager can be a gatekeeper, a nurse can be the initiator, and a plant manager can be the decider for small orders and only an influencer for large ones.

  • “Win over the person you talk to and you have won the sale.”

    Organisational purchases are shared decisions. A seller who reaches only one member of the buying centre leaves the others to be persuaded by someone else, or not at all.

  • “Business buyers only care about price.”

    They care about total cost of ownership, reliability, risk and service, and the people involved care about how the decision reflects on them. A higher price can win if it lowers total cost or risk.

  • “Orders for business equipment move in step with consumer demand.”

    Derived demand for equipment depends on the change in end demand, so it swings far more. In the Swiftline example a 10% rise in parcels doubled van orders and a halt in growth cut them by 45%.

  • “A newcomer can’t win a straight rebuy.”

    Not as a straight rebuy, but a challenger can give the buyer a reason to reopen the decision, turning it into a modified rebuy: a lower total cost, a solution to a problem the in-supplier hasn’t fixed, or a better service level.

In practice

Fictional case

Corvid Energy, a fictional supplier of battery-storage systems for warehouses and depots, has heard that Swiftline Couriers is looking at battery storage for its main depot. Its key account manager, Amara Rhee, prepares an account plan before she makes contact.

She starts with derived demand. Swiftline’s electricity use, and its need for storage, follows its parcel volumes, and its interest in cutting costs follows its margins. Amara reads Swiftline’s annual report and the forecasts for parcel volumes in its region, and notes that a firm whose volumes have just stopped growing will want a short payback.

Next, the buying situation and the rules. Swiftline has never bought storage: a new task, so it will need education, and the supplier that helps it understand peak-demand charges may shape the specification. Swiftline’s published guide for suppliers says that any purchase over $100,000 needs three written proposals and board approval, so a request for proposal is likely. She makes sure Corvid is on Swiftline’s approved supplier list before the request goes out, because it can take weeks to be added.

Then the buying centre. The operations director raised the idea after the depot’s electricity bill rose: the initiator. The site electricians will live with the system: users, and influencers through their view of safety and maintenance. An outside energy consultant will draft the specification: an influencer. The procurement team will run the request for proposal and control contact with suppliers while it is open: buyer and gatekeeper. The finance director and the board will decide.

Amara’s plan gives each a reason to favour Corvid: a site survey and safety briefing for the electricians, open data on performance for the consultant, a ten-year total cost of ownership for finance, and a performance guarantee to lower the board’s risk.

Finally, the relationship. If Corvid wins, the first installation becomes a reference for Swiftline’s other depots, which would be modified rebuys rather than new tasks, and the maintenance contract becomes a straight rebuy that Corvid must defend with good service. Amara’s plan therefore runs past the sale, to the performance review that will decide the next one. The same reasoning lets Lantern plan its catering sales: the course project asks you to apply it to Harbor Tech.

Summary

  • Business markets differ from consumer markets: demand is derived, buyers are fewer and larger, purchasing is professional and formal, several people share each decision, and relationships last.
  • Derived demand for equipment depends on the change in end demand, so orders swing far more than the market behind them (the acceleration effect).
  • Robinson, Faris and Wind’s three buying situations, the straight rebuy, the modified rebuy and the new task, differ in newness, information needed and people involved; each favours the in-supplier or a challenger differently.
  • A buying centre has six roles: initiator, users, influencers, buyer, decider and gatekeeper. Identify them by what people do in the purchase, not by job title; one person can hold several.
  • The business buying process has eight stages, from problem recognition to performance review; rebuys skip most of the search and evaluation.
  • Business buyers weigh total cost of ownership, risk and service as well as price, and work within procurement rules: approval limits, competition thresholds, approved supplier lists and ethics rules.
  • Selling to an organisation means knowing the buying situation, the buying centre and the rules, and reducing each member’s risk; the sale is often the start of a relationship.

Terms introduced

Business market
The organisations that buy goods and services to produce other products, to run their operations or to resell: businesses, resellers, governments and institutions.
Derived demand
Demand for a product that arises from the demand for what it helps to produce, and ultimately from the demand of consumers.
Acceleration effect
The tendency for orders of equipment and other durable inputs to swing by a much larger percentage than the end demand they serve, because orders depend on the change in demand as well as its level.
Straight rebuy
A buying situation in which an organisation reorders something it has bought before, from the same supplier and on the same terms, with little search or deliberation.
Modified rebuy
A buying situation in which an organisation wants to change the specification, price, terms or supplier of something it has bought before.
New task
A buying situation in which an organisation buys something for the first time, with the most uncertainty, information search and people involved.
Buying centre
All the people in an organisation who take part in a purchase decision, each in one or more roles; also called the decision-making unit.
Initiator (buying centre)
In a buying centre, the person who first suggests the purchase.
User (buying centre)
In a buying centre, the people who will use the product or service; they often help define the need and judge the supplier afterwards.
Influencer (buying centre)
In a buying centre, a person whose views or expertise shape the specification or the choice without making the final decision, such as an engineer or a specialist. Not to be confused with a social-media influencer.
Buyer (buying centre)
In a buying centre, the person with formal responsibility for dealing with suppliers and arranging the terms of the purchase.
Decider (buying centre)
In a buying centre, the person or group with the power to choose the supplier or approve the purchase.
Gatekeeper (buying centre)
In a buying centre, a person who controls what information and which salespeople reach the others.
Business buying process
The stages of an organisational purchase: problem recognition, general need description, product specification, supplier search, proposal solicitation, supplier selection, order-routine specification and performance review.
Request for proposal
A formal invitation to suppliers to propose how they would meet a stated need and at what price, used when the buyer wants to compare solutions as well as prices.
Total cost of ownership
Everything an organisation pays to acquire, use, maintain and eventually replace or dispose of a product over its life, not just the purchase price.
Approval limit
The largest amount a person may commit their organisation to on their own authority; larger purchases need approval from someone more senior or a committee.
Approved supplier list
The suppliers an organisation has vetted and allows its staff to buy from; a new supplier must usually be approved before it can be used.
