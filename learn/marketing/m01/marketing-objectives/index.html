Marketing objectives

A business goal says where the business wants to be; a marketing objective says which customers must do what, by when, to get it there. Learn to turn a goal into SMART objectives across the funnel, and to work out how many new customers a growth target really needs once you allow for the customers you lose.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Distinguish business goals, marketing objectives, strategies and tactics, and explain how each one serves the one above it.
  • Write SMART marketing objectives and test objectives against the five criteria.
  • Set objectives for each stage of the marketing funnel, from awareness to advocacy, with a measure for each.
  • Calculate how many new customers a revenue target needs, given retention and revenue per customer.
  • Calculate the leads or enquiries needed to win a number of customers, from the funnel’s conversion rates.
  • Evaluate whether a set of objectives is consistent, achievable and protected against gaming.

Review first

  • Customer value, satisfaction and lifetime value — Retention and what a customer is worth drive how many new customers a growth target needs.
  • Situation analysis — Objectives are set from the situation analysis: where a business stands decides what it can achieve.
  • Business questions and measurable objectives — B01 defined SMART objectives; this lesson applies them to marketing.
  • KPIs: measuring what matters — Objectives are tracked with KPIs; leading and lagging indicators, guardrail metrics and Goodhart’s law come from that lesson.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana Lim’s goal for 2027 is simple to say: catering revenue 25% higher than at its current pace of 18 clients booking about 6 events a year at about $820 an event. It is also impossible to act on as it stands. It doesn’t say whether the extra revenue should come from new clients, from more events with existing clients or from higher prices, and it ignores the fact that not every client stays: on Ana’s estimate, about 61% of Lantern’s catering clients carry on from one year to the next. Turning the goal into something the business can act on, and check, is the job of marketing objectives. This lesson teaches the method on other businesses; the course project applies it to Lantern’s catering.

Goals, objectives, strategies and tactics

Four words are used loosely in business, and it helps to fix their meaning before setting any targets. This course uses them as follows.

Business goal
What the business as a whole wants to achieve, usually in money or size: more revenue, more profit, a larger share of a market. Ana’s 25% is a business goal.
Marketing objective
What customers must do, how much and by when, for the goal to be met: how many customers to win or keep, how many must know the business, how often they should buy. A marketing objective names a customer behaviour, a measure, a level and a date.
Marketing strategy
How the business will reach its objectives: which customers it will serve and how it will compete for them. A marketing strategy is a choice, such as “win small accountancy firms by offering the fastest payroll set-up in the region”.
Tactics
The specific actions that carry the strategy out: this month’s price offer, a tasting for a prospect, an advertisement. Most tactics are marketing-mix decisions, the subject of M02.

Each level serves the one above it, and each is judged by it. A tactic is good if it moves an objective; an objective is good if meeting it delivers the goal. Sources use the words differently. OpenStax’s Principles of Marketing treats goals as broad aims over three to five years and objectives as shorter-term steps of a year or less; other sources use the two words the other way round, or interchangeably. The labels matter less than keeping the four levels apart.

Where growth can come from

A revenue goal can be split into the customer behaviours that produce it, using a driver tree like the ones in B01:

Revenue=Customers×Purchases per customer×Average spend per purchase\text{Revenue} = \text{Customers} \times \text{Purchases per customer} \times \text{Average spend per purchase}Revenue=Customers×Purchases per customer×Average spend per purchase

and the customers at the end of a period are those kept plus those newly won. So there are four ways to grow revenue: win new customers, keep more of the existing ones, sell to each customer more often, or raise what each purchase is worth through price or a richer mix. For Lantern’s catering the same tree reads clients × events per client × revenue per event. Choosing which branches to push is the first marketing decision, because each one leads to different objectives and different work. OpenStax describes the wider set of growth routes (selling more of existing products to existing markets, new products, new markets, or both new); course M10 covers them as the Ansoff matrix.

SMART marketing objectives

B01 introduced the SMART objective: specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. In marketing it means naming which customers, which behaviour and which measure, the level to reach from a stated starting point, and the date or period. Compare two versions of an objective for a fictional dental clinic:

  • Vague: “Attract more patients next year.”
  • SMART: “Raise new-patient registrations from 40 to 55 a month by June 2027, measured from the booking system, while keeping the share of patients who return within 12 months at 80% or more.”

The second version can be checked by anyone with the booking data, says by when, and builds in a second measure so that the first can’t be met by recruiting patients who never come back. The letters themselves vary between sources: OpenStax’s Principles of Marketing expands them as specific, measurable, attainable, realistic (or relevant) and time-bound in section 2.1, and as specific, measurable, actionable, realistic and time-bound in section 2.3. The questions behind the letters are what count.

Key idea: The anatomy of a marketing objective

Who (which customers) · does what (the behaviour) · measured how (the measure and its source) · from what to what (the starting point and the level) · by when (the date or period). If any part is missing, the objective can’t be checked.

In depth

Objectives across the funnel

Customers rarely go from never having heard of a business to buying from it in one step. The marketing funnel is a simple model of the stages they pass through, and marketers set objectives for each. It narrows because at each stage some people drop out.

Example objectives at each stage for Harrowby Payroll, a fictional payroll service for small firms
StageThe questionAn example objective
AwarenessDo the right buyers know we exist?35% of finance managers at firms with 10–50 staff in the region recognise the Harrowby name by December, in a survey
ConsiderationDo they think of us when they need the service?1,374 qualified leads and 412 proposals sent during the year
ConversionDo they choose us?At least 25% of proposals won, each quarter
RetentionDo they stay?At least 85% of the clients on 1 January still clients on 31 December
AdvocacyDo they recommend us?1 new client in 10 coming through a referral from an existing client

In a business market like Harrowby’s the middle of the funnel is usually described in sales terms: leads, qualified leads, proposals and wins. The figures in the table are not arbitrary; the challenging example works out where they come from. The funnel is a planning simplification, not a description of how everyone buys: the consumer-decisions lesson showed that real journeys loop back and skip stages. M02’s promotion lesson meets its older relatives, the hierarchy-of-effects models such as AIDA.

The funnel also sorts measures by timing. Revenue and wins are lagging indicators: they report outcomes after the fact. Awareness, enquiries and proposals are leading indicators: they move first and warn early if the year is going off course. A good set of objectives includes both, each tracked as a key performance indicator with an owner.

From a revenue goal to a customer target

The most important number in many marketing plans is the number of new customers the business must win. Working it out needs four inputs: the revenue target, the revenue each customer brings in a year, the customers at the start of the year, and the retention rate from the customer-value lesson: the share of customers at the start of a period who are still customers at its end. Its complement, 1 minus the retention rate, is the churn rate.

That form shows the trap. A business with 200 customers and 80% retention loses 200×0.20=40200 \times 0.20 = 40200×0.20=40 customers a year. To grow by 10% it must win 200×(1+0.10−0.80)=60200 \times (1 + 0.10 - 0.80) = 60200×(1+0.10−0.80)=60 new customers, of which 40 merely replace the ones it lost. Every percentage point of retention it adds saves 2 new customers (1% of 200).

Three assumptions sit behind the formula; state them whenever you use it. First, every customer brings in the same revenue, the average; if new customers buy less than established ones, more are needed. Second, customers join or leave at the start of the year, so each counts for a full year. Third, the retention rate holds, even though the plans meant to deliver the goal, a price rise for example, may change it.

The second assumption matters more than it looks. Suppose a business whose clients are each worth $3,000 a year needs $60,000 more revenue this year from new clients. If they all signed on 1 January it would need 60,000÷3,000=2060{,}000 \div 3{,}000 = 2060,000÷3,000=20. If they sign evenly through the year, each pays for about half the year, $1,500 on average, so it needs about 60,000÷1,500=4060{,}000 \div 1{,}500 = 4060,000÷1,500=40. Either define the target as the number of customers (or the revenue rate) at the end of the year, or allow for timing; never mix the two.

From new customers to the top of the funnel

Once the number of new customers is known, the funnel’s conversion rates say how much activity it takes. If a share c1c_1c1​ of leads receive a proposal and a share c2c_2c2​ of proposals are won, then each lead becomes a customer with probability c1c2c_1 c_2c1​c2​, and the leads needed are Nnew÷(c1c2)N_{\text{new}} \div (c_1 c_2)Nnew​÷(c1​c2​). The rates multiply: 30% of leads reaching a proposal and 25% of proposals won means 0.30×0.25=0.0750.30 \times 0.25 = 0.0750.30×0.25=0.075, or 7.5% of leads becoming clients, not 55% and not 25%. The same arithmetic works with any number of stages.

Multiply the new customers by the customer acquisition cost and you have the budget the objective implies, which the customer-value lesson taught you to compare with what a customer is worth. The funnel turns a revenue goal into a weekly workload. It also shows the choice every plan faces: the business can put more in at the top (more leads, which usually costs more promotion) or lose fewer on the way down (better conversion, which usually means a better offer or better selling). Setting the level

Targets are set in two directions, and good planning does both. Top-down, the target is derived from the goal, as above. Bottom-up, it is built from what the business has actually achieved: last year’s leads, win rate and retention, and what specific changes could plausibly improve them. If the two disagree, the gap is the most useful number in the plan. Either the plan names what will close it (a new offer, more salespeople, a higher budget) or the goal should be revised before the year starts, not after.

Two more checks make a target achievable. Compare it with the business’s own history and with what similar businesses manage; a win rate that has never been above 20% won’t reach 40% because a plan says so. And check capacity: growing catering by a quarter is pointless if there aren’t enough baristas to serve the extra events.

Objectives that work together

Objectives interact. A plan to raise prices by 10%, double the number of new customers won and cut the promotion budget by a third sets three objectives that pull against each other: the price rise and the smaller budget both make winning customers harder. Check every pair of objectives, as M02 checks every pair of marketing-mix decisions, and keep the set small: three to five objectives that a team can remember will be pursued; fifteen will be ignored.

Objectives also change behaviour, not always as intended. Goodhart’s law, met in B01, warns that a measure stops tracking what it was meant to track once people are rewarded for moving it. Reward a sales team for new clients alone and it may win them with discounts that destroy the margin, or sign clients who leave after three months. The remedy is a guardrail metric: a second measure that must not get worse, such as the average price won or the share of new clients still buying after a year. Finally, prefer objectives that track profit to ones that track revenue when the two can diverge: a customer won at a loss adds to revenue and subtracts from profit.

Caution: A target is not a forecast

A forecast says what is likely to happen; a target says what the business intends to make happen. Plans go wrong when a hopeful target is treated as a forecast, for example when stock, staff or cash are planned around it. Keep both numbers, and plan resources around the forecast, with the target as the stretch.

Common mistakes

  • “Our revenue target is our marketing objective.”

    Revenue is a business goal. It becomes actionable only when it is turned into customer behaviours: how many customers to win and keep, how often they buy, at what price. Without that step nobody knows what marketing is meant to do.

  • “To grow 10% we need 10% more customers.”

    Only if nobody leaves. With 80% retention, a business must first replace the 20% it loses, so 10% growth needs new customers equal to 30% of the starting base: three times as many. Always subtract the customers kept, not the customers you started with.

  • “The funnel rates add up.”

    Conversion rates multiply. If 30% of leads get a proposal and 25% of proposals are won, 7.5% of leads become clients. Adding (55%) or using only the last rate (25%) understates the leads needed several times over.

  • “Comparing this year with last year is always fair.”

    Only for equal periods. Lantern’s workshops ran for ten months in 2026; comparing a twelve-month target with a ten-month total makes the target look 43% higher when it is 19% above the pace. Annualise, or compare the same months.

  • “Hit the number, whatever it takes.”

    A single target invites gaming, as Goodhart’s law predicts: discounts that win clients who leave, classes filled at prices that lose money. Pair each acquisition target with a guardrail on price, margin or retention.

  • “More objectives give more control.”

    Long lists of objectives conflict with each other and are quietly ignored. Three to five, consistent with each other and each with an owner, steer a business better than fifteen.

In practice

Fictional case

Penhallow University, a fictional university, runs an online master’s degree for working professionals. Last year 150 fee-paying students enrolled. The university’s goal for next year is 20% more, 180 enrolments, and its admissions marketing director has to turn that goal into objectives and a budget.

She starts from the funnel, using last year’s rates from the admissions system: 25% of enquiries become applications, 60% of applications receive an offer, 50% of offers are accepted, and 80% of those who accept actually enrol (the rest, in admissions jargon, “melt” away over the summer). Multiplying the rates, each enquiry has a 0.25×0.60×0.50×0.80=0.060.25 \times 0.60 \times 0.50 \times 0.80 = 0.060.25×0.60×0.50×0.80=0.06 chance of becoming a student.

Top-down, 180 enrolments need 180÷0.06=3,000180 \div 0.06 = 3{,}000180÷0.06=3,000 enquiries. Last year’s 150 came from about 150÷0.06=2,500150 \div 0.06 = 2{,}500150÷0.06=2,500. One route is therefore 500 more enquiries, 20% more, which her agency prices at roughly 20% more advertising.

The second route works lower in the funnel. Accepted offers are the weak stage: half of the people the university wants to admit go elsewhere. If faster decisions, a scholarship deadline and calls from current students lifted acceptance from 50% to 60%, each enquiry’s chance would become 0.25×0.60×0.60×0.80=0.0720.25 \times 0.60 \times 0.60 \times 0.80 = 0.0720.25×0.60×0.60×0.80=0.072, and 180÷0.072=2,500180 \div 0.072 = 2{,}500180÷0.072=2,500 enquiries, last year’s number, would be enough.

She proposes a mix, because neither route is certain: an enquiry objective of 2,750 (10% more than last year), an acceptance objective of 55% of offers, and a melt objective of no more than 20%. With those rates, 2,750×0.25×0.60×0.55×0.80=181.52{,}750 \times 0.25 \times 0.60 \times 0.55 \times 0.80 = 181.52,750×0.25×0.60×0.55×0.80=181.5 enrolments, just over the goal. Each objective has an owner: marketing for enquiries, admissions for acceptance, student services for melt.

She also sets a guardrail. The easiest way to hit 180 would be to make more offers to weaker applicants, which would raise the offer rate and lower completion rates a year later. So the offer rate is fixed by academic standards, not by marketing, and the share of students who complete their first year is tracked alongside enrolments. The same pattern applies in any business with a funnel, from a software firm to a coffee caterer: derive the volume from the goal, find the weakest stage, set an objective for each lever with an owner, and protect the measure that the targets could damage.

Summary

  • A business goal (more revenue, profit or share) becomes actionable only when translated into marketing objectives: what customers must do, how much and by when.
  • Goals, objectives, strategies and tactics form a chain; each level is judged by whether it delivers the one above.
  • Revenue grows through new customers, retention, purchase frequency or spend per purchase; the choice decides which objectives to set.
  • A SMART marketing objective names the customers, the behaviour, the measure, the starting point and level, and the date.
  • New customers needed = customers required − customers kept; with unchanged revenue per customer, N₀(1 + g − r). Round up only at the end, and state the timing assumption.
  • Funnel conversion rates multiply: the volume needed at the top is the new customers divided by the product of the rates.
  • Good objectives are few, consistent with each other, checked top-down and bottom-up, and protected by guardrail metrics against gaming.

Terms introduced

Business goal
What a business as a whole aims to achieve, usually in money or size, such as more revenue, more profit or a larger market share.
Marketing objective
A statement of what customers must do, how much and by when, for a business goal to be met, such as the number of customers to win or keep, with a measure, a level and a date.
Marketing strategy
How a business intends to reach its marketing objectives: which customers it will serve and how it will compete for them.
Marketing funnel
A model of the stages customers pass through, from awareness through consideration and conversion to retention and advocacy, with fewer people at each stage.
Win rate
The share of proposals, quotes or bids that turn into sales.
