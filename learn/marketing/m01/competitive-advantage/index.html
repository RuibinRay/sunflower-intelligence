Competitive advantage

Customers choose between alternatives, so a business needs a reason to be chosen that rivals can’t easily copy. Learn where competitive advantage comes from (lower cost, differentiation and focus), how to compare competing on price with competing on value, and how to judge whether an advantage will last.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain competitive advantage as creating more value than rivals, using willingness to pay, price and cost.
  • Distinguish Porter’s generic strategies (cost leadership, differentiation, cost focus and differentiation focus) and classify businesses by the strategy they follow.
  • Calculate a price premium and the highest price a difference in customer value can support against a rival.
  • Compare competing on price with competing on value, including the risks of price wars and of being stuck in the middle.
  • Evaluate whether a resource or capability can give a sustained advantage, using VRIO.
  • Explain how positioning communicates a competitive advantage to target customers.

Review first

  • Customer value, satisfaction and lifetime value — Competitive advantage is built on customer value: what customers get compared with what they give up, judged against the alternatives.
  • Situation analysis — The choice of how to compete starts from the competitor analysis and the SWOT.
  • Marketing objectives — A competitive strategy is the “how” that must deliver the objectives set in that lesson.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern charges about $9.00 a head for catered coffee; Brewline Catering, a larger caterer that uses self-serve urns and pod machines and no baristas, charges $7.50. In 2026 Lantern lost 5 bids, all to Brewline on price. Yet when Ana interviewed 12 of her clients, 9 named coffee quality as a reason for choosing Lantern, 7 its reliability on the day and 6 its baristas’ professionalism; only 2 named price.

Two businesses are selling coffee for the same office events in very different ways. Which way should Lantern compete in 2027? This lesson gives you the tools to answer; the course project asks you to choose and defend the answer.

A business wins customers only if they prefer it to the alternatives. A competitive advantage is whatever lets a business attract customers, earn profits or both, better than its rivals can. OpenStax’s Principles of Management describes it by its results: a firm with an advantage attracts more customers, earns more profit or returns more value to its owners than its rivals. This lesson looks at where those results come from.

Where advantage comes from: value created

Every sale involves three amounts. A customer’s willingness to pay, met in the first lesson of this course, is, here, the most they would pay for an offer rather than go without it. The price is what they actually pay. The cost is what it costs the business to provide the offer to them.

The gap between willingness to pay and cost is the value created by the sale, and the price splits it: the customer keeps willingness to pay minus price, and the business keeps price minus cost. Rival offers come in through a comparison: the customer looks at what each leaves them, willingness to pay minus price, and takes the largest.

A business has a competitive advantage when it creates more value than its rivals: a wider gap between what customers will pay and what it costs to serve them. That gap can be widened from either end. Holding willingness to pay at $10, a business that serves the same customer for $4 instead of $5 creates $6 of value. Alternatively, a business whose offer customers would pay $12 for creates more value even if the better offer costs a little more to provide: $12 − $6 = $6. The first route is a cost advantage; the second is differentiation.

This way of seeing advantage, associated with Adam Brandenburger and Harborne Stuart’s value-based view of strategy, makes one thing clear at once: a low price is not an advantage in itself. A price cut only moves value from the business to the customer. It wins customers, but unless the business also has lower costs than its rivals, they can match the cut, and both end up with less.

Three generic strategies

In Competitive Strategy (1980), Michael Porter argued that firms outperform their rivals in one of three broad ways, which he called generic strategies: overall cost leadership, differentiation and focus. In Competitive Advantage (1985) he made the structure explicit as two questions: is the advantage lower cost or uniqueness, and is it pursued across the whole market or in a narrow segment? That gives four positions, because focus can be pursued through either cost (cost focus) or differentiation (differentiation focus).

Lantern and Brewline illustrate the contrast without settling it. Brewline’s urns and pod machines need no baristas, which suggests it is built to serve each head for less. Lantern’s baristas are a cost Brewline doesn’t carry, and they are among the things Lantern’s current clients say they value. Whether that adds up to an advantage for either firm, and in which part of the market, depends on facts the next sections teach you to look for.

Key idea: Advantage is relative and customer-judged

An advantage exists only compared with specific rivals, and only in things customers care about. Low cost that customers can’t see still counts, because it lets the business price lower or earn more; uniqueness that customers don’t value counts for nothing.

Where do lower costs and valued differences come from? The usual sources are worth listing, because a strategy is only as credible as the source it rests on.

  • Sources of lower cost: scale (spreading fixed costs such as equipment, vans and management over more customers); experience (staff who have done the job many times work faster and waste less); a simpler, standardised offer; better processes and technology; cheaper or more reliable inputs; and location.
  • Sources of differentiation: the product itself (features, quality, reliability); the service around it (speed, flexibility, expertise); the people who deliver it; the brand and what it signals; the channel (being easier to buy from); and relationships built over time.

Each source has a cost side and a customer side. Scale lowers cost only if the business can win the volume; a feature differentiates only if customers value it more than it costs. And the sources interact: standardising an offer to cut costs usually narrows the differences customers can be offered, which is one reason Porter thought firms must choose.

OpenStax’s Principles of Management uses well-known examples: Walmart for cost leadership, built on low prices from suppliers, inexpensive stores and relatively low wages; Starbucks for differentiation, whose customers pay premium prices for its atmosphere, service, quality and brand; and a maker of custom snowboard bindings for a small group of riders willing to pay premium prices as differentiation focus. Notice that cost leadership is not the same as the lowest price. A cost leader may charge average prices and earn above-average margins; low cost gives it the choice.

Porter warned that a firm that tries to pursue cost leadership and differentiation at once, without achieving either, ends up stuck in the middle: too expensive for price-led buyers and not distinctive enough for buyers who pay for quality. Later researchers qualified the warning. Charles Hill (1988) argued that differentiation and low cost need not be opposites, and that in some circumstances a firm can achieve both. One route is easy to see: a differentiated product that wins a large market may gain the economies of scale that lower its costs. Treat the generic strategies as a way of forcing a clear choice about where the advantage lies, not as a law that forbids combinations.

Competing on price and competing on value

A differentiator charges a price premium: the amount by which its price exceeds a rival’s, usually expressed as a percentage of the rival’s price. A premium is sustainable when customers value the difference by at least as much as it costs them.

The logic follows from the value stick. A customer compares what each option leaves them: willingness to pay minus price. If a rival leaves its customers $40 of value, a business whose offer customers would pay $80 more for can charge up to $80 more than the rival and still leave them as well off. In general, the highest price that still wins the customer is the rival’s price plus the difference in willingness to pay.

Three practical consequences follow. First, a differentiator should know, in money, what its difference is worth to the customer: time saved, fewer problems, staff who are happier. The simple worked example below does this for a laundry. Second, the premium must also cover the extra cost of providing the difference, or differentiation destroys value instead of creating it.

Third, willingness to pay varies between customers. Some value the difference highly and will pay the premium; others barely value it and will choose the cheaper rival. Which customers to serve is therefore part of the strategy, and is where focus comes in.

Competing on price works when the business has a genuine cost advantage, because then it can price below rivals and still earn a margin they can’t match. Without one, a price cut is easy to copy, and a rival that matches it leaves both firms with the same share of customers and less margin each. Repeated rounds of cutting are a price war. A differentiator that cuts its price to match a cheaper rival gives away margin and tells customers that its difference wasn’t worth paying for. That doesn’t mean a differentiator should never move its price; it means price moves should follow from what customers value and what the business can afford, which M02’s pricing lessons develop.

Will the advantage last?

An advantage that rivals can copy next month is worth little. The resource-based view of strategy asks what the business has, its resources (equipment, money, brands, relationships, people) and capabilities (what it can do with them), that rivals cannot easily get.

Jay Barney (1991) proposed that a resource can give a sustained competitive advantage if it is valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable and without a ready substitute. That list is often abbreviated VRIN. Barney later recast it as VRIO, folding substitutes into imitability and adding organisation (Barney, 1995, ‘Looking inside for competitive advantage’, Academy of Management Executive 9(4), 49–61). VRIO is the version now usually taught, including in OpenStax’s Principles of Management: four questions asked of each resource or capability in turn.

The VRIO questions and what the answers usually imply
QuestionIf the answer is no
Value: does it let the business exploit an opportunity or counter a threat, in ways customers care about?It gives no advantage, and it may be a cost.
Rarity: do few competitors have it?It is needed just to compete: competitive parity.
Imitability: is it hard or costly for rivals to copy or substitute?The advantage is temporary: it lasts until rivals catch up.
Organisation: is the business organised (processes, people, incentives) to make use of it?The advantage is there but unused.

Only a resource that passes all four tests is a likely source of sustained advantage.

Why are some resources hard to copy? Barney’s paper gives three reasons. Some were built through a particular history that can’t be repeated, such as a reputation earned over decades; some work in ways that even the business can’t fully explain, so rivals don’t know what to copy; and some are socially complex, living in relationships, trust and culture rather than in equipment anyone can buy. A coffee machine is easy to copy; a team whose skill, and whose relationships with clients, were built over years of work together is not.

Two cautions apply. VRIO is a structured set of questions, not a measurement: the answers are judgements, and the honest ones often say “not yet”. And no advantage lasts for ever. Customers’ needs change, technologies arrive and rivals learn; a business must keep asking which of its advantages are eroding and which it is building next.

Positioning: making the advantage visible

An advantage that customers don’t perceive wins no business. Positioning is the place a business wants its offer to occupy in target customers’ minds, relative to competing offers. It is how a competitive strategy is expressed to customers, and the value proposition from the customer-value lesson is its promise in words: the cheapest reliable option, the one with the best coffee at the event, the specialist who understands this kind of client.

A good position is relevant to the target customers, distinct from what rivals claim, credible given what the business actually delivers, and consistent across everything customers see, from the proposal to the price. OpenStax’s Principles of Management notes that a strong position differs from rivals’ positions and is hard to copy, which is why positioning and sustainability go together. M02 shows how the marketing mix carries a positioning out, and course M05 teaches how to choose and test one, with perceptual maps and positioning statements.

Common mistakes

  • “Competitive advantage means being the cheapest.”

    Advantage means creating more value than rivals: a wider gap between what customers will pay and what it costs to serve them. A dearer firm can have the advantage, as Orchard Works does. A low price is an advantage only when it rests on lower costs; otherwise it just hands value to customers until a rival matches it.

  • “Differentiation means being different.”

    Only differences that customers value, and value by more than they cost to provide, count. A feature customers don’t notice adds cost and no willingness to pay. Put a money value on the difference for each kind of customer, as in the laundry example.

  • “If a cheaper rival takes our customers, we should match its price.”

    Without a cost advantage, matching gives away margin on every customer, invites another cut, and tells customers the difference wasn’t worth paying for. First ask which customers are leaving and what the difference is worth to them. The answer may be to cut costs, to add value or to concentrate on the customers who value the difference; it can’t be found by looking at the price alone.

  • “We can be the best and the cheapest for everyone.”

    Porter called the result of trying to be everything “stuck in the middle”. Hill (1988) argued that cost and differentiation can sometimes be combined; if you plan to do both, name the mechanism, such as the scale a popular differentiated product can bring, rather than wishing.

  • “Anything we own or do well is a lasting advantage.”

    VRIO says otherwise. A resource every rival has gives parity; one rivals can copy gives a temporary lead; and even a rare, hard-to-copy resource is wasted if the business isn’t organised to use it.

  • “Customers will notice our advantage.”

    They notice what is communicated consistently and what they experience. Positioning has to make the advantage visible and believable to the target customers, and every part of the marketing mix has to support it.

In practice

Strategists rarely meet a choice as clean as the generic-strategy grid suggests. A useful discipline, before choosing, is to write down for each option what would have to be true for it to work, and then to check which of those things the evidence supports. Applied to Lantern’s position against Brewline, the method organises the question without answering it; answering it is the course project.

Fictional case

The facts: Lantern charges about $9.00 a head and Brewline $7.50. Brewline is larger and uses self-serve urns and pod machines with no baristas. Lantern lost 5 bids in 2026, all to Brewline on price. Its current clients, interviewed in November, mostly named quality, reliability and the baristas as reasons for choosing it. Ana wants catering revenue to grow, and the objectives lesson showed that growth will need new clients as well as kept ones.

Lantern against Brewline: what each route would require Ana to believe (questions, not answers)
RouteWhat would have to be trueWhat Ana doesn’t yet know
Compete on price across the marketLantern can serve a head for less than Brewline, or could find a way toBrewline’s costs; what Lantern would have to give up to cut its own; how Brewline would respond
Differentiate across the marketEnough buyers value baristas and better coffee by more than the price differenceHow much the difference is worth to buyers who chose Brewline; whether they could be persuaded
Focus on a segmentThere is an identifiable group of buyers who value what Lantern does most, and it is large enough for the growth Ana wantsWho those buyers are, how many there are, and how to reach them

Each route raises its own analysis. A price route needs the cost side of the value stick: what it costs Lantern to serve a head, and whether a smaller firm could get below a larger one. A differentiation route needs the willingness-to-pay side: what an hour of barista service and better coffee are worth to an office manager or to the people who attend the event, and whether that exceeds the price gap. A focus route needs segmentation evidence: which clients the interviews describe, and which buyers the lost bids describe. All three need a VRIO check on whatever the advantage would rest on: can Brewline copy it?

The same discipline works far from coffee. A regional airline facing a low-cost carrier, a high-street bookshop facing an online retailer, or a payroll firm facing free software all face the choice between matching on price, out-valuing the rival, or serving the customers the rival serves badly. The answer is rarely “all three”, and it is never found by looking only at the price. Write the options down, list what each needs to be true, gather the evidence, and choose the route the evidence supports and the business can sustain.

Summary

  • A business has a competitive advantage when it creates more value than its rivals: a wider gap between customers’ willingness to pay and its cost of serving them.
  • The price only divides the value created between customer and business; a price cut without a cost advantage is easily matched and destroys margin.
  • Porter’s generic strategies: cost leadership, differentiation, cost focus and differentiation focus. Trying to be everything risks being stuck in the middle, though combinations can work through a clear mechanism.
  • A price premium is sustainable up to the gap in willingness to pay: the highest winning price is the rival’s price plus that gap, and the premium must also cover the extra cost of the difference.
  • Willingness to pay differs between customers, so choosing whom to serve is part of the strategy.
  • VRIO tests whether an advantage will last: valuable, rare, hard to imitate, and a business organised to use it.
  • Positioning makes the advantage visible to target customers; M05 develops it.

Terms introduced

Competitive advantage
Whatever lets a business attract customers or earn profits better than its rivals; in value terms, creating a wider gap than rivals between customers’ willingness to pay and the cost of serving them. It may be temporary or sustained.
Value created
The customer’s willingness to pay minus the business’s cost of serving them; the price divides it between the customer and the business.
Generic strategies
Michael Porter’s broad ways of outperforming rivals: cost leadership, differentiation and focus, with focus pursued through either cost or differentiation.
Cost leadership
A generic strategy of achieving the lowest cost of serving customers in an industry, so that the business can charge lower prices or earn higher margins than its rivals.
Differentiation
A generic strategy of being unique in ways customers value, so that they will pay a premium price.
Focus
A generic strategy of serving one narrow segment better than broad competitors, through lower cost (cost focus) or differentiation (differentiation focus).
Stuck in the middle
Porter’s term for a firm that pursues low cost and differentiation at once without achieving either, and so has no clear advantage.
Price premium
The amount by which a firm’s price exceeds a rival’s for a comparable offer, usually as a percentage of the rival’s price.
Sustained competitive advantage
An advantage that rivals cannot easily copy or substitute, so that it lasts after they have tried.
VRIO
Four questions for judging whether a resource or capability can give a sustained advantage: is it valuable, rare, hard to imitate, and is the business organised to exploit it?
Positioning
The place a business wants its offer to occupy in target customers’ minds, relative to competing offers.
