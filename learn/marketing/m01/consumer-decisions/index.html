How consumers make buying decisions

Every purchase, from a bag of beans to a barista course, comes out of a decision, made in seconds or over weeks. Learn the five stages of the buying process, how involvement changes it, how buyers weigh alternatives (with a scoring model you can calculate), what happens after the sale, and where the tidy model breaks down, online and off.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Describe the five stages of the consumer buying process and what a marketer can do at each one.
  • Distinguish high- from low-involvement purchases and classify buying behaviour as complex, dissonance-reducing, habitual or variety-seeking.
  • Calculate weighted-attribute scores for competing offers and the improvement an offer needs to draw level with the leader.
  • Explain post-purchase dissonance and how sellers can reduce it.
  • Evaluate the limits of linear decision models, including the way online journeys loop, skip stages and add options.

Review first

  • What marketing is — The lesson uses the difference between a need and a want.
  • Customer value, satisfaction and lifetime value — Post-purchase evaluation compares what the buyer got with what they expected, the idea of satisfaction from that lesson.
  • Multiplying and adding decimals, for example 0.5 × 9 + 0.2 × 5.

The idea

Fictional case

By the end of 2026 Lantern Coffee Co. sells to three kinds of buyer. 18 offices and event organisers book its catering. People book places on its barista workshops: 14 so far this year, with about 12 people at each, at $125 a place. And about 1,500 customers have bought beans, the Cold Brew Pack or gift boxes from its online shop. Every name and figure in this case is invented for teaching.

Fictional case

Ana Lim has noticed that these buyers decide in very different ways. Some online customers reorder the same 250 g bag every month without a second look; others spend a week over a $58 gift box; and many people who read the workshop page never book.

This lesson is about the second and third groups: individuals and households buying for their own use, the business-to-consumer side of Lantern. Organisations buy to run their operations or to resell, and the last lesson of this module covers how they buy. Remember from the first lesson that the customer, who chooses and pays, is not always the consumer, who uses the product: someone buying a gift box is the customer, and the person who drinks the coffee is the consumer. Consumer behaviour is the study of how people choose, buy, use and dispose of products, and why. Marketers study it for a practical reason: they can influence a choice only at the moments when it is being made, so they need to know what those moments are.

The five-stage buying process

Introductory textbooks describe a considered purchase as a sequence of five stages, the buying decision process: need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, the purchase decision and post-purchase behaviour. The process starts well before anything is bought and carries on after the sale.

Here is the process in one purchase.

Fictional case

Nadia Farouk was given an espresso machine for her birthday. A month later her milk still comes out as flat foam, while a friend posts photographs of perfect latte art. The gap between what she has and what she wants is need recognition. She first thinks about what she already knows: a barista once poured hearts in her coffee at a conference (internal search). Then she searches online, reads reviews and asks the friend (external search).

Fictional case

She ends up with three options: Lantern’s three-hour Saturday workshop at $125, with hands-on practice in a small group; CremaClass, an online video course at $39; and a six-evening course at Calder College for $210. She weighs them on hands-on practice, price and timing (evaluation of alternatives) and chooses Lantern, but the first Saturday on offer clashes with a wedding, so she books the next one (purchase decision).

Fictional case

Afterwards she compares the workshop with what she expected, posts her first rosetta and, when Lantern’s follow-up email arrives with a practice guide, stops wondering whether the online course would have done (post-purchase behaviour). Nadia, CremaClass and Calder College are fictional.

Involvement: how much the decision matters

Involvement is how much a purchase matters to the buyer: how personally relevant it is, how much is at stake and how interested they are in the category. What is at stake has several sides: money, whether the product will work, what other people will think, and the time lost if the choice turns out wrong. When involvement is high, buyers work through every stage with care; when it is low, they skip most of them.

Involvement belongs to the buyer and the situation, not to the product alone. For Nadia a $125 workshop is a considered choice; for a café owner booking the same workshop for a new employee it may be a routine booking. A bag of beans is a habit for many of Lantern’s online customers and a subject of research for an enthusiast comparing farms and roast dates. A gift raises involvement for almost anyone, because a poor choice reflects on the giver.

The idea has a long history in advertising research. Herbert Krugman (1965) argued that much television advertising works on viewers who are barely paying attention. Rather than persuading them with arguments, repetition gradually shifts which ideas about a product come to mind first, and a shopper may form a clear opinion only after buying and trying it. That reverses the textbook order, in which people evaluate first and buy afterwards.

Key idea: Ask which decision your buyer is making

Before planning any marketing, ask how involved the target buyer is. A high-involvement buyer wants information, comparisons and reassurance. A low-involvement buyer wants the product to be easy to find, easy to recognise and easy to buy again.

In depth

Stage by stage

Need recognition. A purchase begins when a buyer notices a gap between their actual state and a desired state. The trigger can be internal (hunger, shoes that have worn through) or external (an advertisement, a friend’s photographs, the smell from a bakery). Marketers can prompt recognition of a gap the buyer already has, for example with a reorder email to a customer who bought a bag a few weeks ago and may be running low. What they cannot do for long is invent a need the buyer doesn’t have; what they shape is the want, the particular way of meeting the need, as the first lesson of this course explained.

Information search. Internal search draws on memory: past purchases and brands the buyer already knows. External search draws on four kinds of source: personal (family, friends, colleagues), commercial (advertising, websites, packaging, salespeople), public (independent reviews, ratings sites, the media) and experiential (handling, tasting or trying the product). Because personal and public sources don’t come from the seller, buyers often treat them as more credible than commercial ones, which is why the next lesson spends time on word of mouth. Search is longer when involvement and risk are high, when the buyer knows little about the category and when the options differ a lot; it is shorter under time pressure or after a good past experience.

Search also narrows the field. Of all the options that exist, a buyer knows only some, the awareness set, and seriously considers fewer still: the consideration set, sometimes called the evoked set. An option that isn’t in the consideration set when the buyer chooses can’t be chosen, however good it is, which is why so much marketing aims at nothing more than being considered.

Evaluation of alternatives. Buyers judge options on a few attributes that matter to them, such as price, quality, convenience or style, and they care about some attributes more than others. OpenStax describes this as consumers viewing a product as a “bundle of product attributes”. Marketers model the judgement with a weighted-attribute model: give each attribute an importance weight, score each option on each attribute, multiply and add. The option with the highest total is the one the model expects the buyer to prefer; the formula is below. The model is compensatory because strength on one attribute can make up for weakness on another: CremaClass’s low price partly compensates for its lack of hands-on practice.

Not every buyer compensates. Under a non-compensatory rule a weakness can’t be offset. A conjunctive rule sets cut-offs that every option must pass (nothing over $2,200; nothing without a warranty), and only the options that pass all of them are compared. A lexicographic rule picks the best option on the most important attribute and looks at the next attribute only to break a tie. Buyers also use shortcuts: a higher price taken as a sign of higher quality, the familiar brand bought again, a country of origin trusted for a product. Low-involvement buyers lean heavily on such shortcuts.

Purchase decision. An intention to buy is not yet a purchase. Two things can come between them: other people’s attitudes (a partner who thinks $125 is too much for a coffee lesson) and unexpected situational factors (a date clash, a sold-out session, a delivery charge that appears only at the checkout). The decision also has parts: where to buy, when, how much and how to pay. Online, every extra step in the checkout is a chance for the intention to fade, which is why sellers watch the share of baskets that are abandoned.

Post-purchase behaviour. After buying, the customer compares what they got with what they expected. That comparison decides customer satisfaction, as the lesson on customer value set out, and satisfaction decides whether they buy again, what they tell other people and what they write in reviews. The process therefore loops: today’s experience becomes part of the information search for the next purchase, by this customer and by everyone who reads their review.

Post-purchase dissonance

Even a satisfied buyer can feel uneasy. Post-purchase dissonance is the discomfort a buyer feels after a choice when they doubt it was the right one, often because the options they turned down had attractive features of their own. The idea comes from the psychologist Leon Festinger’s theory of cognitive dissonance (1957): people dislike holding conflicting thoughts, such as “I chose this” and “the other one might have been better”, and look for ways to reduce the conflict. In everyday speech it is called buyer’s remorse.

Dissonance is most likely when the purchase is important or expensive, hard to reverse, and the alternatives were close. Buyers reduce it by seeking reassurance (reading good reviews of what they bought) or by returning the product. Sellers can help, and have good reason to, because an uneasy customer is less likely to buy again: confirm the choice with useful follow-up, offer guarantees and easy returns, and above all don’t promise more than the product delivers, because an inflated expectation guarantees a disappointment. Dissonance is not the same as dissatisfaction. Nadia enjoyed her workshop and still wondered, briefly, whether the cheaper course would have done.

Four kinds of buying behaviour

Combining involvement with how much the brands differ, as the buyer sees them, gives a common classification of buying behaviour.

High involvementComplex buying: extensive search and careful evaluation. A car, a university course, a first espresso machine.Dissonance-reducing buying: a fairly quick choice among similar options, then doubts. Floor tiles; a sofa from several similar ranges.

The matrix is usually credited to Henry Assael, Consumer Behavior and Marketing Action (third edition, 1987); the labels follow OpenStax, section 3.1. The examples are illustrations: where a purchase falls depends on the buyer.

Each cell calls for different marketing. Complex buyers want detailed information and fair comparisons. Dissonance-reducing buyers want reassurance after the sale.

Habitual buyers are held by availability, a recognisable pack and a reminder at the right moment; a challenger has to break the habit with a reason to try something new, such as a sample or a trial price. Variety-seekers respond to new flavours and limited editions, and a market leader can keep them by offering variety within its own range. The four types are tendencies, not fixed labels: the same person can buy beans out of habit for the office and with great care as a gift.

Online journeys

Shopping online did not abolish the stages, but it changed how people move through them. Searching costs little, strangers’ reviews are a click away, comparison sites lay options side by side, and many people research on a phone while standing in a shop. Each point at which a buyer meets a brand, from a search result to a delivery text message, is a touchpoint, and the whole sequence of touchpoints and decisions, from first noticing a need to the experience after buying, is the customer journey.

In 2009 four McKinsey consultants, David Court, Dave Elzinga, Susan Mulder and Ole Jørgen Vetvik, studied the purchase decisions of almost 20,000 consumers in five industries on three continents. They argued that the funnel, in which buyers start with many brands and steadily narrow them down, misses how people actually buy. They described instead a circular journey with four phases: initial consideration, active evaluation, closure (the moment of purchase), and the post-purchase experience, which feeds a loyalty loop into the next decision.

Buyers in their study often added brands during evaluation: car shoppers, for example, added an average of 2.2 brands to an initial set of 3.8. And they reported that two-thirds of the touchpoints during active evaluation were driven by consumers, such as reviews and word of mouth, rather than pushed by companies.

For a seller this means three things. Missing the initial consideration set is no longer fatal, because a brand can still be found during evaluation, but only if search results, reviews and comparison sites show it at that moment. What customers say after buying is part of the next buyer’s search. And the post-purchase experience is not the end of the job; it starts the next journey.

The limits of linear models

The five stages are a useful way to organise thinking, not a description of every purchase. Real decisions depart from the model in at least four ways.

  • Stages are skipped. A habitual buyer goes from need recognition straight to purchase. Nothing is searched or evaluated because nothing needs to be.
  • Stages come in a different order. An impulse buyer buys first and evaluates afterwards. Free samples let people try a product before they have searched at all, and Krugman’s low-involvement viewers form opinions after buying.
  • Stages repeat. Buyers loop back from evaluation to search when a new option appears or a review raises a doubt, and an online journey can stretch over weeks of visits.
  • The model assumes deliberate reasoning by one person. Many choices rest on habit, emotion, mood and the shortcuts and biases covered in the next lesson, and many are made by couples, families or groups of friends.

Caution: A checklist, not a law

Use the five stages to ask where a buyer could be lost and what would help them at that point. Don’t use them to predict what a particular person will do, and don’t assume buyers really calculate weighted scores. The model describes the trade-offs people make; its weights and scores have to come from asking real buyers, which M04 Market Research teaches.

Common mistakes

  • “Every purchase goes through all five stages.”

    Only considered, high-involvement purchases do. A habitual buyer goes from need recognition straight to purchase; an impulse buyer buys first and evaluates afterwards. Ask which kind of decision your buyer is making before deciding where to spend effort.

  • “The decision ends at the checkout.”

    Post-purchase behaviour decides repeat purchases, word of mouth and reviews, and reviews feed other people’s searches. A seller who stops at the sale gives up the stage that shapes the next one.

  • “Involvement is a property of the product.”

    It depends on the buyer and the situation. The same bag of beans is a routine purchase for one customer, a hobby for another and a nerve-racking gift for a third.

  • “The highest weighted score is what people will buy.”

    The model describes trade-offs; it isn’t a calculation buyers perform. Its result depends on whose weights you use, and a non-compensatory cut-off (a budget limit, a missing feature) can rule out the top-scoring option before any trade-off is made.

  • “Online buyers move down a funnel that only narrows.”

    Court and colleagues (2009) found buyers adding brands during evaluation, and most touchpoints at that stage were driven by consumers rather than companies. A brand can be added late, and can be dropped late, on the strength of a review.

  • “Buyer’s remorse means the product was bad.”

    Dissonance is doubt about the choice, not dissatisfaction with the product. It is most likely after important, hard-to-reverse choices between close options, and reassurance, guarantees and honest expectations reduce it.

In practice

Fictional case

Holloway Home, a fictional online furniture retailer, sells sofas from $900 to $2,400. A sofa is a high-involvement purchase: expensive, bulky to send back and seen by every visitor to the buyer’s home. Its customer-experience manager, Ines Dunmore, maps the customer journey for sofas stage by stage, using the website’s data and interviews with 40 recent buyers and 40 people who left without buying.

Her map turns each stage into a question. At information search, buyers can’t touch the fabric, so many order free swatches; she cuts swatch delivery from a week to two days, because a buyer waiting for swatches is a buyer still searching elsewhere. At evaluation, interviewees compare three or four sofas on comfort, size, fabric, price and delivery time, so the site’s comparison page lets visitors filter on the attributes they care about most: a weighted-attribute model in practical form.

The purchase decision is where the data point. Each month about 2,000 shopping baskets contain a sofa and about 1,400 of them are abandoned, 70%, leaving about 600 orders. The interviews explain much of it: the delivery date appeared only at the last step of the checkout, and buyers who discovered it was six weeks away left. Ines has the delivery date shown on every product page and in the basket.

Over the next two months abandonment averages 64%, so about 2,000 × 0.36 = 720 baskets become orders, 120 more a month. At an average order of about $1,500, that is roughly $180,000 a month of extra sales. Before claiming the credit she checks against a baseline, as B01 taught: the same months last year, and the abandonment rate for chairs and tables, whose pages did not change. Her measure, the share of baskets that become orders, is a cousin of the conversion rate B01 defined on visits, so she states its denominator: baskets, not visits.

At post-purchase, interviews show that buyers worry most in the weeks between paying and delivery, when they can’t yet sit on the sofa and keep seeing others they might have bought. Holloway now sends a note after payment with the delivery date, care instructions for the chosen fabric and a reminder of the returns policy. It is an attempt to reduce post-purchase dissonance, and Ines will judge it by the share of orders cancelled before delivery.

The same map works anywhere a decision takes time. A university can follow applicants from enquiry to enrolment and ask where they drop out; a hotel can follow guests from searching to booking to reviewing. Lantern can do it for its workshops: the applied example found a timing problem in evaluation, and the same method would show whether people are lost later, at a clumsy booking form or a session that is already full.

Summary

  • Considered purchases pass through five stages: need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, the purchase decision and post-purchase behaviour. Each is a point where a marketer can help or lose the buyer.
  • Involvement, how much a purchase matters to the buyer, decides how much of the process is used. It depends on the buyer and the situation as well as the product.
  • Combining involvement with perceived brand differences gives four kinds of buying behaviour: complex, dissonance-reducing, habitual and variety-seeking.
  • The weighted-attribute model multiplies each score by its importance weight and adds them up. Different groups of buyers have different weights, and non-compensatory cut-offs can rule an option out before any trade-off.
  • The gap formula shows how much one attribute must improve to draw level with the leader; the heaviest-weighted attribute needs the smallest rise, but cost matters too.
  • Post-purchase dissonance is doubt after an important choice between close options; reassurance, guarantees and honest expectations reduce it.
  • Online journeys loop, add options during evaluation and depend on consumer-driven touchpoints such as reviews. The five stages are a checklist for finding where buyers are lost, not a law of behaviour.

Terms introduced

Buying decision process
The five stages a consumer goes through in a considered purchase: need recognition, information search, evaluation of alternatives, purchase decision and post-purchase behaviour. Routine purchases skip stages.
Need recognition
The first stage of the buying decision process: the buyer notices a gap between their actual state and a desired state, prompted by an internal or external trigger.
Information search
The stage in which a buyer looks for information about ways to meet a need, from memory (internal search) or from personal, commercial, public and experiential sources (external search).
Consideration set
The options a buyer seriously considers when choosing, a subset of the options they know about; also called the evoked set.
Weighted-attribute model
A compensatory model of choice: each option’s score on each attribute is multiplied by the attribute’s importance weight and the results are added. The option with the highest total is the one the buyer is expected to prefer.
Non-compensatory rule
A choice rule under which a weakness on one attribute can’t be made up by strength on another, such as a cut-off every option must pass (conjunctive) or choosing the best option on the most important attribute (lexicographic).
Post-purchase dissonance
Discomfort after a purchase when the buyer doubts the choice, often because the rejected options had attractive features; also called buyer’s remorse.
Involvement
How much a purchase matters to the buyer: its personal relevance, what is at stake and the buyer’s interest in the category. It depends on the buyer and the situation as well as the product.
Complex buying behaviour
Buying with high involvement among brands that differ significantly: extensive search and careful evaluation.
Dissonance-reducing buying behaviour
Buying with high involvement among brands that seem alike: a fairly quick choice, often followed by doubts the buyer then tries to reduce.
Habitual buying behaviour
Buying with low involvement among brands that seem alike: routine repurchase with little search or evaluation.
Variety-seeking buying behaviour
Buying with low involvement among brands that differ: switching brands for a change rather than from dissatisfaction.
Touchpoint
Any point at which a customer meets a brand, such as an advertisement, a search result, a review, a website, a salesperson or a delivery message.
Customer journey
The whole sequence of touchpoints and decisions a customer goes through, from first noticing a need to the experience after the purchase.
