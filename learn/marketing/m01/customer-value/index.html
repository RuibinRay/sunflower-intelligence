Customer value, satisfaction and lifetime value

Customers choose the offer they judge best value, stay if it meets the expectations they formed, and are worth what they bring in over the years they stay. Learn customer-perceived value, satisfaction and loyalty, how to calculate a simple customer lifetime value with its assumptions stated, how to compare it with the cost of winning a customer, and how to write a value proposition.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain customer-perceived value as benefits weighed against money and non-money costs, and why the customer’s judgement is the one that counts.
  • Explain satisfaction as perceived performance measured against expectations, and how it relates to loyalty and retention.
  • Calculate a customer’s lifetime value from margin, retention and a discount rate, stating the assumptions about timing and horizon.
  • Compare lifetime value with the cost of acquiring a customer to judge whether acquisition or retention spending pays.
  • Evaluate a value proposition: whom it is for, what it promises, and why that beats the alternatives.

Review first

  • What marketing is — This lesson builds on exchange, willingness to pay and the marketing process.
  • What finance is: money, time and risk — Lifetime value discounts future margins, so you need present value and the discount rate.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

In November 2026 Ana asked 12 of Lantern’s 18 catering clients why they had chosen Lantern. Each could give several reasons. Coffee quality came up 9 times and reliability on the day 7 times; price came up only twice.

Fictional case

Lantern is not the cheap option. Brewline Catering, a larger caterer with self-serve urns and pod machines and no baristas, charges $7.50 a head; Lantern charges $9.00, which is $1.50 a head more. In 2026 Lantern also lost 5 bids, all to Brewline on price.

Both facts are about value. The clients who chose Lantern judged its offer worth $1.50 a head more than Brewline’s; the five organisations that chose Brewline judged otherwise. Neither group made a mistake. Customer value is the customer’s judgement of the benefits of an offer relative to its total cost, compared with the alternatives, and different customers judge differently. This lesson follows value from that judgement to satisfaction after the purchase, then to loyalty and retention, and finally to what a customer is worth to the business over the years.

What customers weigh

Valarie Zeithaml (1988), who asked consumers what “value” meant to them, found the word used in several senses. For some it meant no more than a low price. For others it meant “what I get for what I give”, and that is the sense marketers build on: a judgement of everything received against everything given up.

  • Benefits can be functional (the coffee keeps people alert), emotional (a small treat in a long day) or social (visitors notice that the firm looks after its guests). Organisations also weigh economic benefits: an event that runs smoothly protects the reputation of the person who booked it.
  • Money costs are the price and any extras, such as delivery charges, deposits and cancellation fees.
  • Non-money costs are time (the hours an office manager spends organising), effort (setting up and clearing away) and risk: the chance that the coffee runs out, the staff arrive late or the product disappoints.

Most of the reasons Lantern’s clients gave are benefits or reductions in risk, not price. Reliability on the day, named by 7 of the 12, is a reduction in risk: an office manager who books catering for 90 colleagues is the one embarrassed if it goes wrong. A client who weighs that risk heavily can rationally choose the dearer caterer; one organising a simple internal meeting can rationally choose the cheaper one.

When the benefits and the non-money costs can be put in money, value can be calculated. The first lesson introduced willingness to pay, the most a customer would pay for an offer rather than go without it. Subtract the price and the money value of the time, effort and risk, and what is left is the net value of the offer to that customer. She chooses the offer with the highest net value, which need not be the one with the lowest price.

Satisfaction: performance against expectations

Customer satisfaction is the customer’s judgement, after buying, of how well the offer performed compared with what they expected. Richard Oliver (1980) modelled satisfaction as depending on expectations and on expectation disconfirmation: the gap between the performance the customer perceives and the performance she expected. Performance above expectations (positive disconfirmation) leads to satisfaction; performance that matches them (confirmation) leads to satisfaction; performance below them (negative disconfirmation) leads to dissatisfaction.

Expectations come from past experience, from what friends and reviews say, from the price and from the seller’s own promises. That gives a business two levers, not one: it can improve performance, or it can set expectations it is able to meet. A caterer that promises “the best coffee you will ever taste” turns a very good cup into a disappointment. One that promises baristas on site 30 minutes before every event, and keeps the promise, builds satisfaction on something it controls.

Loyalty and retention

Customer loyalty has two sides: behaviour (buying again) and attitude (preferring the business and recommending it). Satisfaction usually helps both, but the link is loose. Satisfied customers can still leave, for variety or for a better offer; dissatisfied ones can stay when there is no real alternative or switching is costly. That is why businesses measure what customers do as well as what they say.

The simplest measure of what they do is the retention rate: the share of customers at the start of a period who are still customers at its end. Its complement is the churn rate, the share who leave, so churn = 1 − retention. Lantern expects 61% of its catering clients to stay from one year to the next, so 39% leave each year and have to be replaced before the business can grow at all.

What a customer is worth

A customer who stays is worth more than one sale. Customer lifetime value (CLV) is the present value of the margins a customer is expected to bring in over the whole relationship. It turns retention into money, and it answers a question every marketer faces: how much is it worth spending to win a customer, or to keep one?

Fictional case

Lantern’s online shop offers a bean subscription. Suppose (these are assumptions for this lesson, not figures from the case) that a typical subscriber orders 8 times a year at about $45 an order, $360 of revenue a year, and that 40% of it is left after the beans, packaging, delivery and payment fees: a margin of $144 a year. Suppose too that 70% of subscribers renew each year, and that Lantern discounts future money at 10% a year. The theory section works out what such a subscriber is worth.

The value proposition

A business can’t be everything to everyone, so it makes a promise to the customers it wants. A value proposition is the promise of benefits that gives target customers a reason to choose an offer over the alternatives. A good one answers four questions: for whom, which benefits, why this offer delivers them better than the alternatives, and what it will cost the customer.

Compare two versions for Lantern’s subscription. “High-quality coffee at great prices” names no customer, promises nothing that can be checked and could be printed by any rival. “For people who make coffee at home every morning: beans delivered within three days of roasting, so every cup tastes as it should; skip or cancel any month” names the customer, states a benefit and a reason to believe it (the roasting date is printed on every bag), and lowers the risk of signing up. The value proposition is chosen in step 2 of the marketing process, delivered by the offer in step 3 and tested by satisfaction in step 4.

In depth

Two ways to write customer value

OpenStax writes customer value as a ratio, value = benefits ÷ price, and M02 uses the same ratio with total cost in place of price. A ratio is a way of thinking: value rises if benefits rise or costs fall. When the benefits and the costs can be put in money, a difference is more useful, because it says how much better off the customer is and can be compared across offers. That is the net value in the formulas below. It extends the first lesson’s buyer’s gain, willingness to pay minus price, by subtracting the money value of time, effort and risk as well.

Two cautions apply to either form. Value is judged against the next-best alternative, which may be doing nothing or doing the job yourself. And it differs between customers: in the simple example below, the same flight is the better value for one traveller and the worse for another. Groups of customers who weigh benefits and costs alike are segments, the subject of M05.

Expectation disconfirmation and its limits

The disconfirmation model is a useful simplification. Customers don’t always form clear expectations, especially for things they have never bought. Performance is perceived, not measured: two guests at the same event can judge it differently. Pleasant surprises raise the bar, because today’s surprise can become tomorrow’s expectation. And satisfaction surveys hear from the customers willing to answer, who are not a random sample of all customers, so their scores can mislead (non-response bias, from B01).

Calculating customer lifetime value

Customer lifetime value adds up the margins a customer is expected to bring in, each weighted by the chance that the customer is still there and discounted to today. Five assumptions go into it, and each must be stated.

  • Margin, not revenue. mmm is what the customer brings in each year after the costs that serving them causes: for the subscriber, $144, not $360. It is a contribution margin per customer per year, the same idea M02 applies to each unit sold.
  • Retention. The customer is there for year 1, then stays from one year to the next with probability rrr, the same every year.
  • Discount rate. Money received later is worth less today, as F00 showed. ddd is the business’s discount rate, here 10% a year.
  • Timing. Each year’s margin arrives at the end of that year.
  • Horizon. Either a fixed number of years, TTT, or every future year.

Caution: Lifetime value is an estimate built on assumptions

Change retention from 70% to 65% and the subscriber’s CLV falls from $360 to $320; raise it to 75% and CLV rises to about $411. Retention is uncertain, and the formula assumes it never changes. Treat CLV as a range under stated assumptions, not as a fact about a customer.

Texts also time the margins differently. Gupta, Lehmann and Stuart (2004), who used retention and margins to value companies’ whole customer bases, count a first margin at the moment a customer is acquired; others count the first margin only once the customer has renewed. For the Lantern subscriber those two conventions give about $396 and $252, against $360 here. None is wrong, provided you say which you use. This course uses the timing above throughout: margins at the end of each year, with the customer certain to be there for the first.

Lifetime value against acquisition cost

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is the marketing and sales spending on winning new customers in a period, divided by the number of new customers won. It is the cost per acquisition that M02 measures, with becoming a customer as the action. Suppose Lantern spends $2,700 on a subscription campaign (social ads and a free first bag) and wins 30 subscribers: CAC = $2,700 ÷ 30 = $90. Each subscriber is expected to bring in $360 of discounted margin, four times what it cost to win, so the campaign creates $360 − $90 = $270 of value per subscriber, on these assumptions.

  • Compare like with like. CLV should be built from margin, and CAC from all the spending that winning the customers caused, including free samples and discounts.
  • Averages hide the margin. The last customers a campaign wins usually cost more than the average and may stay less. The applied example below works this out.
  • Past acquisition spending is sunk. As Gupta, Lehmann and Stuart point out, the cost of winning existing customers has already been incurred, so it plays no part in valuing them or in deciding how to keep them.
  • Retention competes for the same money. Raising retention lifts the CLV of every customer, so a dollar spent keeping customers can be worth more than one spent finding them. The challenging example compares the two.

Testing a value proposition

A value proposition is worth writing down only if it survives a few tests. Does it name customers specific enough that you could find them? Does it promise a benefit those customers value, which the business can check against what they say? Lantern’s clients named quality and reliability, not price.

Does it give a reason to believe, such as a guarantee, a printed roasting date or a client who will vouch for it? Can the business keep the promise every time, since the promise sets the expectations that satisfaction will be judged against? And is it different from what the rivals promise? A proposition that fails the last test may still be true, but it gives customers no reason to choose.

Common mistakes

  • “Customer value means a low price.”

    Price is one cost among several, and benefits count too. The consultant in the simple example chose the dearer flight because it cost her less in time and risk, and most of Lantern’s clients chose the dearer caterer for quality and reliability. A low price is one way to offer value, not the definition of it.

  • “A satisfied customer is a loyal customer.”

    Satisfaction helps loyalty, but satisfied customers can still leave for variety or a better offer, and dissatisfied ones can stay when switching is hard. Measure behaviour, such as retention and repeat purchases, as well as satisfaction scores.

  • “Promise as much as possible; customers like ambition.”

    Promises set the expectations that performance is judged against. Over-promising turns good performance into negative disconfirmation and a dissatisfied customer. Promise what you can deliver every time, then deliver it.

  • “Lifetime value is the revenue a customer brings in.”

    CLV is built from margin, what is left after the costs of serving the customer. Using the subscriber’s annual revenue ($360) instead of the $144 margin gives a CLV of $900 rather than $360, two and a half times too much, and would justify spending far too much to win customers.

  • “CLV is a fact about a customer.”

    It is a forecast built on assumptions about margin, retention, the discount rate and timing. Five points of retention move the subscriber’s CLV between $320 and about $411. Say what you assumed, and show how the answer changes if the assumptions are wrong.

  • “If CLV beats our average CAC, every extra customer is worth winning.”

    The extra customers a bigger campaign wins usually cost more than the average and may be worth less. Compare the extra spending with the extra customers, and value those customers on their own assumptions. And don’t count money already spent winning existing customers: it is sunk.

In practice

Fictional case

Rowan Lane, a fictional online clothing retailer, wins new customers in two ways. Social media ads cost it $35 for each new customer they bring. A referral programme gives an existing customer a $10 credit and the friend she refers $10 off a first order: $20 for each new customer. Its marketing analyst, Kofi, has to advise how to split next year’s budget between them.

Kofi starts from the value of the customers each route brings, not the cost of winning them. Following last year’s new customers through their first year, he estimates that customers from ads bring an annual margin of about $28 and that 30% of them buy again the following year, while referred customers bring about $40 a year and 50% of them come back. With Rowan Lane’s 10% discount rate, a customer from ads has CLV=28÷(1+0.10−0.30)=35\text{CLV} = 28 \div (1 + 0.10 - 0.30) = 35CLV=28÷(1+0.10−0.30)=35 dollars, and a referred customer CLV=40÷(1+0.10−0.50)≈66.67\text{CLV} = 40 \div (1 + 0.10 - 0.50) \approx 66.67CLV=40÷(1+0.10−0.50)≈66.67 dollars.

On these figures an ad customer is worth exactly what it costs to win, $35 against $35, so the ads create no value. A referred customer is worth about $66.67 against a $20 cost: about $46.67 of value each.

Kofi’s first instinct is to move the whole budget to referrals. He resists it for two reasons. Referrals are limited by how many customers are willing to recommend the shop, so the programme can’t absorb the whole budget. And referred customers may differ from ad customers in ways the programme didn’t cause: people whose friends recommend a shop were perhaps more likely to like it anyway.

His recommendation has three parts. Grow the referral programme as far as customers will take it, and track what each extra referral costs. Cut the ads back to the campaigns whose customers renew most, and test whether the rest bring in any customers who wouldn’t have come anyway, by holding back a randomly chosen group from seeing them, as M02 describes. And find out why ad customers leave. If most leave after a disappointing first order, such as clothes that don’t fit or a late delivery, the problem is satisfaction, not acquisition, and a better size guide may be worth more than any change to the budget.

The last point brings the lesson full circle. Lifetime value is the money at the end of a chain that starts with whether customers judge an offer worth what it costs them, and whether it meets the expectations the business set. A marketer who manages only the last link, by spending more to win customers, is pouring water into a leaking bucket.

Summary

  • Customer value is the customer’s judgement of an offer’s benefits relative to its total cost (money, time, effort and risk), compared with the alternatives; when all can be put in money, net value = W − (P + N).
  • Satisfaction depends on perceived performance against expectations (Oliver, 1980): positive disconfirmation and confirmation satisfy; negative disconfirmation disappoints. Promises set expectations.
  • Loyalty has a behavioural side (buying again) and an attitudinal side (preferring and recommending). Retention is the share of customers who stay; churn = 1 − retention.
  • Customer lifetime value is the present value of expected margins: with margins at year end and constant retention, CLV = m ÷ (1 + d − r). Use margin, not revenue, and state the timing.
  • CLV is an estimate: small changes in retention move it a lot. Compare it with the customer acquisition cost of the extra customers a decision would win, and treat past acquisition spending as sunk.
  • A value proposition names the target customers, the benefits, the reason to believe and the cost to the customer; it must be a promise the business can keep.

Key formulas

  • Net value of an offer to a customer (when benefits and costs can be put in money)
  • Customer lifetime value (margins at year end; retention constant)
  • Customer acquisition cost, and the net value of winning a customer

Terms introduced

Customer value
The customer’s judgement of the benefits of an offer relative to its total cost in money, time, effort and risk, compared with the alternatives.
Customer satisfaction
The customer’s judgement, after buying, of how well an offer performed compared with what they expected.
Expectation disconfirmation
The gap between an offer’s perceived performance and the customer’s expectations. Performance above expectations is positive disconfirmation, below them negative disconfirmation, and equal to them confirmation.
Customer loyalty
A customer’s tendency to keep buying from a business (behavioural loyalty) and to prefer and recommend it (attitudinal loyalty).
Retention rate
The share of customers at the start of a period who are still customers at its end.
Churn rate
The share of customers at the start of a period who have stopped buying by its end: 1 minus the retention rate.
Customer lifetime value
The present value of the margins a customer is expected to bring in over the whole relationship. Abbreviated CLV.
Customer acquisition cost
Marketing and sales spending on winning new customers in a period, divided by the number of new customers won. Abbreviated CAC.
Contribution margin
Price received minus variable cost per unit: what each unit contributes towards fixed costs and then profit. As a share of price it is the contribution margin ratio.
Value proposition
The promise of benefits that gives target customers a reason to choose an offer over the alternatives.
