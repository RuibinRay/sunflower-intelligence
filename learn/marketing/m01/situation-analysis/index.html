Situation analysis

Before deciding where to go, a business needs an honest picture of where it stands. Learn to scan the marketing environment with PESTEL, profile competitors and compare them with numbers, and build a SWOT analysis that rests on evidence and leads to decisions, knowing what each tool can and can’t tell you.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Distinguish the microenvironment from the macroenvironment, and classify environmental factors with PESTEL.
  • Analyse competitors, including indirect competitors and substitutes, and compare them with market share and a weighted competitive-strength scorecard.
  • Construct a SWOT analysis that separates internal from external factors and supports each factor with evidence.
  • Evaluate the evidence behind a situation analysis, including small samples and research drawn only from current customers.
  • Explain the known weaknesses of SWOT and PESTEL, and use the TOWS matrix to turn a SWOT into strategic options.

  • Market orientation: putting customers at the centre — A situation analysis is market orientation in practice: gathering intelligence about customers and competitors before deciding.

  • Collecting data: sources, samples and bias — The lesson judges Lantern’s client interviews with two ideas from that lesson: convenience samples and survivorship bias.

The idea

Fictional case

It is late November 2026. Lantern Coffee Co. opened on 1 March, and its catering business now serves 18 clients, offices and event organisers that book about 6 events a year each at $9.00 a head. Ana Lim wants catering revenue to grow by 25% in 2027. Before she sets targets or decides how to compete, she wants an honest picture of where Lantern stands. She has some evidence: interviews with 12 of the 18 clients, notes on the 5 bids Lantern lost this year, all of them to Brewline Catering on price, and what she has learned about how Harbor Tech, one of her first clients, buys catering.

Plans that skip this step tend to rest on what the owner already believes: that customers love the product, that the competition is weak, that next year will look like this one. A situation analysis replaces those beliefs with a structured review of the business’s current position: its customers, its competitors, the wider forces acting on its market, and its own strengths and weaknesses. It is an early section of almost every marketing plan, coming straight after the executive summary.

Where a situation analysis fits

Marketing planning can be summed up in four questions, asked in order:

  1. Where are we now? The situation analysis: this lesson.
  2. Where do we want to be? Objectives, stated precisely enough to be measured: the next lesson.
  3. How will we get there? Strategy, starting with how the business will compete (the third lesson of this module), and then the marketing mix that carries the strategy out (M02).
  4. How will we know? Measures and reviews, which feed the next round’s situation analysis.

The order matters. An objective set without knowing the starting point is either timid or fanciful, and a strategy chosen without knowing the competitors may be one they already own.

Three things to look at

A situation analysis looks outward before it looks inward. First, the environment: who and what around the business affects its ability to serve customers. Second, the competitors: who else is trying to win the same customers, and how. Third, the business itself: what it does well and badly, judged against those competitors and by its customers. A SWOT analysis, at the end, pulls the three together.

The marketing environment

The marketing environment is everything outside the marketing function that affects its ability to build and keep relationships with customers. It is usually divided into two layers.

The microenvironment is made up of the actors close to the business that it deals with directly: its customers, its suppliers, the intermediaries that help it reach customers, its competitors, its publics (any group with an interest in it, such as the local press or the people who review it online) and the rest of the business itself, since marketing depends on finance, operations and staff. For a caterer like Lantern, suppliers include whoever provides the beans, the milk and the servicing of the espresso machines; intermediaries might include event venues and event planners who recommend caterers to their clients.

The macroenvironment is made up of the wider forces that shape the whole market and that no single business controls: politics, the economy, society, technology, the natural environment and the law. Lantern can negotiate with its milk supplier; it cannot negotiate with the price of coffee beans on world markets or with a city’s rules on single-use cups.

A standard checklist for the macroenvironment is PESTEL: political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal factors. Its purpose is to make sure nothing important is missed. It doesn’t say whether a factor is good or bad news. A rise in bean prices hurts every coffee business, but it hurts most the ones that compete only on price; a new rule on reusable cups is a cost for one caterer and a selling point for another that already uses them. Deciding which way a factor cuts, for this business, is the job of the SWOT analysis.

Competitors, and the business itself

A competitor analysis identifies the businesses, and the other options, that customers could choose instead, and studies their offers, prices, strengths, weaknesses and likely next moves. It includes more than look-alike firms. Lantern’s catering competes with other caterers, but also with cafés that deliver, with coffee machines an office can lease, and with the option of doing nothing special at all.

The inward look asks what the business does well and badly. The test is always relative and from the customer’s side: a strength is something the business does better than its rivals in a way its customers value. A caterer’s baristas are a strength only if clients notice them and care, and only if rivals can’t offer the same.

Key idea: Describe first, judge second

Collect the facts about the environment, the competitors and the business before deciding which are strengths, weaknesses, opportunities or threats. Judging too early lets the plan you already favour decide which facts you notice.

It is a model of how attractive an industry is, and it overlaps with this list. Course M11 covers it.

PESTEL in more depth

The six PESTEL factors, with questions a coffee caterer might ask

Three cautions apply. First, the categories overlap: a tariff is both a political decision and an economic cost, and pressure to cut waste is both a social trend and an environmental one. OpenStax’s Principles of Management notes the overlap and argues, rightly, that it doesn’t make the checklist less useful. Put each factor where it helps the discussion, and don’t argue about the label.

Second, as the same text stresses, PESTEL factors are not opportunities or threats in themselves; they become one or the other only in relation to a particular business. Third, the letters vary. PEST, STEEP and STEEPLE (which adds ethics) are all in use; what matters is covering the ground, not the acronym.

A PESTEL scan easily produces dozens of factors, most of which will never matter. To prioritise, managers often score each factor for its likely impact on the business and the likelihood that it happens within the planning period, say from 1 to 5 each, and concentrate on the factors with the highest product. The scores are judgements, but writing them down makes disagreements visible and arguable.

Treat the product as a rough ranking, not a measurement. The scales are ordinal, 5 × 1 ties with 1 × 5, and a factor with impact 5 but likelihood 2 can drop out of a top-five list although it would hurt most if it happened. Review high-impact factors separately, and score impact by its size, whether the effect is good or bad.

Competitor analysis

Start by asking whom customers actually compare you with. Direct competitors offer much the same thing to the same customers: for Lantern, other office caterers. Indirect competitors meet the same need in a different way: a café that delivers coffee, or a leased bean-to-cup machine. Economists call an offer that meets the same need differently a substitute.

Customers can also choose to do the job themselves, or not at all. Lost sales, and what prospects say they would otherwise do, are the best evidence of who the real competitors are.

For each important competitor, find out what it offers and at what price, whom it targets, what it does well and badly in customers’ eyes, how big it is and how it is likely to respond if you move. Sources include its website and price lists, what clients and prospects tell you, the feedback from bids you lose, trade events and public records. Gather it honestly: posing as a customer to obtain a rival’s confidential price list is the kind of practice that damages a firm’s reputation and may break the law.

The case gives enough to begin a profile of Lantern’s main catering competitor. Notice the unknowns: they are the questions the next round of research should answer.

A competitor’s size in its market is measured by its market share: its sales as a percentage of the total sales in the market, over the same period. Share can be measured in money or in units, and it depends on how the market is defined. A caterer with 13% of the office-catering market in one city may have far less than 1% of “catering” nationally, so always state the market, the area and the period.

To compare competitors on more than size, list the factors that customers say decide their choice, weight each by its importance, score each competitor on each factor and add up the weighted scores: a weighted competitive-strength scorecard. It is the same arithmetic as the weighted-attribute model of the consumer-decisions lesson, seen from the seller’s side. Its value is that it forces two explicit judgements, how much each factor matters and how well each firm performs, and lets you see how sensitive the conclusion is to them. The weights should come from customers, not from the analyst’s view of what ought to matter.

Know the scorecard’s limits. It is compensatory: a high rating on one factor makes up for a low one, so screen out any firm that fails a must-have, such as a required safety certificate, before you score. Counting how often customers mention a factor measures what is top of mind, which is not necessarily what matters most: few clients mention safety until something goes wrong. And the ratings are judgements on a 1–5 scale, so gaps of about 0.1 in the weighted scores are not meaningful.

Some planners organise the whole review as the 5Cs: company, customers, competitors, collaborators (suppliers, intermediaries and partners) and context (the macroenvironment). It covers the same ground as the micro and macro layers in a different order. Use whichever checklist your organisation uses; none of them is a theory.

SWOT analysis

A SWOT analysis summarises the situation in four lists. Strengths and weaknesses are internal: they belong to the business, which can change them. Opportunities and threats are external: they exist in the environment whether or not the business acts. OpenStax’s Principles of Marketing describes opportunities as openings for something positive to happen, but only if the business can take advantage of them.

Five rules make a SWOT worth reading.

  1. Internal means controllable. A rival’s new branch is external even though it is close by; a shortage of vans is internal even though it was caused by a supplier’s delay.
  2. Opportunities are conditions, not actions. “Launch a subscription service” is something the business might do, a strategy. The opportunity, if there is one, is the condition that makes it attractive, such as employers looking for ways to support people who work from home.
  3. Strengths and weaknesses are relative and customer-judged. Being “good at customer service” is a strength only if customers see it and rivals can’t match it. A belief the owner holds without evidence is a hypothesis to test, not a strength.
  4. Every item needs evidence. Say where it comes from: client interviews, sales records, lost bids, a competitor’s price list. Mark the items that rest on opinion.
  5. Prioritise. Rank strengths and weaknesses by how much they matter competitively, and opportunities and threats by how likely and how large they are. Three well-supported items in each box beat fifteen vague ones.

Some facts belong on both sides. A small business’s size can be a strength (it can respond to a client within a day) and a weakness (it has no cover when someone is ill). Split such a fact into its two effects and list each where it belongs.

A SWOT describes; it doesn’t decide. The TOWS matrix, proposed by Heinz Weihrich in 1982, turns it towards strategy by pairing the boxes: SO options use strengths to take opportunities, ST options use strengths to counter threats, WO options fix weaknesses in order to take opportunities, and WT options limit the damage where weaknesses meet threats. The result is a list of options to evaluate, not a decision. The next two lessons of this module, and later course M11, take that step further.

What these tools can’t do

SWOT is popular because it is quick and needs no data skills; that is also its weakness. Terry Hill and Roy Westbrook studied how consultants used it with 50 companies in a UK government manufacturing-planning scheme; SWOT was used in more than 20 of them. They found long lists of vaguely worded factors (over 40 on average), no prioritisation, no checking of whether the factors were true, and, most damning, no use of the results in the later stages of planning. Their article was titled “SWOT analysis: it’s time for a product recall”.

OpenStax’s Principles of Management adds two limits: a SWOT is only as good as the person doing it, and because it is hard to imagine everything, it tends to miss important issues. Both SWOT and PESTEL are heuristics: checklists that prompt good questions, not models that produce answers. Use them with the rules above, attach evidence, keep them short, and make sure each item leads somewhere, to an objective, an option or a question for research.

Caution: The snapshot problem

A situation analysis describes one moment. Competitors move, clients leave, rules change. Date it, note what would change your view, and repeat it at least once a year, or whenever a large change, such as a new competitor, arrives.

The evidence behind the analysis

A situation analysis is only as good as its inputs. The usual ones are the business’s own records (sales, clients won and lost, complaints), customer research (interviews and surveys), win–loss reviews (asking prospects why a bid was won or lost) and secondary data such as industry reports and official statistics. Course M04 teaches how to design and judge market research. Two questions apply to every input: who was asked, and who wasn’t?

Lantern’s interviews show why. Ana asked 12 of her 18 catering clients why they chose Lantern, and each could give several reasons.

Reasons given by 12 catering clients for choosing Lantern, November 2026 (fictional case)

Each client could name several reasons, so the shares add up to more than 100%.

Three cautions follow. The sample is small: one client more or less changes a share by about 8 percentage points (1 ÷ 12). The shares can’t be added up, because the 28 mentions came from 12 people.

Most important, everyone interviewed is a current client, someone who has already chosen Lantern. That is a textbook case of survivorship bias: the prospects who chose Brewline are invisible in these figures, and the 5 lost bids suggest they weighed price very differently. A finding such as “only 2 of 12 clients mentioned price” describes the people who stayed with Lantern, not the market. How these findings belong in Lantern’s SWOT is part of the course project.

Common mistakes

  • “Opportunities are things we could do.”

    An opportunity is a condition outside the business, such as a change in what customers want or a gap a rival has left. “Launch an app” or “enter a new city” are actions, and belong among the options that come after the SWOT. Listing actions as opportunities skips the analysis and smuggles in a decision.

  • “A strength is anything we are proud of.”

    A strength must be something the business does better than its rivals, in a way customers value, and there must be evidence for it. A feature every competitor also has is not a strength, and an owner’s belief that nobody has checked is a hypothesis.

  • “A long SWOT is a thorough SWOT.”

    Hill and Westbrook found SWOTs made of long lists (over 40 factors on average) of general, unprioritised, unverified points that nobody used later in planning. Keep a few items per box, give each its evidence, rank them, and make sure each one leads to an objective, an option or a research question.

  • “PESTEL factors are threats.”

    PESTEL lists forces; it doesn’t judge them. A new packaging rule may hurt one firm and help another that already complies. Decide whether a factor is an opportunity or a threat only in relation to a particular business and its objectives.

  • “Our clients say price doesn’t matter.”

    If only current clients were asked, the answer describes the people who already accepted your price. Those who chose a cheaper rival are missing from the sample, which is survivorship bias. Lost bids and lost clients are the evidence that corrects it.

  • “Our competitors are the firms that sell what we sell.”

    Customers also compare you with substitutes (a different way of meeting the same need) and with doing the job themselves. Ask lost prospects what they chose instead; the answer is often not a look-alike firm.

In practice

Fictional case

Pellham Dairy, a fictional regional dairy, supplies milk, yoghurt and butter to supermarkets and independent shops. Each autumn its marketing manager writes the situation analysis that opens the next year’s marketing plan. Her approach shows how a professional keeps the tools short, evidenced and pointed at decisions.

She starts with the evidence, not the grid. She pulls the year’s sales by customer and product, the list of shops that stopped stocking Pellham and the reasons they gave, the results of a small shopper survey, the main competitors’ prices from store visits, and notes from her sales team. She dates the pack: the analysis describes October, and she will revisit it if anything large changes.

She scans the macroenvironment and ranks it. Her team lists about twenty PESTEL factors in an hour. She scores each for impact and likelihood over the coming year, from 1 to 5, and keeps the top five for discussion.

Pellham Dairy’s five highest-priority PESTEL factors (fictional scores)
FactorPESTELImpactLikelihoodPriority
A proposed rule requiring recyclable caps and labels from 2028Legal, environmental4416
Rising feed and energy costs pushing up the price farmers are paidEconomic5315
More younger shoppers buying plant-based drinks (Pellham’s own survey)Social3412
Cheaper sensors for tracking milk temperature in transitTechnological2510
A possible change in funding for milk in schoolsPolitical326

Priority = impact × likelihood. The scores are the team’s judgements, recorded so they can be challenged.

She profiles three competitors, not thirty. For the national brand, a supermarket’s own-label milk and a rival regional dairy she records price, range, where they are stocked and what buyers say about them, with a column for what she doesn’t know. Lost listings tell her the most: two independent shops switched to the rival dairy because it delivers six days a week, against Pellham’s three.

Her SWOT fits on one page. Each box has at most four items, each with its source: “strength: the best-known local dairy brand among surveyed shoppers (shopper survey)”; “weakness: three deliveries a week, against the rival’s six (two lost listings)”; “threat: recyclable-packaging rule (draft regulation, priority 16)”. She then writes one or two options for each TOWS pairing. One ST option, for example, is to switch to recyclable caps before the rule arrives and tell shoppers so, using the strong local brand to turn a threat into a reason to choose Pellham.

She ends with questions, not conclusions. The last section lists what the analysis can’t yet answer and the research that would: would independents pay more for six deliveries a week? How many plant-based buyers also buy dairy? Those questions, and the prioritised SWOT, are what the objectives and strategy sections of the plan build on. The same discipline applies whether the business is a dairy, a hotel, a software firm or a coffee caterer: few items, each with evidence, each leading somewhere.

Summary

  • A situation analysis answers “where are we now?”: it reviews the environment, the competitors and the business itself before objectives and strategy are set.
  • The microenvironment is the actors a business deals with (customers, competitors, suppliers, intermediaries, publics, the rest of the business); the macroenvironment is the wider forces, checked with PESTEL.
  • PESTEL factors are neither opportunities nor threats in themselves; prioritise them by impact and likelihood.
  • Competitor analysis includes substitutes and doing nothing. Market share measures size; a weighted competitive-strength scorecard compares firms on what customers value, and its answer depends on whose weights you use.
  • In a SWOT, strengths and weaknesses are internal and customer-judged; opportunities and threats are external conditions, not actions. Every item needs evidence and a priority.
  • SWOT and PESTEL are heuristics with known weaknesses (long, vague, unverified, unused lists). The TOWS matrix turns a SWOT into options; it doesn’t make the decision.
  • Judge the evidence: research with current customers only suffers from survivorship bias, and small samples move a lot with one answer.

Terms introduced

Situation analysis
A structured review of a business’s current position (its customers, competitors, wider environment and its own strengths and weaknesses) used as the starting point of a marketing plan.
Marketing environment
The actors and forces outside the marketing function that affect its ability to build and keep relationships with customers; divided into the microenvironment and the macroenvironment.
Microenvironment
The actors close to a business that it deals with directly: customers, competitors, suppliers, intermediaries, publics and the rest of the business itself.
Macroenvironment
The wider political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal forces that shape a whole market and that no single business controls.
PESTEL
A checklist for scanning the macroenvironment: political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal factors. Variants include PEST, STEEP and STEEPLE.
Competitor analysis
Identifying the firms and other options customers could choose instead, and studying their offers, prices, strengths, weaknesses and likely responses.
Substitute
A product or service that meets the same customer need in a different way, such as a leased office coffee machine instead of a coffee caterer.
Market share
A business’s sales as a percentage of total sales in a defined market over the same period, measured in money or in units.
Weighted competitive-strength scorecard
A comparison of competitors in which each is rated on the factors customers use to choose, each rating is multiplied by the factor’s weight, and the results are added up.
SWOT analysis
A summary of a business’s situation in four lists: internal strengths and weaknesses, and external opportunities and threats.
TOWS matrix
Heinz Weihrich’s (1982) way of turning a SWOT into strategic options by pairing its boxes: strengths with opportunities (SO) or threats (ST), and weaknesses with opportunities (WO) or threats (WT).
