Market orientation: putting customers at the centre

Businesses differ in what they start from: what they can make, how good the product is, how hard they sell, or what customers need. Learn the five classic orientations and their limits, what a market orientation requires in practice (intelligence about customers and competitors, shared across the business and acted on), how to read what customers tell you, and why ethics and society belong in the picture.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Distinguish the production, product, selling, marketing and societal marketing concepts by what each starts from and what it risks.
  • Explain market orientation as behaviour, using Kohli and Jaworski’s three activities and Narver and Slater’s three components.
  • Compare customer-centric and product-centric organisations and the measures each relies on.
  • Calculate the share of respondents giving each reason in customer research, and interpret what such figures can and cannot show.
  • Evaluate a marketing decision for its effects on society, using the AMA’s ethical norms and the rules on environmental claims.

Review first

  • What marketing is — Marketing myopia and the marketing process are the starting points here.
  • Customer value, satisfaction and lifetime value — Market orientation exists to create customer value; the challenging example uses retention and lifetime value.
  • Collecting data: sources, samples and bias — Interviews are a way of collecting data: who was asked, and who wasn’t, decides what the answers can show.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

In November 2026 Ana interviewed 12 of Lantern’s 18 catering clients and asked why they had chosen Lantern. Each could give several reasons. Coffee quality came up 9 times, reliability on the day 7, the baristas’ professionalism 6, flexible menus 4 and price 2. She also went through the bids she had lost in 2026: 5, all to Brewline Catering on price. Brewline is a larger caterer with self-serve urns and pod machines and no baristas, and it charges $7.50 a head against Lantern’s $9.00.

Ana’s interviews are an act of listening, and listening is where this lesson starts. Every business decides what to make and how to sell it from some starting point. It may start from what its equipment does well, from what its engineers are proud of, from what it needs to sell this month, or from what customers need. That starting point is the business’s orientation, and it shapes almost everything the business does.

Five orientations

Marketing textbooks describe five classic orientations, often called concepts. Each is a belief about what makes a business succeed. Here is each one as it might sound at Lantern.

  • The production concept holds that customers want offers that are available and cheap, so the business should make as much as it can as efficiently as possible. At Lantern: “Serve as many cups per barista-hour as we can; clients will take what is cheap and on time.”
  • The product concept holds that customers want the best quality, performance and features, so the business should keep improving the product. At Lantern: “Perfect the espresso; good coffee sells itself.” Pushed too far, it leads to what Levitt called marketing myopia.
  • The selling concept holds that customers won’t buy enough unless they are persuaded, so the business should sell and promote hard what it has already decided to make. At Lantern: “Phone every office in the district with a discount until someone books.”
  • The marketing concept holds that a business reaches its goals by finding out what target customers need and want and satisfying them better than competitors do. At Lantern: “Find out what makes an office event go well for the people who book it, and build the service around that.”
  • The societal marketing concept holds that the business should satisfy customers in ways that also protect the long-term interests of society and the environment. At Lantern: “Do all of that, and be fair to our baristas, honest in our claims and careful with waste.”

Each orientation describes something a business needs. Lantern must be efficient, its coffee must be good and someone must ask for the business. The trouble starts when one of them becomes the whole view. A caterer that only cuts costs, only perfects the espresso or only pushes discounts will sooner or later offer something its customers don’t want enough. The marketing concept puts customers’ needs first and makes the other three serve them; the societal marketing concept adds the people outside the exchange who are affected by it.

From a philosophy to a practice

The marketing concept is a belief, and beliefs are easy to hold and hard to act on. In 1990 two pairs of researchers, publishing in the Journal of Marketing, set out what acting on it looks like. Ajay Kohli and Bernard Jaworski described market orientation as three organisation-wide activities: generating market intelligence about customers’ current and future needs and the forces that shape them, disseminating that intelligence across the departments of the business, and responding to it.

John Narver and Stanley Slater treated market orientation as part of a business’s culture, which shows in three behavioural components and is guided by two decision criteria. The components are customer orientation, understanding target customers well enough to keep creating value for them; competitor orientation, understanding what current and potential competitors can and will do; and inter-functional coordination, using the whole business, not just a marketing department, to create value for customers. The criteria are a long-term focus and profitability: market orientation is not giving customers whatever they ask for at any price.

At Lantern the three activities are small but real. Ana’s interviews, the event feedback forms her clients’ staff fill in and her record of lost bids are intelligence generation. Telling the baristas what clients said about their professionalism, and telling whoever plans the rota that 7 of the 12 clients named reliability, is dissemination. Changing something as a result, and checking afterwards whether it worked, is responsiveness. A business can be poor at any one of the three: it can collect feedback forms that nobody reads, hold meetings where nothing is decided, or change things without asking whether the change helped.

Listening to customers

Customer research is the most direct way of generating intelligence, and it has to be read carefully. When customers can give several reasons, the useful measure is the share of respondents who gave each one, and those shares need not add up to 100%. Equally important is who was asked. Ana spoke to clients who had chosen Lantern; the organisations that chose Brewline were not in the room. The applied example below works through what her interviews can and cannot show, and M04 Market Research teaches how to design research that answers the question you actually have.

OpenStax presents the concepts as a sequence of eras: a production concept from just after the Civil War (1861–1865) until the 1920s, a product concept from the 1920s to the 1950s, then a selling concept, then the marketing concept, which it describes as the idea that an organisation will achieve its goals when it satisfies the needs and wants of the consumer, and finally the societal marketing concept.

Historians have challenged that tidy sequence. Ronald Fullerton (1988), studying Britain, Germany and the United States, argued that the “Production Era” is a myth, and that, like the sales and marketing eras, the idea hides how extensive and sophisticated marketing was long before the twentieth century. The safer reading is that the five concepts are attitudes that coexist, often inside one firm: the operations manager thinks in production terms, the engineers in product terms and the sales team in selling terms. The marketing concept is the claim that customers’ needs should coordinate them all.

Two descriptions of market orientation

Kohli and Jaworski built their account from the marketing literature and 62 field interviews with managers, because, as they noted, little had been written about how the marketing concept is actually put into practice. Three features of their definition matter. Intelligence covers future needs as well as current ones, and the forces that shape needs, such as regulation, technology and competitors, not only what customers say. It is organisation-wide: a salesperson who hears a complaint and tells nobody has generated intelligence the business never uses. And responsiveness means action: the intelligence has to change what the business offers or how it delivers it.

Narver and Slater describe the same idea as a culture and the behaviour it produces; reviews often contrast this cultural view with Kohli and Jaworski’s view of market orientation as a set of activities. Customer orientation and competitor orientation are what the business pays attention to; inter-functional coordination is how the whole business turns that attention into value for customers. In a study of 140 business units, including both commodity and non-commodity businesses, they reported a substantial positive effect of market orientation on profitability in both kinds of business. It is still one study of business units at one point in time, so it shows an association; whether market orientation caused the extra profit, or profitable units could afford to be more market-oriented, needs other evidence, as B01’s lesson on interpreting results explains.

Key idea: Market-oriented is not the same as customer-led

A market-oriented business listens to customers, watches competitors and coordinates its functions, and then decides with a long-term focus on profitability. That can mean saying no: losing 5 bids on price doesn’t by itself oblige Lantern to match Brewline’s price. Doing whatever the most recent customer asks is not market orientation; it is giving up on judgement.

Customer-centric and product-centric organisations

A related distinction is between organisations built around products and organisations built around customers. Neither is wrong everywhere, but they ask different questions and measure different things.

The measures matter most. A business that rewards staff for units sold this quarter will behave in a product-centric way whatever its mission statement says; one that tracks retention and lifetime value, as the previous lesson did, gives people a reason to keep customers satisfied. Lantern is small enough that Ana is every function at once, which makes coordination easy and makes it easy, too, to measure only what is in front of her: this week’s bookings.

The limits of listening

  • Customers describe today’s problems, not tomorrow’s solutions. Kohli and Jaworski stress future needs for this reason. Lantern’s clients can say what goes wrong at events; they are less likely to invent a new kind of service.
  • Current customers are not the market. People who chose you are the people your offer already suits. Their reasons understate whatever drove others away, which is why lost bids and non-customers are part of the intelligence.
  • Customers are not the only ones affected. Employees, suppliers, neighbours and the environment are affected by what is sold and how. Craig Smith, Minette Drumwright and Mary Gentile (2010) named a single-minded focus on customers, to the exclusion of other stakeholders, as one source of what they called “the new marketing myopia”.
  • Listening is not deciding. Research tells a business what customers value; strategy decides which customers to serve and how to compete. Module 3 of this course turns to those decisions.

Society, ethics and sustainability

The societal marketing concept asks marketers to weigh the long-term interests of society alongside customers’ wants. In practice that means questions such as: is this product safe and is its risk explained? Are the claims true? Is the price fair to vulnerable buyers? Who bears the waste?

The AMA’s Statement of Ethics frames the answers as three norms, do no harm, foster and maintain integrity, and embrace ethical values, and five values: honesty, responsibility, equity, transparency and citizenship. Equity, in its terms, includes recognising the needs of vulnerable market segments.

Sustainability claims are where ethics most often meets the law. A claim that a product is better for the environment, made without evidence or so vaguely that it can’t be checked, is called greenwashing.

In the United States the Federal Trade Commission’s Green Guides, first issued in 1992 and last revised in 2012, explain how the law against deceptive advertising applies to environmental claims. They advise marketers not to make unqualified general claims such as “eco-friendly”, because such claims are hard to interpret and it is highly unlikely that a marketer could substantiate every reasonable reading of them. Claims should instead be limited to a specific benefit, stated clearly and prominently, and backed by competent and reliable evidence. Other countries have their own rules.

Fictional case

Lantern’s cups are certified compostable in industrial composting facilities. A cup printed “100% eco-friendly” would be exactly the unqualified general claim the Green Guides advise against. Even a plain “compostable” would need qualifying if Harbour City has no industrial composting facility: the Guides ask marketers to qualify compostable claims when such facilities are not available to a substantial majority of the communities where the item is sold. A specific, honest claim (what the cups are certified for, and how clients should dispose of them where they are used) is far less likely to mislead, and more convincing. This detail is invented for the lesson.

Common mistakes

  • “Market-oriented means doing whatever customers ask.”

    Market orientation means generating intelligence about customers and competitors, sharing it and responding, with a long-term focus on profitability. Sometimes the right response is to explain, or to decline: a caterer doesn’t have to match a rival’s price because some prospects asked for it.

  • “Market orientation is the marketing department’s job.”

    Kohli and Jaworski describe organisation-wide activities, and Narver and Slater make inter-functional coordination one of the three components. Intelligence that the marketing team collects and nobody else acts on changes nothing.

  • “Business moved neatly from a production era to a sales era to a marketing era.”

    That sequence is a teaching simplification. Fullerton (1988) argued that sophisticated marketing existed long before the twentieth century, and today the five orientations coexist, often inside one firm. Use the concepts to diagnose attitudes, not to date them.

  • “If our customers are happy, we understand our market.”

    Current customers are the people your offer already suits. Lost prospects, former customers and non-customers hold the intelligence your customers can’t give you, such as why Brewline won the 5 bids Lantern lost.

  • “Shares of respondents should add up to 100%.”

    Only when each respondent gives exactly one answer. When several reasons are allowed, as in Lantern’s interviews, each share is a share of respondents and the total can be well over 100% (233% there).

  • “If it is legal, it is ethical, and green claims are just marketing.”

    The AMA’s norms (do no harm, foster integrity, embrace ethical values) ask more than the law does. And green claims are regulated: the FTC’s Green Guides advise against unqualified claims such as “eco-friendly” and require evidence for specific ones.

In practice

Fictional case

Oakhurst Clothing, a fictional online clothing brand, has a problem it first noticed in its accounts rather than its marketing: 30% of the items it sells come back. Returns cost it shipping both ways, and many returned items can’t be sold again at full price.

Its customer insight manager starts by generating intelligence. Instead of treating returns as a cost of doing business, she reads them as customers telling Oakhurst something. In one quarter the return form gives a main reason for 2,000 returns: 52% (1,040 items) don’t fit, 18% (360) look different from the photographs, and 30% (600) are other reasons, such as a change of mind or a late delivery. Because each return has one main reason, these shares add to 100%.

Dissemination is the hard part, because the causes sit in three different departments. Sizes are set by the design team, photographs by the studio, and fit advice by customer service. She brings the three together with the figures and with a sample of customers’ comments. Each sees something different: the designers learn that the same size is cut differently in different ranges; the studio learns that every item is photographed on one model; customer service learns that its fit advice is rarely asked for because customers can’t find it.

The response is coordinated: one sizing standard across ranges, photographs on models of several sizes, and a fit guide built from garment measurements and from what earlier buyers said about fit. Two quarters later, returns on new orders are down from 30% to 26%, a fall of 4 percentage points, or about 13% of the returns rate. She reports it with care: other things changed in those two quarters too, so she compares ranges that adopted the new standard first with those that adopted it later.

The societal side follows. Fewer returns mean fewer journeys and less waste, and the marketing team wants to call the next range “eco-friendly”. The manager points to the Green Guides. Instead, the range is labelled with what Oakhurst can prove, such as the share of recycled fibre by weight in each garment, backed by its suppliers’ certificates. The claim is narrower, true and checkable, which is also what makes it persuasive.

Summary

  • Five classic orientations: production (make it cheap and available), product (make it better), selling (push what is made), marketing (start from target customers’ needs) and societal marketing (serve customers in ways that also protect society).
  • The orientations coexist rather than following each other in neat eras; Fullerton (1988) called the “Production Era” a myth.
  • Market orientation turns the marketing concept into practice: generate intelligence, disseminate it across the business and respond (Kohli and Jaworski, 1990); customer orientation, competitor orientation and inter-functional coordination, with a long-term focus on profitability (Narver and Slater, 1990).
  • Customer-centric organisations are organised and measured around customers (retention, satisfaction, lifetime value); product-centric ones around products (units, product margin).
  • Read customer research by asking who was asked. With several reasons per respondent, shares of respondents add to more than 100%; current customers understate what drove others away.
  • Ethics and sustainability belong in marketing decisions: the AMA’s norms and values set the standard, and environmental claims must be specific and substantiated (the FTC’s Green Guides).

Terms introduced

Production concept
The belief that customers want offers that are available and affordable, so a business should concentrate on making as much as it can as efficiently as possible.
Product concept
The belief that customers want the best quality, performance and features, so a business should concentrate on continually improving its product.
Selling concept
The belief that customers won’t buy enough unless persuaded, so a business should sell and promote hard what it has already decided to make.
Marketing concept
The belief that a business reaches its goals by finding out what target customers need and want and satisfying them better than competitors do.
Societal marketing concept
The belief that a business should satisfy customers in ways that also protect the long-term interests of society and the environment.
Market orientation
The marketing concept put into practice: organisation-wide generation of intelligence about customers’ current and future needs, its dissemination across departments and responsiveness to it (Kohli and Jaworski, 1990); described by Narver and Slater (1990) as customer orientation, competitor orientation and inter-functional coordination.
Market intelligence
Information about customers’ current and future needs and the forces that shape them, including competitors, technology and regulation, gathered for decisions.
Customer orientation
Understanding target customers well enough to keep creating value for them; one of Narver and Slater’s three components of market orientation.
Competitor orientation
Understanding what current and potential competitors can and are likely to do; one of Narver and Slater’s three components of market orientation.
Inter-functional coordination
Using the whole business, not just a marketing department, to create value for customers; one of Narver and Slater’s three components of market orientation.
Customer-centric
Describes an organisation built and measured around customers and their needs (for example by retention and lifetime value) rather than around its products.
Greenwashing
Making environmental claims about a product or business that are vague, exaggerated or unsupported by evidence.
