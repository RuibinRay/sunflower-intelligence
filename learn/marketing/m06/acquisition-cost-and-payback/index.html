Acquisition cost and payback

What does it cost to win a customer, and how long until they pay it back? Measure acquisition cost with the right costs, customers and period, blended, paid and by channel; work out payback with and without retention and discounting; read cohort payback curves; and judge the value-to-cost ratio without treating a rule of thumb as a law.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate customer acquisition cost blended, for paid channels and by channel, stating which costs, which customers and which period it covers.
  • Distinguish the average cost of the customers a campaign wins from the incremental cost of those it wins who would not have come anyway.
  • Calculate the payback period simply and with retention and discounting, and explain why the simple figure flatters.
  • Read cohort payback curves to find when a cohort’s margin per joiner first covers its acquisition cost.
  • Evaluate an acquisition plan with the value-to-cost ratio, its assumptions and their sensitivity, treating the 3:1 guideline as a convention.

Review first

  • Customer lifetime value — The value of a new customer (timing A) and a café member’s value from the cohort table.
  • Customer value, satisfaction and lifetime value — Acquisition cost as spend ÷ customers won, and comparing it with lifetime value.
  • Measuring promotion and bringing the mix together — Attribution rules and incrementality: which sales a campaign actually caused.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana is planning a spring 2028 campaign (a fictional proposal, with costs she has estimated): flyers and local social ads costing $1,800 for March to May, and a free drink for everyone who joins the app in those months, which costs the cafés $1.10 in ingredients a time. She expects 1,000 new members over the three months. Without any campaign she would expect about as many as signed up in spring 2027, 765. Is the campaign worth it?

What it costs to win a customer

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is the spending on winning new customers in a period divided by the number of new customers won. It is the other side of lifetime value: CLV says what a new customer is worth, CAC what one costs, and a business that pays more to win customers than they are worth loses money on every one, however fast it grows.

The formula is a division, and each half needs a definition. Which costs: everything the acquisition causes, not only advertising: media, design and printing, sales commissions, sign-up offers and the cost of free products, and staff time spent on it. Costs of serving the customer afterwards belong in the margin, not in CAC. Which customers: new ones, not returning or reactivated customers. Which period: spending and customers should match, allowing for a lag when people sign up weeks after seeing an advertisement.

Blended, paid and by channel

Glenmarra Climbing Centre (fictional) sells monthly memberships. From January to March it spent $8,100 on acquisition and gained 200 new members, but not all through the same route.

The blended CAC divides all acquisition spend by all new members: $8,100 ÷ 200 = $40.50. It flatters, because 85 of the 200 came with no spending at all. The paid CAC divides the spend by the members the paid channels brought: $8,100 ÷ 115 = $70.43. And by channel the costs range from $30.00 for a referral to $120.00 for a member won through social ads. A decision about the social budget needs the social figure; the blended one hides it.

Even the channel figures rest on a rule for giving credit. Here each member is assigned to the channel they named at sign-up; someone who saw a social ad and then searched for the centre counts as a search member. Attribution rules (last click, first click, shares across touchpoints) move members between channels and so move the channel CACs, which is why M02 treats them with caution.

The customers a campaign actually wins

Back to Lantern. Ana’s campaign would cost $1,800 + 1,000 × $1.10 = $2,900.00, and every joiner gets the free drink, including those who would have joined anyway. Divided by all 1,000 joiners, that is $2.90 a member. But only about 235 of the 1,000 (1,000 − 765) are members the campaign would have won; the rest would have signed up at the till regardless. The cost per member won is $2,900.00 ÷ 235 = $12.34, the incremental acquisition cost. It is the one to compare with what a member is worth, because it is the cost of the extra members the decision buys.

Key idea: Average or incremental?

Blended CAC answers “what did all our new customers cost on average?” Incremental CAC answers “what does each extra customer this spending wins cost?” For Lantern’s campaign the first is $2.90 and the second $12.34, more than four times as much. Decisions about spending more (or less) need the second, and it depends on an estimate of how many customers would have come anyway: here last spring’s 765, an assumption, and the most uncertain figure in the plan.

How long until a customer pays back?

The payback period is the time until the margin a newly won customer brings in covers what it cost to win them. The quick version divides CAC by the margin per period: a Glenmarra member won through social ads costs $120 and brings $28 of margin a month, 120/28=4.3120 / 28 = 4.3120/28=4.3, and counting the joining month as month 1 the cost is covered in month 5. That assumes every member stays. With 90% of members renewing each month and discounting at 0.8% a month, the expected discounted margin builds up more slowly: $28.00 after month 1, $95.25 after month 4, $113.05 after month 5 and $128.93 after month 6, so payback comes in month 6.

For café members, payback can be read straight from the cohort table. The figure shows the cumulative margin per joiner (discounted at 0.8% a month) of two 2027 cohorts against the campaign’s two CACs. The blended $2.90 is covered on the join visit itself. The incremental $12.34 is covered in month 2 (the third month, counting the join month as month 0) for the March joiners and in month 1 (the second) for the August joiners.

Retention matters more for payback than the simple figure admits. At 80% monthly retention instead of 90%, a social-ads member takes 10 months to pay back; at 78%, 14; and at 75% they never do, because their lifetime value falls to 28 × 1.008 ÷ 0.258 = $109.40, below the $120 they cost.

The value-to-cost ratio

Dividing the lifetime value of a new customer by CAC gives a value-to-cost ratio, often written LTV:CAC. Above 1 the customers are worth more than they cost; the margin above 1 has to cover the risk that the assumptions are wrong and the fixed costs the margin doesn’t. A widely quoted guideline, from the venture investor David Skok’s writing on software-as-a-service (SaaS) businesses, is a ratio above 3 and CAC recovered within 12 months. Skok states its conditions: it is for SaaS firms with gross margins of 80% or more, and the lifetime value in it is revenue-based, before the gross-margin adjustment, so on a margin basis like this lesson’s it corresponds to roughly 3 × 0.8 = 2.4. It is a guideline for one industry, not a law of marketing. Glenmarra’s social channel sits at 2.18 on a margin basis, below even that equivalent; search sits at 3.73.

The ratio and the payback period answer different questions. The ratio says whether customers are worth winning at all; payback says how long the cash is tied up, which matters to a small business that must fund acquisition before the margins arrive. A channel can have a good ratio and a slow payback (long-lived customers who pay little each month) or a quick payback and a poor ratio (customers who spend early and leave).

Which lifetime value?

The ceiling on acquisition spending is the full value of a new customer (timing A, or B), because winning the customer wins their first margin too. Fader and Hardie (2012) point out that some textbooks set the ceiling with the residual formula, which leaves out the first margin; that understates what winning a customer is worth. Equally, the value must be that of the customers the spending will win. Customers drawn in by a free offer, or from a channel that reaches people less interested in the product, may be worth less than the existing base.

Average and marginal

CAC usually rises as spending grows: the first advertisements reach the people most likely to respond, and each extra dollar reaches fewer. The average CAC of a campaign therefore hides the cost of its last customers. To decide whether to spend more, estimate the extra customers the extra spending would win (from a test, or from past changes in spending) and divide; M01 called this the marginal CAC. It is the same logic as the incremental CAC of Lantern’s campaign, applied to a change in the size of a budget rather than to a campaign as a whole.

Cohort payback curves

A cohort payback curve plots a cohort’s cumulative margin per joiner by months since joining, as in the figure, and the month it crosses the cohort’s CAC is its payback period. Drawing every cohort shows whether newer cohorts pay back faster or slower, which a single average would hide. For Lantern’s 2027 cohorts the incremental $12.34 is crossed in month 1 or month 2 by every cohort from March to October; November’s joiners, with only two months observed, stood at $12.29, just short. Two cautions apply: the curves are averages, so many individual joiners never pay back (of the March joiners, 60.7% had brought $12.34 of undiscounted margin by 31 December; the rest had not); and a cohort that joined in a busy month may look better for seasonal reasons, not because it was better acquired.

Limits of the numbers

  • Incrementality is an estimate. “How many would have come anyway” is the hardest input. A randomised holdout (some areas or weeks without the campaign) measures it; the last lesson of this module shows how.
  • Attribution moves costs between channels. A channel’s CAC is only as good as the rule that credited customers to it.
  • Payback ignores everything after it. Two channels with the same payback can differ greatly in value; read payback with the ratio.
  • Every figure inherits the lifetime value’s assumptions: margin, retention or decay, discount rate, timing and horizon.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing by every new customer

    Blended CAC counts customers who came with no spending, so it understates what marketing pays per customer it brings. Glenmarra’s blended $40.50 hides a $120 social-ads cost. Use the paid or channel figure, and for a decision the incremental one.

  • Counting only the advertising

    Offers, free products, design, printing, commissions and staff time are acquisition costs. Lantern’s free drinks are more than a third of the campaign’s cost; leaving them out would cut the incremental CAC from $12.34 to $7.66.

  • Using simple payback as if customers stayed

    CAC ÷ margin assumes no one leaves. With 90% monthly retention Glenmarra’s simple payback in month 5 becomes month 6; with 75% retention the member never pays back. Use the retention-weighted, discounted sum, or a cohort’s actual curve.

  • Treating 3:1 as a law

    The 3:1 guideline is one David Skok gives for software-as-a-service firms with gross margins of 80% or more, with lifetime value measured on revenue; on a margin basis it is about 2.4. The right threshold depends on how reliable the value estimate is, the fixed costs to cover and how long the cash is tied up. A 2:1 ratio with solid evidence can beat a 5:1 ratio built on hopeful retention.

  • Setting the acquisition ceiling with residual value

    Winning a customer wins their first margin too. The ceiling is the full value of a new customer; the residual formula, which leaves the first margin out, understates it (Fader and Hardie, 2012).

  • Using the average when deciding on more

    The average CAC of past spending says little about the cost of the next customers, who are usually harder to win. Estimate the extra customers the extra spending brings.

In practice

Fictional case

Ana’s recommendation to Lantern’s owners (fictional proposal): “The spring campaign would cost $2,900.00. Spread over everyone who joins it looks like $2.90 a member, but that counts the 765 or so who would have joined anyway. Counting only the extra members it should win, about 235, it costs $12.34 each. A new member has been worth about $32 of margin, and our 2027 members covered $12.34 within three months of joining, so the campaign should pay even if the extra members are worth much less than last year’s. The figure I trust least is how many would have joined anyway, so I propose running it at Millbrook only for March and comparing sign-ups with Harbourside before deciding on April and May. I’d also give the free drink only to people who bring a flyer.”

In practice acquisition cost is where marketing meets finance. Subscription businesses report CAC and payback to investors; retailers and banks set channel budgets from them; start-ups live or die by whether customers pay back before the cash runs out. The analysis that holds up is the one that says which costs and customers it counts, separates the customers spending won from those it merely touched, and states the lifetime value it compares with.

The most common error is optimism through definitions: blended CAC instead of paid, advertising without the offer costs, simple payback instead of retention-weighted, a lifetime value from the best customers applied to new ones. Each makes acquisition look cheaper or customers more valuable than they are, and each can be fixed by writing down the definition before calculating.

The most valuable habit is to measure incrementality instead of assuming it. A campaign run in some places or weeks and not others, chosen at random, shows how many customers it really adds, and the incremental CAC that follows is a fact rather than a guess. The next lesson applies the same idea to retention offers.

Summary

  • CAC = acquisition spending ÷ new customers won, with every cost the acquisition causes (offers and free products included), new customers only, and spending and customers in the same period.
  • Blended CAC divides by all new customers, paid CAC by those the paid channels brought, channel CAC by each channel’s customers (Glenmarra: $40.50, $70.43, and $30 to $120); channel figures depend on the attribution rule.
  • For a decision, use the incremental CAC: spending ÷ the customers who would not have come anyway (Lantern’s campaign: $2.90 blended, $12.34 incremental).
  • Simple payback, CAC ÷ margin, assumes no one leaves; with retention and discounting it is the first period in which the expected discounted margin covers CAC (Glenmarra social: month 5 against month 6), and if CAC exceeds lifetime value there is none.
  • Cohort payback curves show when each cohort’s cumulative margin per joiner covers its CAC; they are averages, and many individual customers never pay back.
  • The value-to-cost ratio CLV ÷ CAC says whether customers are worth winning; Skok’s 3:1 guideline is for SaaS firms with gross margins of 80% or more and revenue-based lifetime value (about 2.4 on a margin basis), a convention. Read it with payback and with the uncertainty in both figures.

Terms introduced

Incremental acquisition cost
Acquisition spending divided by the new customers it won who would not otherwise have come; the cost per extra customer, as opposed to the average over all new customers.
Payback period
The time until the margin a newly won customer brings in covers what it cost to win them; with retention and discounting, the first period in which the expected discounted margin reaches the acquisition cost.
Value-to-cost ratio
A new customer’s lifetime value divided by the cost of acquiring them, often written LTV:CAC; above 1 the customers are worth more than they cost.
