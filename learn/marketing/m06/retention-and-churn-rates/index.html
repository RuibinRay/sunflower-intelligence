Retention and churn rates

Measure how many customers a business keeps, with the right denominator and over the right horizon. See why average retention rises with tenure when customers differ, why “churn” must be defined before it can be counted when nobody cancels, and how revenue retention differs from customer retention.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate a period’s retention and churn rates from customers at the start, customers at the end and customers won during the period.
  • Convert a constant churn rate between months, quarters and years by compounding, and calculate the expected lifetime it implies.
  • Explain why a group’s retention rate rises with tenure when customers differ, even though no customer becomes more loyal.
  • Define churn for a noncontractual business with a lapse rule, and show how the choice of rule changes the figure.
  • Calculate gross and net revenue retention and say what each adds to customer retention.

Review first

  • Customer value, satisfaction and lifetime value — Retention rate, churn rate and customer lifetime value were introduced there; this lesson measures them properly.
  • Customer metrics — Recency as of a stated date, and the difference between contractual and noncontractual settings.
  • Cohort analysis — Cohorts and the share of a cohort active in each month since joining.
  • Interest and inflation — Compounding: a rate applied period after period multiplies, it doesn’t add.

The idea

Fictional case

In January 2028 Ana asks a simple question about the cafés’ loyalty app: “How many of our members are we keeping?” Three people give her three answers. The café managers say 108%, because more members visited in December than in November. Her analyst says 68.5%, the share of November’s visitors who came back in December. The app’s dashboard says 51.2% of members have “churned”. None of them has made an arithmetic mistake. Each has measured something different, and this lesson is about telling them apart.

Retention and churn for a period

The retention rate for a period is the share of the customers a business had at the start who are still customers at the end. The churn rate is its complement, the share who have gone: churn = 1 − retention. Both are about one group of people, those present at the start, followed to the end. That one sentence settles most arguments about retention, because it says what belongs in the denominator and what doesn’t.

Corriemoor Mobile (fictional) sells phone contracts. It had 12,000 customers on 1 January and 12,900 on 31 March. During the quarter it signed 2,400 new customers who were still with it on 31 March. Those 2,400 were not customers at the start, so they can’t be “retained”: of the 12,900 at the end, 12,900 − 2,400 = 10,500 were there on 1 January. Retention for the quarter is 10,500 ÷ 12,000 = 87.5%, and churn is 12.5%: Corriemoor lost 1,500 of its starting customers.

The tempting shortcut, end ÷ start = 12,900 ÷ 12,000 = 107.5%, measures growth, not retention. A business can grow while losing customers quickly, as long as it signs new ones faster; it can also shrink while keeping almost everyone, if it stops selling. The managers’ 108% for the cafés is this shortcut. Mixing the new customers into either side of the ratio is the denominator trap, and it always flatters a business that is acquiring customers.

Key idea: Who counts

Start with the customers you had when the period began. Follow only them. New customers won during the period are reported separately, as acquisition; a new customer who joins and leaves within the period appears in neither the start nor the end, but counts towards how many were won.

Months, quarters and years

A churn rate belongs to a period, and it can’t be scaled like a price. If Corriemoor keeps 87.5% of its customers each quarter, a customer is still there after a year with probability 0.875 × 0.875 × 0.875 × 0.875 = 0.586, so annual retention is 58.6% and annual churn 41.4%, not 4 × 12.5% = 50%. Churn compounds, exactly as interest does: 1−(1−c)T1 - (1 - c)^{T}1−(1−c)T over TTT periods. Going the other way, the equivalent monthly churn is 1−0.8751/31 - 0.875^{1/3}1−0.8751/3 = 4.35%, a little more than a third of the quarterly rate.

A constant churn rate also gives a customer’s expected lifetime. If each quarter a customer leaves with probability 12.5%, the number of quarters they stay, counting the first, averages 1 ÷ 0.125 = 8 quarters, or two years. The formula is the mean of a geometric distribution; it holds only if the rate really is constant, which the next section questions.

Why retention seems to grow with tenure

Businesses that follow a group of customers from the day they join almost always see the retention rate rise: a large share leave in the first period, fewer in the second, fewer again later. Fader and Hardie’s (2007) data on the “Regular” customers of an unnamed subscription business, the share of a group acquired together that is still active in each of 12 years, show it clearly. Of those who started, 63.1% renewed for a second year; of those still there, 74.2% renewed for a third, then 81.6%, and by year 12 the rate was 94.5%.

The natural reading is that customers grow more loyal the longer they stay. Often it isn’t true, and it doesn’t need to be. Suppose Thistlebank Insurance (fictional) has 1,000 new home-insurance customers of two kinds, 500 who renew each year with probability 50% and 500 who renew with probability 90%, and nobody ever changes. In year 1 retention is 0.5 × 50% + 0.5 × 90% = 70%. But the leavers are mostly the first kind, so the 700 who renew are 250 of the first kind and 450 of the second, and in year 2 the group’s retention is (125 + 405) ÷ 700 = 75.7%. In year 3 it is 80.6%, and it keeps rising towards 90%. No customer changed; the mix did.

This is the sorting effect: when customers differ, the ones likely to leave leave first, and the survivors are increasingly the loyal ones. Fader and Hardie conclude that the rise in their data is “simply due to heterogeneity”, an effect long known as the ruse of heterogeneity. It matters because a business that measures one rate and assumes it holds for ever gets the future wrong. Thistlebank’s year-1 rate of 70%, held constant, predicts that 0.7⁵ = 16.8% of the 1,000 are still insured after five renewals; in fact 31.1% are. The survival-analysis lesson builds a model that expects the rise.

The formulas, with their assumptions

Caution: Averages of rates

Don’t average monthly retention rates over months of different sizes, and don’t compare one business’s monthly churn with another’s annual churn. Convert to one horizon first, by compounding, and weight by the customers at risk.

The rise can still have other causes: customers who stay may learn to use a product more, or face costs of switching that grow with time. Aggregate data can’t tell sorting from genuine change, because both raise the group’s rate. What the sorting effect shows is that rising retention is not evidence that customers become more loyal, and that a single average rate, used to project far ahead, will understate how many customers remain.

When nobody cancels: defining churn

Lantern’s loyalty-app members never tell the cafés that they have stopped coming; they just stop. In this noncontractual setting a customer’s departure is never observed, only inferred from silence, and a silence can end. So “churn” has no meaning until someone defines it. The two common definitions give very different numbers.

  • Active this period, active next period. Of the 905 members who visited in November 2027, 620 visited again in December: 68.5%. This is the analyst’s figure. But a month without a visit is not a departure. Of the 314 members who visited in September and not in October, 91 (29.0%) visited again in November or December. “Churn” of 31.5% a month would badly overstate how many members Lantern is losing.
  • A lapse rule. A member has lapsed on a date if they haven’t visited for more than L days. On 31 December 2027, with L = 60, 1,324 of the 2,584 members (51.2%) had lapsed: the dashboard’s figure. With L = 30 it would be 1,609 (62.3%); with L = 90, 1,069 (41.4%). The rule, not the members, decides the answer.

The month-to-month version also hides a denominator trap of its own. December had 975 active members: 202 who joined in December, 620 who had also visited in November, and 153 who came back after skipping November. The contractual formula, (975 − 202) ÷ 905 = 85.4%, treats the 153 returners as if they had been kept from November, which they weren’t. In a noncontractual setting the end of a period has three kinds of customer, not two: kept, new and reactivated.

A good lapse rule is chosen from the business, checked against later behaviour, and stated every time the figure is quoted. Choose LLL long enough that few “lapsed” customers come back: of the 909 members who on 31 October had been silent for more than 60 days, 68 (7.5%) visited in November or December. A rule that wrongly writes off 7.5% of its lapsers may be acceptable for counting; for deciding whom to send a win-back offer, the members it misclassifies are exactly the ones who matter. The Predicting churn lesson models the chance of coming back instead of drawing one line.

Members, too, look more loyal with tenure

The sorting effect appears in the members’ data. Pooling all cohorts, of the members who visited in the month they joined, 60.5% visited the next month; of those who visited in their fourth month after joining, 70.8% visited in their fifth. Pooled curves mix cohorts of different ages, so the later months come only from the earlier joiners. Restricting to the members who joined from March to July, who can all be followed for five months, gives the same start, 60.5%, and necessarily the same end, 70.8%, since only March–July joiners reach their fifth month; in between it is not a steady climb (64.1% in the second month, then 62.1%). The data were generated with each member visiting at a fixed rate and having a fixed chance of dropping out after each visit, so the rise can’t be growing loyalty: it is mostly sorting, with some help from the calendar, since each month since joining falls in different calendar months for different members.

Revenue retention

Customer retention counts heads. Businesses whose customers pay different and changing amounts also track how much of the revenue from existing customers they keep. Take the recurring revenue from customers at the start of a period. Gross revenue retention (GRR) keeps what is left after customers who leave and customers who downgrade, and can’t exceed 100%. Net revenue retention (NRR) also adds what continuing customers spend on upgrades, so it can exceed 100%. New customers are excluded from both, for the same reason as before.

Tarnfield Software (fictional) began the year with $400,000 of annual recurring revenue from 200 customers. Eighteen customers, worth $36,000, left; continuing customers downgraded by $12,000 and upgraded by $58,000; 30 new customers brought $90,000. Customer retention was 182 ÷ 200 = 91.0%. GRR was (400,000 − 36,000 − 12,000) ÷ 400,000 = 88.0% and NRR (352,000 + 58,000) ÷ 400,000 = 102.5%. Total revenue grew 25.0%, to $500,000, but most of that growth was bought with new customers. Definitions of these measures vary between companies (some use monthly revenue, some exclude price rises), so read the definition before comparing two firms’ figures.

Common mistakes

  • Counting new customers as retained

    End ÷ start, or (end − start) ÷ start, mixes acquisition into retention. Remove the customers won during the period from the end before dividing by the start.

  • Scaling churn by multiplying

    Monthly churn of 3% is annual churn of 1 − 0.97¹² = 30.6%, not 36%. Rates compound; convert with powers, not multiplication.

  • Reading rising retention as growing loyalty

    A group’s retention rises with tenure whenever customers differ, because the likeliest leavers go first. Projecting the first period’s rate for ever understates how many customers remain; reading the rise as each customer’s loyalty growing may be wrong.

  • Quoting a noncontractual churn figure without its rule

    “51% churn” is meaningless until the lapse rule is stated: 51.2% of members had not visited for more than 60 days on 31 December, but 62.3% for 30 days and 41.4% for 90. And a missed month is not a lost customer.

  • Treating net revenue retention as customer retention

    NRR above 100% can coexist with many customers leaving, if those who stay spend more. Report customer retention and GRR alongside it.

In practice

Retention figures go into budgets, valuations and bonuses, so the definition behind them is a business decision, not a technicality. A telecoms finance team that forecasts subscriber numbers needs churn on the same horizon as its forecast, compounded correctly, and from the customers at the start. A software company reporting net revenue retention to investors needs its definition fixed in advance, so the figure can’t be flattered by moving upgrades or price rises in and out.

The sorting effect changes how managers should read their dashboards. A rising retention rate among long-standing customers is good news about who they are, not necessarily about anything the business did; a falling rate among new customers may reflect who was recruited (a promotion that brought in deal-seekers) rather than worse service. Comparing cohorts at the same tenure, as the cohort-analysis lesson does, separates the two better than one blended rate.

Fictional case

Ana’s report to the board defines its terms. “Retention” is used only for contractual products, which start and stop. For the app’s members it reports two labelled measures: the share of each month’s visitors who visit the next month (68.5% from November to December), and the share lapsed on 31 December under a 60-day rule (51.2%), with the note that 7.5% of members past 60 days come back within two months. She adds a cohort chart instead of a single rate, because the members who joined in December can’t yet have lapsed under a 60-day rule.

In a noncontractual business, a lapse rule is a tool for counting, and every tool has an error rate. Choose L from how often customers normally buy (a café sees members weekly; a furniture shop might wait years), check how many “lapsed” customers come back, and keep the rule fixed so that figures from different years compare. When a decision depends on individual customers, such as whom to send an offer, replace the line with a prediction, which the next two lessons build.

Summary

  • Retention for a period = (customers at the end − those won during the period) ÷ customers at the start; churn = 1 − retention. End ÷ start measures growth (Corriemoor: 87.5% retention, 107.5% growth).
  • Churn compounds: over T periods it is 1 − (1 − c)^T (12.5% a quarter is 41.4% a year, not 50%). With constant churn c the expected lifetime is 1 ÷ c periods.
  • When customers differ, the group’s retention rises with tenure because the likeliest leavers go first (Fader and Hardie’s Regular customers: 63.1% in year 1, 94.5% by year 12). Projecting an early rate understates how many remain (Thistlebank: 16.8% projected, 31.1% actual).
  • In a noncontractual setting churn is never observed and must be defined: a lapse rule of L days without a purchase. The rule decides the figure (51.2% of members at 60 days, 41.4% at 90), so state it and check how many “lapsed” customers come back.
  • Month-to-month activity is not retention: missed months end, and December’s active members include new and reactivated members.
  • Gross revenue retention removes lost and downgraded revenue; net revenue retention adds upgrades and can exceed 100%. Both exclude new customers.

Terms introduced

Expected lifetime
The average number of periods a new customer stays, counting the first; with a constant churn rate c per period it is 1 ÷ c.
Sorting effect
The rise in a group’s retention rate with tenure that occurs when customers differ in their chance of leaving: those most likely to leave do so first, so the survivors are increasingly the loyal ones, even if no customer changes.
Lapse rule
A definition of churn for a noncontractual business: a customer counts as lapsed on a date if they have not bought for more than a stated number of days, L.
Gross revenue retention
The share of the recurring revenue from customers at the start of a period that remains at the end after losses from customers who leave and downgrade; new customers and upgrades are excluded, so it cannot exceed 100%.
Net revenue retention
The recurring revenue at the end of a period from customers who were there at the start, including their upgrades and net of losses and downgrades, divided by their revenue at the start; it can exceed 100%.
