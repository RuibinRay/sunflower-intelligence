Survival analysis

Measure how long customers stay when some of them haven’t left yet. Estimate a survival curve with the Kaplan–Meier method, compare groups with the log-rank test, define lapsing for customers who never cancel, and project contractual retention with a model that lets customers differ.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain censoring and show why dropping censored customers, or counting them as churned, biases estimates of how long customers stay.
  • Calculate a Kaplan–Meier survival curve step by step, read the median lifetime from it, and interpret Greenwood standard errors and confidence intervals.
  • Compare the survival of two groups with the log-rank test and state what the result does and doesn’t show.
  • Define lapsing for a noncontractual business and explain how the length of the lapse rule changes the survival curve.
  • Apply the shifted-beta-geometric model to project contractual retention, and evaluate it against constant retention on periods not used for fitting.

Review first

  • Retention and churn rates — Retention and churn rates, lapse rules, and why a group’s retention rises when customers differ.
  • Proportions and chi-square tests — Chi-square tests of observed against expected counts, the idea behind the log-rank test.
  • Confidence intervals — Standard errors and 95% intervals.
  • Logistic regression — Maximum likelihood, used to fit the sBG model.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana wants to know how long a new loyalty-app member keeps coming to the cafés. The obvious calculation, the average time from joining to the last visit, gives a strangely short answer, and it is short for a reason: the members who are still coming haven’t had their last visit yet. A member who joined in November can’t have been a member for more than two months, however loyal. Any honest answer has to use what is known about them, which is that they are still here, without pretending to know when they will stop.

Time to churn and censoring

Survival analysis studies the time until an event, here a customer leaving. For each customer it needs two things: a time, and whether the event was seen. A customer who left after 7 months contributes a time of 7 and an event. A customer who joined 5 months ago and is still a customer contributes a time of 5 and no event: all we know is that their lifetime is longer than 5 months. That incomplete observation is censoring, and almost every customer dataset has it, because the data stop on a date while customers are still active.

Glaisdale Climbing Centre (fictional) follows ten members who joined at different times over the past year. Five have cancelled, after 2, 3, 5, 7 and 10 months. Five are still members today, having joined 3, 6, 8, 12 and 12 months ago, so their lifetimes are at least that long. Two shortcuts both mislead. Dropping the five censored members and looking only at the cancellers says the median membership lasts 5 months and that nobody stays beyond 10: it keeps only the people who left. Counting the five as if they cancelled today gives a median of 6 months: it invents departures. Both answers are too short.

The Kaplan–Meier estimator

The Kaplan–Meier estimator uses every member for exactly as long as they were watched. At each time when someone leaves, it asks: of the members still being watched just before then (the risk set), what share stayed? The survival function S(t), the share of members expected still to be members after time t, is the running product of those shares.

The member censored at month 3 is still counted at risk at month 3 (they were there when the month-3 cancellation happened) and leaves the risk set afterwards; those censored at 6 and 8 leave it before months 7 and 10.

After month 2, 9 of 10 remain: S = 0.900. At month 3, 8 of the 9 still at risk stay: S = 0.900 × 8/9 = 0.800. Between months 3 and 5 two members leave the risk set, one by cancelling at month 3 and one by being censored, so 7 are at risk at month 5 and S = 0.800 × 6/7 = 0.686. The censored members never cause a drop; they simply stop being counted. The median lifetime is the first time at which S falls to 0.5 or below: 10 months, twice what the cancellers-only shortcut said.

Members who never cancel: lapsing

Lantern’s app members don’t cancel, so the event has to be defined, as the previous lesson did, with a lapse rule. Here a member lapses at a visit that is followed by more than L days without another; their lifetime is the days from joining to that visit. A member whose last visit is within L days of 31 December 2027 hasn’t lapsed yet and is censored at their last visit. With L = 60, of the 2,584 members, 1,540 had lapsed and 1,044 were censored. 469 lapsed at day 0: they joined and didn’t come back within 60 days. All 423 members who joined in November or December are censored; none can have had 60 days of silence yet.

The Kaplan–Meier curve for the members starts with that drop at day 0, to 81.8%, and falls fastest in the first weeks: 61.1% are still active (not lapsed) at 30 days, 38.1% at 90 days and 25.4% at 180. The median lifetime is 52 days. The shortcuts are wrong here by even more than at Glaisdale: using only the members who lapsed gives a median of 17 days, and counting the censored members as lapsed gives 31.

The rule matters as much as the method. With L = 30 the median is 20 days; with L = 90 it is 93 days. A short rule calls every holiday a lapse; a long one can’t confirm a lapse until L days have passed, so more members are censored and recent lapses go unrecognised. The curve moves up as L grows: S(30) is 40.6%, 61.1% and 70.3% for L = 30, 60 and 90. The survival widget below lets you change L and watch the curves move.

Comparing groups

Members with notifications on (1,418) and off (1,166) give two curves. With L = 60 the medians are 57 and 48 days, and 41.1% against 34.5% are still active at 90 days. The log-rank test asks whether the curves differ by more than chance: at each lapse time it compares the lapses seen in each group with those expected if both groups shared one curve, and adds up the differences. Members with notifications on had 815 lapses against 871.0 expected; χ² = 8.83 on 1 df, p = 0.003. The difference is unlikely to be chance.

Caution: A difference in survival is an association

Members chose whether to turn notifications on. The log-rank test says the two groups’ curves differ; it can’t say that turning notifications on would make a member stay longer. Keener members may both choose notifications and keep coming. Only a randomised test, as in B06, measures the effect of switching them on.

  • Censoring must be uninformative. The method assumes that a censored customer’s chance of churning is the same as that of customers still watched. Censoring because the data end on a date is usually fine. Censoring because a customer moved to a partner business, or because their records were deleted when they complained, may not be.
  • Late entrants. Customers who joined late are watched for a short time and censored early. Kaplan–Meier uses them for the early part of the curve only; the tail rests on the earliest joiners. If the business changed, the tail describes the old customers.
  • The curve stops where the data stop. If the last observation is censored, the curve never reaches 0, and the median is “not reached” if it never falls to 0.5. The mean lifetime can’t be estimated without assumptions about the tail.

The log-rank test

At each event time, the risk sets give the expected churners in each group if both shared one curve: group A’s share of those at risk times all churners at that time. Summing gives the observed (O) and expected (E) churn for each group. The simplest form of the statistic is ∑(O−E)2/E\sum (O - E)^2 / E∑(O−E)2/E over the groups, compared with a chi-square distribution on (groups − 1) degrees of freedom; software uses a slightly more exact variance, which this course’s engine and the widget use. The test compares whole curves, weighting every event time equally. It is weak when curves cross (one group lapses early and the other late), so always look at the curves as well, and it gives a p-value, not the size of a difference: report the medians or survival at chosen times alongside it.

For the cafés, the conclusion depends on L. Millbrook members (median 54 days with L = 60) and Harbourside members (51 days) differ with χ² = 5.30, p = 0.021, at L = 60; at L = 30 the difference is stark (χ² = 23.90, p below 0.001); at L = 90 it isn’t clear (χ² = 2.34, p = 0.126). The notifications difference holds at all three (p = 0.004, 0.003 and 0.031). A finding that survives a range of sensible lapse rules is more trustworthy than one that appears at only one.

Projecting contractual retention

Kaplan–Meier describes the past; it can’t say what happens after the last observation. For contractual customers, who renew or don’t at known periods, a model can. The simplest assumes a constant retention rate r, so S(t)=rtS(t) = r^tS(t)=rt. The previous lesson showed why this fails when customers differ: the group’s retention rises as the flighty leave.

The shifted-beta-geometric model (sBG) of Fader and Hardie (2007) builds that in. Each customer has their own churn probability θ\thetaθ, the same in every period, so their lifetime is geometric. Across customers, θ\thetaθ follows a beta distribution with parameters α\alphaα and β\betaβ, a flexible family of distributions on 0 to 1; the mean churn probability is α/(α+β)\alpha/(\alpha + \beta)α/(α+β). Averaged over customers, the share still active after ttt periods and the retention rate in period ttt are

Fader and Hardie show the model can be fitted in a spreadsheet.

Their High End customers, fitted to years 0–7 only, give α = 0.668 and β = 3.806 (standard errors 0.104 and 0.786), so the average new customer has a 14.9% chance of leaving in a year, with retention rising from 85.1% in year 1 to 94.2% in year 8. The model projects that 37.8% of the customers are still active after 12 years; the actual figure, unseen when fitting, is 39.4%. A constant retention rate fitted to the same seven years (89.7% a year) projects 27.0%. For the Regular customers (α = 0.704, β = 1.182) the sBG projects 17.0% against an actual 17.3%, and constant retention 4.6%. Fader and Hardie report that regression curves fitted to survival over time project far worse, and that the sBG’s year-12 figures are within 4% (High End) and 2% (Regular) of the truth. The paper says the sBG “overestimates” year-12 survival; its fitted values are in fact slightly below the actual ones, as the figures above show.

Note: A typo in the source

The paper’s text gives α = 0.688 for the High End segment; its appendix, and the fit in this course’s engine, give 0.668. Use 0.668.

The sBG is for contractual settings, where a customer is either renewed or gone. For noncontractual customers such as the cafés’ members, the equivalent models (the Pareto/NBD and BG/NBD families; Fader, Hardie and Lee 2005) describe each customer’s buying and their unseen drop-out together, and give from recency and frequency the probability that a customer is still active; the members’ data were in fact generated from a process of that kind. They are beyond this course; the next lesson predicts lapsing with logistic regression instead.

Common mistakes

  • Averaging the lifetimes of customers who have left

    It keeps only the customers who left, and so the shortest lifetimes. At Lantern it gives a median of 17 days against Kaplan–Meier’s 52. Use every customer, with the censored ones counted for as long as they were watched.

  • Treating censored customers as churned

    A customer still active when the data end hasn’t churned. Counting them as churned at that point invents departures and shortens lifetimes (a median of 31 days for the members).

  • Reading the tail of the curve as firmly as the start

    The late part of a Kaplan–Meier curve rests on the few earliest customers still watched; its interval is wide. Look at the standard errors and the numbers at risk before quoting survival at long times.

  • Treating a significant log-rank test as an effect of the grouping

    Survival curves of self-selected groups (notifications on and off) differ for many reasons. The test shows a difference, not its cause.

  • Projecting with the average retention rate

    When customers differ, retention rises with tenure, and one rate fitted to early periods understates later survival badly (27.0% against 39.4% for the High End customers after 12 years). Use a model that allows heterogeneity, such as the sBG, and check it on periods not used to fit it.

In practice

Survival analysis is how subscription businesses, insurers, banks and telecoms describe customer lifetimes, because their data are always censored: most customers are still customers. An analyst asked “how long do our customers stay?” answers with a Kaplan–Meier curve and its median, with the numbers at risk printed under it, and says what counts as the event and when observation stops. Comparing curves by acquisition channel, plan or region is often the first diagnostic of where churn comes from.

Fictional case

Ana adds a survival chart to her report: the share of app members still active by days since joining, under a 60-day rule, with the medians for notifications on and off (57 and 48 days) and a note that the difference holds under 30- and 90-day rules but is an association. She leaves out the café comparison, which changes with the rule. For planning she uses survival at fixed times rather than the median: of every 100 new members, about 61 are still active after a month and about 38 after three months.

For contractual businesses, projection is where the money is. Budgets, investor valuations and acquisition targets all depend on how many of today’s customers will still be there in three or five years. A constant churn rate is the default in most spreadsheets, and it is biased downwards whenever customers differ, which is almost always. The sBG needs only the counts of a cohort still active in each period, fits in a spreadsheet, and should be judged as Fader and Hardie judge it: fitted to the early periods, tested on the later ones.

Two cautions travel with every survival analysis. Know why observations are censored, because censoring linked to churn (customers who switch to a partner, accounts closed after a complaint) biases the curve. And keep the event definition fixed: for a noncontractual business the lapse rule is part of the result, and curves made with different rules can’t be compared.

Summary

  • Survival data record, for each customer, a time and whether churn was seen. Customers still active when the data end are censored: their lifetime is at least the time observed.
  • Dropping censored customers or counting them as churned both make lifetimes look too short (members’ median: 17 or 31 days, against 52).
  • Kaplan–Meier: Ŝ(t) = Π(1 − d_j ÷ n_j) over event times up to t, with censored customers in the risk set until they leave it. Greenwood’s formula gives the standard error; log–log limits stay inside 0 to 1.
  • For a noncontractual business the event is a lapse: a visit followed by more than L days without one. L changes the curve (members’ median 20, 52 or 93 days for L = 30, 60 or 90).
  • The log-rank test compares observed with expected churn in each group under one shared curve (notifications: χ² = 8.83, p = 0.003). It shows a difference, not a cause; check it under other lapse rules.
  • For contractual retention, the sBG gives each customer a fixed churn probability drawn from a beta distribution, so retention rises with tenure; fitted to years 0–7 of Fader and Hardie’s High End data it projects 37.8% after 12 years against an actual 39.4%, while constant retention projects 27.0%.

Terms introduced

Censoring
Incomplete observation of a time to an event: the event had not happened when observation stopped, so the time is known only to exceed the time observed. Customers still active when the data end are censored.
Survival function
S(t), the probability that the event (such as churn) has not happened by time t; for a group of customers, the share expected still to be customers after time t.
Kaplan–Meier estimator
A nonparametric estimate of the survival function from censored data: the running product, over the times at which events occur, of the share of those at risk who did not have the event.
Log-rank test
A test of whether two or more groups share one survival curve, comparing the events observed in each group with those expected at each event time if they did; the statistic is compared with a chi-square distribution.
Shifted-beta-geometric model
A model of contractual retention in which each customer has a fixed probability of leaving at each renewal, and these probabilities vary across customers according to a beta distribution; abbreviated sBG. It implies retention rates that rise with tenure.
