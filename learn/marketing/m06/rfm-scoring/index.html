RFM scoring

Score every customer from 1 to 5 on recency, frequency and monetary value, relative to the rest of the base: set quintile cut points, handle ties, reverse recency, and choose between 125 cells, a combined score and the R × FM grid. See why scores depend on the method, the number of groups and the date.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain where RFM comes from and why recency is usually put first.
  • Calculate quintile cut points with PERCENTILE.INC and score customers 1–5 on recency, frequency and monetary value, reversing recency.
  • Compare quantile cut points with equal-sized groups (NTILE), and explain how each handles ties and why frequency’s groups are uneven.
  • Combine scores as 125 RFM cells, a combined or weighted score, or the R × FM grid, and judge what each hides.
  • Explain why scores are relative to a customer base and an as-of date, and how changing the number of groups changes them.

Review first

  • Customer metrics — Scores are calculated from the customer table: recency, frequency and monetary value as of a stated date.
  • Psychographic and behavioural segmentation — RFM is a behavioural segmentation base: usage rate and loyalty status measured from records.
  • Descriptive statistics and regression in Excel — Percentiles and PERCENTILE.INC give the cut points.
  • Functions, logic, text and dates — Nested IFs turn a value and its cut points into a score.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

The café managers asked Ana for a list of Lantern’s best members. A list of the top spenders would be dominated by members who joined before September (441 of the top 517, 85.3%, against 64.4% of all members); a list of the most recent visitors over-represents the newest joiners (December’s are 7.8% of members but 22.4% of the most recent fifth). She wants a way to describe every one of the 2,584 members on all three behaviours at once, in terms anyone in the cafés can read.

RFM analysis does that. Each customer gets three scores, usually from 1 to 5: one for recency (R), one for frequency (F) and one for monetary value (M), where 5 means among the most recent, the most frequent or the biggest spenders in the base. A member scored 5-4-3 visited very recently, comes often and spends a middling amount.

The method comes from direct marketing. Catalogue and mail-order companies had to decide whom to send the next mailing to, and used each customer’s past buying to choose. Fader, Hardie and Lee (2005) describe RFM as drawn from the direct-marketing literature, and note, citing Hughes (2000), that it is called RFM rather than FRM because recency is usually the most powerful of the three.

From values to scores

A score ranks a customer against the others. The usual recipe sorts customers by a value and cuts the range at four cut points: the 20th, 40th, 60th and 80th percentiles. These divide the base into quintiles, five groups of about a fifth each. A value scores 1 plus the number of cut points it is above. For frequency and monetary value, high is good, so the score rises with the value; for recency, low is good, so the scale is reversed: the most recent fifth score 5.

Member A1694 (recency 15 days, 4 visits, $18.15) is above one recency cut point (9) but not the second (36), so the unreversed score is 2 and R = 6 − 2 = 4. Four visits is above the cut points 2 and 3 but not 6: F = 3. $18.15 is above $6.93 and $13.36 but not $24.44: M = 3. Written as a cell, A1694 is 433.

Ties make uneven groups

Quintiles are only “a fifth each” when the values are spread out. Frequency isn’t: 475 members visited once and 382 twice, and the first cut point is 2. Everyone at or below a cut point shares the lower score, so F1 holds 857 members (33.2%) and F2 only 267. Recency and monetary value have few ties, and their groups come out close to 517 each.

You can force equal groups instead by sorting the customers and dealing them out in fifths (SQL’s NTILE does this). Then the members with two visits are split between F1 and F2 by nothing more than the order the rows happened to be in. Neither choice is wrong; each costs something, and the scores depend on which you make.

Three scores, or one?

With five scores each, R, F and M make 5 × 5 × 5 = 125 RFM cells, from 111 to 555. That is too many to act on, and at Lantern only 71 of the 125 are used, 16 of them by fewer than 10 members, while 555 holds 184 and 111 holds 162. Analysts usually compress. A combined RFM score adds the three (3 to 15) or weights them. An FM score averages F and M, because at Lantern (and in most businesses) frequent visitors are the big spenders: the two scores are equal for 66.1% of members. R and FM then form a 5 × 5 grid that keeps recency separate, the grid the next lesson names.

Key idea: A score is relative

An R5 member is in about the most recent fifth of this base on this date. Add new members, change the window or move the as-of date, and the cut points move: a member can lose a score without doing anything. Compare scores only within one scoring run.

In depth

Quantile cut points

With k groups, the cut points are the k − 1 percentiles at 1/k, 2/k, …, (k − 1)/k. Excel’s PERCENTILE.INC (the same rule as QUARTILE.INC, NumPy’s and R’s default) sorts the n values and, for a percentile p, finds position (n − 1)p counting from 0, interpolating between neighbours when it falls between two. A value v then scores

S(v)=1+#{ j:v>cj }S(v) = 1 + \#\{\, j : v > c_j \,\}S(v)=1+#{j:v>cj​}

so a value exactly on a cut point stays in the lower group (the interval is closed on the right). Ties always share a score, which is the main argument for this method: two customers who behaved identically get the same score. The cost is unequal groups wherever values pile up.

Equal-sized groups

The alternative sorts customers and deals them into k groups of near-equal size; SQL’s NTILE makes the first groups one larger when n doesn’t divide by k. Every score then has a fifth of the base, which suits a campaign that must mail exactly a fifth. The cost falls on ties: at Lantern, the 382 members with two visits are split 42 into F1 and 340 into F2. Which ones go down depends on the sort’s tie-break, here the order of the rows: the 42 earliest in the row order (all joined by 13 April) score F1. Microsoft’s documentation describes NTILE as nondeterministic: a database that returns tied rows in no fixed order can give a different answer each time it runs, so add a tie-breaker such as the customer ID.

The boundary convention

Where a value lands when it equals a cut point is a convention, and for heavily tied values it matters more than the method. A lookup with MATCH against a table of lower bounds (0, then the four cut points) puts a value on a cut point in the higher group. For Lantern’s frequency that moves 881 members (everyone with exactly 2, 3, 6 or 11 visits) up a score, and F1 shrinks to the 475 one-visit members. For recency it moves 60 members and for spend none, because few values sit exactly on those cut points. Pick one convention, write it down and use it every time.

Reversing recency

Recency is the only one of the three where a small value is good. Either score it as usual and reverse (R = k + 1 − S), or write the tests the other way round (5 if recency ≤ the first cut point, 4 if ≤ the second, and so on). Both give the same scores. Forgetting to reverse is the commonest RFM error: it labels the members who left long ago as the best.

How many groups?

Five is a habit, not a rule; deciles are also used, and Fader, Hardie and Lee (2005) use three groups on each dimension for their example. More groups make finer distinctions but sparser cells: at Lantern three groups use 21 of 27 cells, four use 40 of 64 and five 71 of 125. Heavily tied values also limit k: with three groups, frequency’s cut points are 3 and 7 and the groups hold 1,124, 627, 833 members; with four, 857, 476, 612, 639.

Combining the three

  • 125 cells keep every detail but are too many to act on and spread customers thinly (16 of Lantern’s used cells hold fewer than 10 members).
  • A combined score, R + F + M from 3 to 15, ranks everyone on one scale but hides the profile: A1694 (433) and A0774 (343) both total 10, one recent but lighter, the other heavier but quieter.
  • A weighted score, such as 3R + 2F + M, encodes a judgement that recency matters more. There are no standard weights; choose them from evidence, such as how well each score predicted response to a past campaign, and test them again later.
  • FM is the rounded mean of F and M (halves round up), which works because the two measure much the same thing: their rank correlation at Lantern is 0.96, and F and M differ by at most one for 98.8% of members. The R × FM grid has 25 cells, holding 514, 491, 497, 496, 586 members in FM 1 to 5.

Lantern’s own data agree with the direct-marketing lore about recency. Score the members who had joined by 31 August as of 31 October, and see who visited in November or December: 86.8% of R5 members came back against 3.3% of R1, a much wider spread than across F scores (58.6% against 13.9%) or M scores (57.7% against 11.6%). By the combined score, 92.4% of those scoring 15 came back and 6.5% of those scoring 3. These are associations in one period; the lessons on RFM segments and predicting churn check scores against later behaviour properly.

Monetary value: total or average?

This course scores M on total spend, so M largely repeats F. Fader, Hardie and Lee define monetary value as the average spend per transaction and treat it as separate from how often someone buys. At Lantern the average ticket barely relates to frequency (rank correlation -0.14): scoring it would add information that total spend doesn’t. Either is defensible; say which you used.

Scores depend on the base and the date

Scored on 31 October 2027, when 2,161 members had joined, the cut points were 8, 25, 64, 125 days for recency, 1, 3, 5, 10 visits and $6.55, $12.60, $22.55, $42.45. By 31 December they were 9, 36, 94.8, 171; 2, 3, 6, 11; and $6.93, $13.36, $24.44, $47.02. Of the 1,324 October members who didn’t visit in November or December, 557 had a lower R score on 31 December, and 327 a different F score although their frequency hadn’t changed. Member A0082 went from 555 to 355; member A0001, with two visits in March, from 122 to 112, because the first frequency cut point rose from 1 to 2.

For one decision on one date, relative scores are exactly what you want: they rank the base. To track customers over time, either fix the cut points from a reference date and reuse them (the engine’s “cuts” method), or track the underlying recency, frequency and spend.

In Excel

  1. Lay the sheet out as in Explore: recency in B, visits in C and spend in D (rows 2–2585), the percentiles 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 in F2:F5, and the recency, visits and spend cut points in H, I and J.
  2. Cut points: =PERCENTILE.INC($C$2:$C$2585,F2) in I2, filled down to I5 (and the same with columns B and D in H and J).
  3. A score with nested IFs: =IF(C2<=$I$2,1,IF(C2<=$I$3,2,IF(C2<=$I$4,3,IF(C2<=$I$5,4,5)))). The same in one function: =1+COUNTIF($I$2:$I$5,"<"&C2), which counts the cut points below the value.
  4. Recency reversed: =5-COUNTIF($H$2:$H$5,"<"&B2), or nested IFs that give 5 to the smallest values.
  5. MATCH with lower bounds: put 0 in I1 and use =MATCH(C2,$I$1:$I$5,1). It is compact, but a value equal to a cut point scores one higher than with the formulas above; for frequency that changes many scores.
  6. FM: =ROUND((F2+G2)/2,0), which rounds 3.5 up to 4 as Excel’s ROUND rounds halves away from zero. A cell label: =R2&F2&M2 joins the three scores as text.

Common mistakes

  • “R5 means the member visited long ago.”

    Recency is the one variable where small is good. Reverse it (R = 6 − S) or write the tests the other way; check that the members with the smallest recency score 5.

  • “Quintiles always hold a fifth of the customers each.”

    Only without ties. With quantile cut points Lantern’s F1 holds 33.2% of members because 475 visited once and 382 twice. Equal groups are possible, but only by giving identical customers different scores.

  • “A value on a cut point goes in the higher group.”

    That is MATCH’s convention on a table of lower bounds, not PERCENTILE.INC’s cut-point rule, and for frequency it moves 881 Lantern members. Choose one convention and state it.

  • “Two customers with a combined score of 10 are equally valuable.”

    A1694 (433) and A0774 (343) both score 10: one recent and lighter, one heavier and quieter. Keep the cells, or at least R and FM, alongside any combined score.

  • “Her score fell, so she is buying less.”

    Scores are relative to the base and the date. Member A0001 went from 122 to 112 between October and December without any change in the member’s visits: the cut points moved. Track the underlying values for individuals.

  • “RFM shows why customers behave as they do.”

    RFM describes past behaviour. It says nothing about why a member stopped coming, and a high score is not a cause of anything: it predicts, roughly, what comes next.

In practice

Fictional case

Ana’s first scoring run goes to the café managers as one page: the cut points, the number of members with each score, and the R × FM grid shaded by members and by spend. The recipe is printed under the grid: quintile cut points (PERCENTILE.INC), a value on a cut point in the lower group, recency reversed, M on total spend since joining, FM = the rounded mean of F and M, as of the last day of the month.

The grid shows the base at a glance. On 31 December, 227 members sit in the top-right cell (R5, FM5) and bring 27.6% of members’ spend; 162 sit in the bottom-left (R1, FM1), members who came once or twice long ago. The Millbrook manager asked why so many cells near the middle were thin: frequency’s ties push members into F1 and F2.

She resists over-reading individual scores. A manager who saw his favourite regular drop from R5 to R4 over the Christmas fortnight was shown the underlying days: two weeks without a visit had moved her past the first recency cut point, nine days. The score was right about the base and too coarse for a judgement about one person.

Direct marketers used the same scores to decide whom to mail. A mail-order company (fictional numbers) mails every cell in a test, records each cell’s response rate, and next time mails only cells whose rate beats the break-even rate: a mailing that costs $0.80 and earns $20 of margin per response breaks even at 0.80 ÷ 20 = 4%. Lantern has no mailing cost, but the logic of testing a score against a later outcome before acting on it carries over.

The next lesson names the grid’s cells (Champions, At risk, Hibernating and others), sizes them, and asks what Lantern should do with each, and with whom it should do nothing.

Summary

  • RFM scores each customer 1–5 on recency, frequency and monetary value against the rest of the base, 5 best; it comes from direct marketing, where recency was usually the strongest predictor.
  • Quintile cut points are the 20th, 40th, 60th and 80th percentiles (PERCENTILE.INC); a value scores 1 plus the number of cut points it is above, and recency is reversed.
  • Ties share a score under cut points, so heavily tied variables such as frequency give unequal groups; equal-sized groups (NTILE) split ties by row order instead.
  • Whether a value on a cut point goes up or down is a convention that can move many scores; state it.
  • 125 cells are too many to act on; a combined or weighted score hides the profile; FM works because F and M largely agree, and the R × FM grid keeps recency separate.
  • Scores are relative to the base, the window and the date: cut points move, so compare scores only within one run, and track underlying values for individuals.

Terms introduced

RFM analysis
Scoring customers on the recency, frequency and monetary value of their past purchases, relative to the rest of the customer base, to rank and group them.
RFM score
A customer’s score on recency, frequency or monetary value, usually 1 to 5 with 5 best, from the customer’s position among all customers on a stated date.
Cut point
A value that divides a sorted variable into groups; for quintiles, the 20th, 40th, 60th and 80th percentiles.
Quintile
One of five groups formed by cutting sorted values at the 20th, 40th, 60th and 80th percentiles; with ties, the groups need not be equal.
RFM cell
The combination of a customer’s three RFM scores written as a label, such as 534: with five scores each there are 125 cells.
Combined RFM score
The sum of a customer’s R, F and M scores (3 to 15 with five groups), or a weighted sum.
FM score
The rounded mean of a customer’s frequency and monetary scores, used with the recency score to form a 5 × 5 grid.
