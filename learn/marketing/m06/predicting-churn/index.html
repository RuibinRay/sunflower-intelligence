Predicting churn

Predict which customers will lapse from what you knew before they did. Set up calibration and holdout periods without leakage, fit a logistic regression and a simpler recency rule, judge the score with the base rate, lift, gains and AUC, and use it to choose whom to contact, knowing that the likeliest to leave are not necessarily the ones an offer would change.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Set up a churn prediction with a calibration period for the predictors and a later holdout period for the outcome, and identify features that leak.
  • Build and interpret a logistic regression of lapsing on recency, frequency, spend, tenure and member characteristics, and compare it with a recency-only rule.
  • Evaluate a churn score with the base rate, a lift table, a gains curve and the AUC, on customers not used to fit it, and describe its calibration.
  • Calculate the expected value of contacting a customer from the chance they lapse, the chance an offer changes that, the value kept and the cost.
  • Explain why the customers most likely to churn are not necessarily those a retention offer would change, and why measuring that needs a randomised control group.

Review first

  • Logistic regression — Log-odds, odds ratios, the likelihood-ratio test and classification tables against the base rate.
  • Retention and churn rates — Lapse rules, and why a period without a purchase is not the same as a lost customer.
  • Survival analysis — How long members stay, and why recent silence matters more than old activity.
  • Normalisation and standardisation — Data leakage: information a model would not have when it makes real predictions.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

On 1 November 2027 Ana wants a list. The cafés can send an app message with a small offer to members who seem to be drifting away, and she would like to send it to the members who would otherwise not come back before the new year. She has every member’s visits up to 31 October. She doesn’t yet know who will visit in November and December, but by January she will, and so she can test any rule she makes against what happened.

Before and after: calibration and holdout

A churn model learns from the past how members who later lapsed looked beforehand. That needs two periods. The calibration period supplies the predictors: here, each member’s recency, frequency, spend and tenure as of 31 October, with their café, whether they live or work nearby and whether notifications are on. The holdout period supplies the outcome: whether the member visited at all in November–December. A member with no visit in those two months has lapsed: the retention lesson’s 60-day rule (more than 60 days without a visit), applied on 31 December.

The table covers the 1,664 members who joined by 31 August, so that everyone has at least two months of history. Of them, 566 came back in November–December and 1,098 did not: a base rate of lapsing of 66.0%. Any score has to beat what that number alone says: if Ana contacted members at random, two in three would be lapsers.

Caution: Leakage

Every predictor must be something Ana could have known on 31 October. Recency measured on 31 December instead looks harmless, but it already contains the answer: a member who visited in November or December has a recency of 60 days or less on 31 December, and one who didn’t has more. It separates lapsers from returners perfectly (AUC 1.000), and it is useless on 1 November. That is data leakage, the same error B02 warned about in data preparation.

Recency does most of the work

The simplest rule ranks members by recency, how long ago they last visited. The holdout shows how strong it is: the longer a member has been silent, the likelier they are to stay away.

Each extra day of silence multiplies the odds of lapsing by 1.039, holding the others fixed; 30 days multiply them by e raised to 30 times the coefficient, 3.19. None of the other six is clearly different from no association once recency is in the model, and together they add little (p = 0.214). That doesn’t mean they don’t matter: without recency, the other six still rank members with an AUC of 0.750, because frequent visitors tend to have visited recently. Recency carries most of what they know.

One refinement is worth having. Allowing recency’s effect to depend on frequency (a product term, recency × frequency) improves the fit sharply (G = 104.4 on 1 df, p below 0.001). For a member silent for 60 days, the model gives a 65.5% chance of lapsing if they had made 2 visits and 89.0% if they had made 10; for one seen 10 days ago, 31.4% and 22.5%. A regular who goes quiet has probably stopped; a regular seen recently is probably still coming. Raw data say the same, in the bands the rfm-segments lesson used: among members silent for 61–90 days, 22.3% of those with one or two visits came back in November–December, against 1.6% of those with seven or more. This is the increasing frequency paradox (the rfm-segments lesson found the same in return rates).

In depth

Judging a score: base rate, lift and gains

A churn model is used to rank customers, so judge it by how well it ranks. At a threshold of 0.5 the seven-predictor model classifies 85.1% of members correctly, against 66.0% for predicting that everyone lapses. But Ana won’t contact everyone above 0.5; she will contact as many as she can afford, from the top of the list. The lift table answers the question that matters: if she contacts the top fifth, the top two-fifths and so on, what share of each group lapses, and how does that compare with the base rate?

The top fifth lapses at 96.7%, a lift of 1.47 over the base rate. That looks modest, and it is close to the most possible: when two-thirds of members lapse anyway, no group can be more than 1 ÷ 0.660 = 1.52 times as rich in lapsers. Lift is easy to read but depends on the base rate, so compare lifts only between scores on the same customers. The last column is the gains curve: contacting the top 40% finds 57.7% of all lapsers, and the top 60% finds 82.6%. A random list of the same size would find 40% and 60%. Recency has ties, and some fall on group boundaries: 9 members silent for 178 days straddle the edge of the top tenth, so the group rates depend slightly on the order of the rows; the AUC avoids this by counting ties as half.

The area under the ROC curve (AUC) summarises the ranking in one number: the probability that a randomly chosen lapser scores higher than a randomly chosen returner, counting ties as half. A score that ranks at random has 0.5; a perfect one 1. Recency alone has an AUC of 0.901, the seven-predictor model 0.899, and the model with the recency × frequency term 0.910. The extra predictors buy almost nothing; the product term buys a little. The seven-predictor model even ranks a shade worse than recency alone: maximum likelihood chooses coefficients to fit the probabilities, not to maximise the ranking, and predictors that add nothing can cost a little.

Testing on customers the model hasn’t seen

Scores judged on the members used to fit them flatter the model, because the coefficients were chosen to fit those members. A fair estimate fits on some members and scores others. Fitting the seven-predictor model on alternate members (832) and scoring the other 832 gives an AUC of 0.906 on the scored half, against 0.893 on the fitting half: no sign of overfitting, as expected with seven predictors and hundreds of each outcome. On the scored half the top tenth lapsed at 98.8%, a lift of 1.48. A model with dozens of predictors, or one tuned repeatedly on the same data, would need this check far more. Even better is a later holdout: fit on, say, members described in August and their September–October outcome, then score 1 November’s members.

Calibration asks whether the predicted probabilities mean what they say: of members given about 40%, do about 40% lapse? Sorting by the model’s probability into five groups, the mean predicted and actual rates are 99.3% against 97.0%, 94.4% against 93.4%, 76.0% against 82.9%, 39.3% against 42.0% and 20.8% against 14.5%. It is within about 3 points in the top, second and fourth fifths; it understates lapsing in the middle fifth (76.0% against 82.9%) and overstates it in the bottom fifth by six points (20.8% against 14.5%). Most of the bottom fifth are recent visitors. A straight line in recency on the log-odds scale can’t bend as the data do. For ranking that matters little; for budgeting how many members will come back, check calibration and fix it, for example with recency bands as predictors.

Whom to contact, and at what cost

The obvious rule, contact those most likely to lapse, quietly assumes that the offer works equally well on everyone. It doesn’t. What a contact is worth depends on the chance the customer lapses ppp, the chance the offer changes that (the uplift, uuu), the value of a customer kept VVV and the cost of contact ccc: the expected gain is p×u×V−cp \times u \times V - cp×u×V−c. A member who has been silent for six months has ppp near 1, but if they have moved away, uuu is near 0. A member seen last week has a small ppp: most would come anyway, and the offer mostly rewards visits that were going to happen. The members worth contacting are those whose behaviour the offer can change, often in the middle of the risk ranking.

Research supports the caution. As the American Marketing Association summarises it, Ascarza (2018), in the Journal of Marketing Research, used field data from a wireless provider and a membership organisation to show that targeting retention efforts by how customers respond to them (lift, in her terms) reduces churn more effectively than targeting by their risk of leaving. And in a field experiment at a wireless carrier described by Knowledge at Wharton (2015), customers randomly assigned to a campaign recommending a different pricing plan were more likely to leave two months later than those assigned to no campaign, about 10% against 6%; the researchers argue that being contacted itself changed some customers’ behaviour. Contact can backfire.

Key idea: Uplift needs a control group

A churn score predicts who will lapse if nothing is done. It can’t say who an offer will change, because in the data nobody received the offer. To measure uplift, send the offer to a random part of each group and hold out the rest (a holdout group, the control group of the experiment). The difference in lapse rates between the two, within each band of the score, is the uplift.

Common mistakes

  • Using information from after the prediction date

    Recency or visits measured after 31 October leak the outcome (AUC 1.000). Build every predictor from the calibration period only.

  • Judging the model by accuracy alone

    85.1% accuracy sounds high, but predicting “everyone lapses” scores 66.0%. Compare with the base rate, and for targeting look at lift and gains.

  • Comparing lifts across different customer bases

    Lift is capped at 1 ÷ base rate: 1.52 here, but 10 when 10% churn. A lift of 1.47 can be excellent and one of 3 poor; compare scores on the same customers, or use the AUC.

  • Contacting the customers most likely to churn

    Risk is not responsiveness. The longest-silent members are mostly gone, and recent ones would mostly come anyway; the offer pays where it changes behaviour, which only a randomised test can show.

  • Reading a churn model’s coefficients as levers

    Notifications have an odds ratio of 0.963 here, and members chose them; neither the coefficient nor its absence of significance says what switching notifications on would do.

In practice

Churn models are standard in telecoms, banking, insurance, software and retail. The workflow is the one followed here: fix the prediction date and the outcome window; build features from before the date; fit a model, often a logistic regression or a tree-based method; and judge it on later customers with lift, gains and the AUC. The score then feeds a decision: who receives a retention call, an offer, a service visit or nothing.

The modelling is the easier half. The harder half is knowing what the contact does. A retention team that sends offers to its highest-risk customers and sees many of them leave anyway may conclude the offer failed when it simply reached people it couldn’t keep; one that sends offers to its safest customers will see them stay, and may credit the offer for loyalty it didn’t cause. Without a randomised holdout, neither team knows. Uplift modelling, which predicts the difference an action makes rather than the outcome, is built on such tests.

Fictional case

Ana’s plan for the next campaign: on 1 January, score every member by recency and frequency as of 31 December. Within each of four recency bands, send the offer to a random half and hold out the other half. In March, compare the share who visited in January–February in each half of each band (a difference of two proportions with its confidence interval, from B04), and use the measured uplifts and the cost of the offer to choose next quarter’s list. The churn score decides where to look; the test decides where to spend.

Two practical cautions. A model fitted to one period can drift: members who joined during a festival, or a change to the app, can change who lapses. Refit and re-check calibration regularly against fresh holdout periods. And a list of customers “likely to leave” is sensitive information about them; use it to serve them better, not to treat them worse, and follow the data-protection rules that apply to profiling.

Terms introduced

Calibration period
In churn prediction, the period whose data supply the predictors, ending on the date the prediction is made; the outcome is taken from a later holdout period.
Gains curve
A chart of the cumulative share of all positive outcomes (such as churners) found against the share of customers contacted, taking customers in order of their score; a random order gives the diagonal.
Area under the ROC curve
A summary of how well a score ranks two classes: the probability that a randomly chosen positive case scores higher than a randomly chosen negative one, counting ties as half. Abbreviated AUC; 0.5 is random and 1 perfect.
Uplift
The change in a customer’s probability of an outcome caused by an action, such as a retention offer; estimated by comparing randomly chosen treated and untreated customers.
