Customer lifetime value

Put a number on what a customer is worth: historical and predictive value, margin not revenue, the constant-retention formula with its two timing conventions, a finite horizon, why one average retention rate understates value when customers differ, and a simple projection for members who never tell you they have left.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Distinguish historical from predictive customer value, and build either from margin rather than revenue.
  • Calculate lifetime value under constant retention with the first margin now or one period away, over a finite or an infinite horizon, stating the margin, period, retention, discount rate and timing.
  • Explain why a single average retention rate understates value when customers differ, and calculate value with the shifted-beta-geometric model.
  • Estimate what a new noncontractual member is worth from a cohort’s history, and check the projection against months it was not fitted to.
  • Compare value across groups of customers and state what a lifetime value can and cannot support.

  • What finance is: money, time and risk — Discounting: why a dollar next year is worth less than a dollar now.
  • Cohort analysis — Revenue per joiner by months since joining, read along a cohort’s row.
  • Survival analysis — The shifted-beta-geometric model of contractual retention and its rising retention rate.

The idea

Fictional case

January 2028. Lantern’s two cafés signed up 2,584 loyalty-app members in 2027, and Ana is planning 2028’s marketing. Before she decides what she can afford to spend winning a member, or keeping one, she needs to know what a member is worth. The members never tell Lantern when they stop coming, so she cannot simply read off how long they stay.

Value from margins, over time

A customer’s customer lifetime value (CLV) is the present value of the margins the customer is expected to bring in over the whole relationship. Each part of that sentence matters. Margins, because what a customer is worth to the business is what is left after the costs of serving them, not what they pay. Expected, because nobody knows how long a particular customer will stay. Present value, because a dollar of margin next year is worth less than a dollar now.

For the cafés the margin on a dollar of sales is about 67 cents. F03’s figures for 2027 put the cost of goods (beans, milk, cups, bought-in pastries) at 31% of café sales, and card fees at $4,720 on $236,000 of sales, 2%. So the margin is 100% − 31% − 2% = 67% of what a member spends. Wages and rent are left out because they don’t change with one more member’s visits; a lesson that charged them to members would be valuing the cafés, not a customer.

Historical and predictive value

Two different numbers are both called customer value. Historical value adds up what a customer has already brought in: Lantern’s 242 March 2027 joiners spent $40.30 each, on average, by 31 December, worth $27.00 of margin. Predictive value looks forward: what a customer will bring from now on, or what a new customer will bring over their whole relationship. Historical value is a fact about the past; predictive value is a forecast, and every assumption behind it should be stated.

Most decisions need the predictive kind. What a member spent in 2027 is sunk; what matters for a 2028 budget is what next year’s new members will be worth, and what this year’s members still have to give. The rest of this lesson builds that forecast: first for customers whose departures are recorded (a contractual setting, where the retention rate is observed), then for the cafés’ members, whose departures are not.

The constant-retention formula

In a contractual business a customer stays from one period to the next with probability rrr, the retention rate. If the margin per period is mmm and the discount rate per period is ddd, the expected margin ttt periods from now is m rtm\,r^tmrt and it is worth m rt/(1+d)tm\,r^t/(1+d)^tmrt/(1+d)t today. Adding every period gives a geometric series, and the sum depends on when the first margin arrives.

Quillmore Alarm Monitoring (fictional) sells yearly monitoring contracts with a margin of $120 a year; 75% of customers renew each year, and it discounts at 8% a year. Timing A, the first margin paid now and each renewal at the start of its year, gives m(1+d)/(1+d−r)=120×1.08/0.33=392.73m(1+d)/(1+d-r) = 120 \times 1.08/0.33 = 392.73m(1+d)/(1+d−r)=120×1.08/0.33=392.73 dollars: the value of a customer just signed. Timing C counts only what is still to come after this year’s margin has been received, m r/(1+d−r)=120×0.75/0.33=272.73m\,r/(1+d-r) = 120 \times 0.75/0.33 = 272.73mr/(1+d−r)=120×0.75/0.33=272.73 dollars: the residual lifetime value of a customer already on the books. Timing B, M01’s convention (every margin at the end of its year, the first one certain), gives m/(1+d−r)=363.64m/(1+d-r) = 363.64m/(1+d−r)=363.64 dollars.

Key idea: Say which value you mean

The three numbers answer different questions. A (or B) values a customer you are about to win, so it is the ceiling on what winning one is worth; C values a customer you already have, so it is what you stand to lose if they leave. None of them is “the” CLV, and a figure quoted without its timing can’t be compared with another.

When customers differ, one retention rate misleads

The formula assumes every customer has the same chance of leaving, every year. Real customers differ: some were never going to stay long, some will stay for decades. The flighty leave first, so the survivors are increasingly the loyal ones and the retention rate of a cohort rises with tenure even though nobody’s own loyalty changes. Fader and Hardie’s (2007) published data show it: of their “Regular” customers 63.1% renewed after year 1, 74.2% of those still there renewed after year 2, and 92.0% of those still there after year 7.

Fit a single retention rate to those seven years and you get 77.4%; fit the shifted-beta-geometric (sBG) model, in which each customer has their own fixed churn probability drawn from a beta distribution, and you get α = 0.704 and β = 1.182, Fader and Hardie’s estimates. With a margin of $100 a year (a fictional figure; the data give only survival) and a 10% discount rate, the constant rate values a new customer at $337.39, the sBG at $369.49. For a customer who has already renewed five times the gap is far wider: $261.13 against $626.44. A constant rate has no memory, so it gives a ten-year customer the same future as one who signed last month.

Members who never say goodbye

The cafés’ members are a noncontractual setting: a member who stops coming simply stops, so there is no retention rate to put in the formula. It is tempting to use the share of members who came back at least once (81.6% of all 2,584 by 31 December), but that is a repeat-purchase rate, not a retention rate, and Fader and Hardie (2012) warn that the constant-retention formulas don’t apply to such settings at all. What the data do show, directly, is how much a cohort of joiners spends in each month after joining.

The 242 March joiners spent $8.68 each in March, $7.04 in April and $1.60 in December, the tenth month. Spend per joiner falls month by month as members drift away, and those still coming are increasingly the less frequent visitors, but it hasn’t reached zero: 17.4% of them still visited in December. A simple forecast continues the fall at its recent pace. From May (month 4) to December spend per joiner fell by a factor of 0.850 a month on average; continuing that, and discounting at 0.8% a month, the months from January 2028 on are worth another $5.36 of margin per March joiner, on top of the $26.37 (discounted to the month each joined) already earned. A new member, on this evidence, is worth about $32 of margin.

Sensitivity: retention matters most

Lifetime value is most sensitive to retention, because rrr appears in a denominator that can be small. At Quillmore, five points of retention either way move the value from $341.05 (70%) to $462.86 (80%) around the $392.73 at 75%: a change of about 18% upwards and 13% downwards. Report CLV with the range that plausible retention rates give, not as a single figure.

Noncontractual value, kept simple

Where departures are never observed, value has to come from purchases. The approach in this lesson uses the cohort table directly: spend per joiner in each month since joining, times the margin, discounted, summed over the months observed, plus a projection of the months not yet seen. It needs a cohort old enough to show the shape of the decline, and it projects a pace that is itself an assumption.

Test the projection the way any forecast is tested: fit it on early months and check it on later ones. Using only what was known on 31 October (months 2 to 7 for the March joiners), spend per joiner fell by a factor of 0.833 a month; continuing that predicts $1.84 for November and $1.54 for December, against $1.82 and $1.60 actually spent. Two months is a short test, and the monthly ratios bounce around (from 0.74 to 0.91) with the seasons and the cafés’ busy days. The pace matters: a factor of 0.80 instead of 0.85 makes the projected months worth $3.83, and 0.90 makes them $8.29.

Note: Models for noncontractual customers

The geometric projection treats the whole cohort as one decaying stream. Probability models built for this setting describe each customer as buying at their own rate while “alive” and dropping out unseen at some point, and estimate both from each customer’s recency and frequency. They are beyond this course; the cohort projection is a transparent first answer that any spreadsheet can reproduce.

Value by segment

Customer value differs between groups, and comparing groups is often more useful than any single figure. Of the 1,664 members who joined from March to August, over their join month and the next four Harbourside’s 603 brought in $25.11 of margin each and Millbrook’s 1,061 $19.98, although Millbrook’s members visited more often (8.31 visits against 6.49): Harbourside’s tickets are larger ($5.77 a visit against $3.59). Members with notifications on brought $23.15 and those with them off $20.32; members who live or work within a mile $23.43 and the rest $20.83.

Caution: A segment’s value is an association

Members chose notifications, and where they live; nobody assigned them. That members with notifications on are worth more says nothing about what switching a member’s notifications on would add: keener members may simply be the ones who turn them on. Only a randomised test (the last lesson in this module) measures what an action changes.

What CLV is for, and what it isn’t

  • A ceiling on acquisition spending. Winning a customer is worth at most their full lifetime value (timing A or B); the next lesson compares it with what winning one costs.
  • Priorities for retention. Residual value says what is at stake if a group of existing customers leaves, and so how much keeping them could be worth.
  • Valuing a customer base. The sum of residual values is what the existing customers are expected to bring in.
  • Not a person’s worth. CLV is an average forecast for a kind of customer. It doesn’t say any one member will spend that much, and it is not a reason to treat people with low values badly.
  • Not a fact. It rests on a margin, a retention or decay assumption, a discount rate, a timing and a horizon, each of which can be wrong. State them, and show how the answer moves when they change.

Common mistakes

  • Building value from revenue

    A member who spends $40 is not worth $40: at the cafés about 33 cents of every dollar goes on goods and card fees. Revenue-based values overstate worth by the ratio of revenue to margin and justify spending too much to win customers. Use margin, and say which costs it takes off.

  • Quoting a CLV without its timing

    With the same margin, retention and discount rate, Quillmore’s customer is worth $392.73, $363.64 or $272.73 depending on when the margins are counted. Draw the timeline and name the convention; never compare two figures built on different ones.

  • Using one average retention rate for everyone

    Customers differ, so retention rises with tenure as the flighty leave. A constant rate understates value, slightly for new customers and heavily for established ones ($261 against $626 for Regular customers after five renewals). Fit a model that allows customers to differ, such as the sBG, and check it on later periods.

  • Treating a repeat-purchase rate as a retention rate

    In a noncontractual business nobody is observed leaving. The 81.6% of members who came back at least once is not a retention rate, and putting it into r ÷ (1 + d − r) gives a number with no meaning. Use the cohort’s spend over time, or a model built for the setting.

  • Reading a segment’s value as the effect of a feature

    Members with notifications on were worth more, but they chose the setting. The difference is an association; switching notifications on for others may add much less, or nothing. Test it.

  • Treating CLV as a fact about a person

    It is a forecast for a kind of customer, with a range. Present it with its assumptions and the values plausible alternatives give, and update it as cohorts age.

In practice

Fictional case

Ana’s note to the café managers, January 2028: “What is a new app member worth? Our March 2027 joiners are the only group with ten months of history. Each brought in $26.37 of margin (after goods and card fees, valued at the date they joined) in those ten months, and if their spending keeps falling at its recent pace another $5.36 after that: about $32 in all, roughly $30 to $35 depending on how fast spending falls. About 60% of it comes in the first five months. Harbourside members are worth more than Millbrook’s ($25.11 against $19.98 in their first five months) because their tickets are bigger, though Millbrook members visit more often. Members with notifications on are worth more too, but they chose the setting, so that isn’t evidence that notifications add value. I’ll redo these figures each quarter as the later cohorts build up history.”

In practice lifetime value is a planning tool, not a valuation of people. Retailers, banks, telecoms companies and software firms use it to decide how much they can spend to win a customer through each channel, how much it is worth spending to keep different groups, and how to value the customer base when a business is bought or sold. The calculations that work are the transparent ones: the margin, the retention or decay assumption, the discount rate, the timing and the horizon written down, and the figure shown under alternatives.

The two failures are mirror images. Over-optimism comes from revenue instead of margin, retention rates from the best customers applied to all, or long horizons in a business whose products change quickly. Under-valuation comes from one average retention rate applied to customers who differ, which hides that the customers who have stayed are the ones most likely to keep staying. The first leads to overspending on acquisition; the second to under-investing in the loyal customers who carry the business.

For noncontractual businesses such as cafés, shops and restaurants, the honest starting point is a cohort’s history read along its row. It shows how much value comes early, which is where acquisition and onboarding decisions bite, and it gives a projection that can be checked month by month against what members actually do. The next lesson sets this value against the cost of winning a member.

Terms introduced

Residual lifetime value
The expected present value of the margins an existing customer will bring in from now on, excluding margins already received.
Historical customer value
The margin (or spend) a customer has already brought in over a stated window; a fact about the past, as opposed to the forecast that predictive lifetime value makes.
Discounted expected lifetime
The expected number of periods a customer will pay for, each discounted to today; multiplied by the margin per period it gives lifetime value. Abbreviated DEL.
