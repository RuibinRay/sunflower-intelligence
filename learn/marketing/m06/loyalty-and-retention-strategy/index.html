Loyalty and retention strategy

Loyalty programmes, win-back offers and other ways of keeping customers: what the published evidence shows, why self-selected members can’t tell you whether a programme works, how a randomised holdout measures what an offer adds, and how to weigh its cost against the value of the customers it keeps.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Describe the main kinds of loyalty programme and state precisely what published studies have found about their effects.
  • Explain double jeopardy and what it implies for how much loyalty a programme can create.
  • Explain why comparing members who chose a feature with those who didn’t can’t show what the feature does, using the cafés’ notifications.
  • Analyse a randomised holdout test with the difference in proportions, its confidence interval and the power to detect a given difference.
  • Evaluate a retention offer by setting its cost, including what it gives to customers who would have stayed anyway, against the value of the customers it keeps.

Review first

  • Customer lifetime value — The value of a customer kept, which a retention offer must beat.
  • Predicting churn — Scoring who is likely to lapse, and why a good churn score is not the same as knowing whom an offer changes.
  • Proportions and chi-square tests — Comparing two proportions with a z test and a confidence interval.
  • Hypothesis testing — Significance, Type I and II errors and power.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

January 2028. 1324 of the cafés’ 2,584 app members had not visited for more than 60 days by 31 December, 861 of them for 61 to 180 days. Ana has two ideas: a message offering lapsed members a free pastry with their next drink, and a stamp card in the app (a free drink after so many visits). A supplier is also pitching a points scheme, with a slide claiming that “keeping just 5% more of your customers can nearly double your profits”. Before spending money she wants to know what such schemes actually do, and how she would tell whether hers worked.

Loyalty programmes and what they are for

A loyalty programme rewards customers for buying repeatedly. The common forms are stamp cards (a free item after a set number of purchases), points (earned per dollar and exchanged for rewards), tiers (better treatment as spending passes thresholds, as airlines and hotels use) and apps that combine these with offers and messages. They promise to keep customers who would otherwise leave and to raise what each spends. They also cost money: every reward given to a customer who would have bought anyway is a discount with nothing in return.

What the evidence shows

Dowling and Uncles (1997) reviewed customer loyalty programmes and argued, in the article’s published summary, that such programmes mainly raise marketing spending without creating much extra brand loyalty. The summary cites research finding that only about 10% of buyers of many frequently bought consumer goods are completely loyal to one brand over a year, and that more than 80% of the European business travellers surveyed belonged to more than one frequent-flyer programme. They also called the idea that loyal customers are always more profitable a gross simplification, and argued that programmes do better when they support the product’s value to the customer and when rewards are valued and not long delayed.

Sharp and Sharp (1997) tested a large multi-brand loyalty programme against what a standard model of repeat buying (the Dirichlet) predicts for brands of each size. They found only weak “excess loyalty” overall, inconsistent across brands: two of the six participating brands deviated substantially, and those deviations also appeared among non-members, so they were probably due at least partly to the brands’ other loyalty initiatives.

The pattern behind both studies is double jeopardy, described by Ehrenberg, Goodhardt and Barwise (1990): a small brand typically has far fewer buyers than a large one in a given period, and its buyers also tend to buy it less often. Loyalty measures largely follow from a brand’s size, so a programme that hopes to make a small brand’s customers unusually loyal is pushing against one of the most regular findings in marketing.

Programme design can still change behaviour within the programme. Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006) studied a reward card at a café on a large university campus: ten coffee purchases earned a free coffee. As members got closer to the reward the time between purchases shrank, by about 20% from the first interval to the last. In a field experiment with 108 of the café’s customers, those given a 12-stamp card with two stamps already filled in completed the ten purchases they needed in 12.7 days on average, against 15.6 days for those with a plain 10-stamp card. And members who sped up more towards their first reward were more likely to earn a second and started on it sooner. This is the goal-gradient effect: effort rises as a goal comes near.

Note: What these studies don’t say

The café study shows how members of a programme behave as a reward approaches, and that a card that looks further along is completed faster. It doesn’t estimate how many more coffees the café sold over a year than it would have with no card. The programme reviews describe weak effects on loyalty for typical programmes; they don’t say no programme can pay. Neither tells Ana what her stamp card would add. Only a test at the cafés can.

The 5% claim

Reichheld and Sasser’s 1990 Harvard Business Review article on “zero defections” is the source of a much repeated claim, summarised by Bain (where Reichheld worked) as companies being able to boost profits by almost 100% by retaining just 5% more of their customers. The article is about service companies, and how far the claim holds depends on the business. Lifetime value shows why. With a 10% discount rate and margins at the start of each period, five more points of retention raise a new customer’s value by 11% if retention is 60%, by 20% at 80% and by 33% at 90%. The gain is largest where retention is already high, and none of these figures subtracts what it costs to buy the extra retention.

Lantern’s app: a programme nobody assigned

The cafés’ app already works like a light programme: members can turn on notifications. With a lapse defined as more than 60 days without a visit, the 1418 members with notifications on stayed active for a median of 57 days before lapsing, against 48 days for the 1166 with them off, and the log-rank test puts the difference at p = 0.003. By 31 December members with notifications on had made 8.03 visits each, against 6.93.

It is tempting to read that as notifications working. But members chose the setting. Those who turned notifications on may be the keener members, and keenness alone would produce the same gap. One obvious difference doesn’t explain it (40.3% of members with notifications on live or work within a mile of a café, against 40.4% with them off), but keenness can’t be measured from the till. A self-selected feature can never show its own effect: the comparison is between different kinds of people.

Measuring what an offer adds

The answer is to decide by chance who gets the offer. In Ana’s fictional January 2028 test, the 861 members who had not visited for 61 to 180 days are split at random: 431 receive the pastry offer and 430 form a holdout group that hears nothing. Within 30 days 41 of the 431 offered (9.5%) visit, and so do 22 of the 430 in the holdout (5.1%). Because chance decided who was offered, the groups differ only by chance and by the offer, so the 4.4-point difference estimates what the offer added: its incrementality.

The holdout also shows something an offer’s redemption count hides. Of the 41 offered members who came back, about 22 would have come back anyway, at the holdout’s rate; only about 19 were won back by the offer. A report that said “41 lapsed members won back” would overstate the offer’s effect more than twofold.

In depth

Win-back and save tactics

Retention actions fall into two families. Win-back tactics aim at customers who have already lapsed: a reminder, an offer, a new product, a personal call. Save tactics act before the customer leaves: putting a problem right after a complaint, contacting customers whose use is falling. Both are offers made to a chosen group, and both raise the same three questions: whom to contact, what the contact adds compared with doing nothing, and whether that is worth its cost. The first is the subject of the previous module’s churn scoring; this lesson deals with the second and third.

Key idea: Use the interval for the decision

A test whose interval sits entirely above the break-even uplift says the offer pays. One whose interval straddles break-even, as Ana’s does, says it probably pays but might not. With a cheap offer and small sums at stake, “probably” may be enough; with an expensive one, run a larger test first.

How big a test? Power in outline

Power is the chance a test detects a difference that is really there (B04). For two proportions with equal groups, the number per group needed for power 1 − β at a two-sided significance level α is approximately the sample-size formula in the Formulas section: it grows with the square of the z values and shrinks with the square of the difference to detect, (pT−pC)2(p_T - p_C)^2(pT​−pC​)2. Ana’s 431 a group gave her only about a 70% chance of a significant result even if the true uplift were the 4 points she observed. Small effects need big tests, and a business with a few hundred lapsed customers will often have to decide with wide intervals, or pool several months.

Designing a test that can be trusted

  • Randomise individuals (or whole units) by chance, not by staff judgement or by who opens the app, and keep the holdout untouched.
  • Fix the outcome and the window in advance (a visit within 30 days), so the result can’t be chosen after seeing the data.
  • Count the cost on everyone who redeems, and the value only on the difference between the groups.
  • Watch the longer run. An offer can pull forward visits that would have happened later, or teach customers to wait for offers; a later window (60 or 90 days) shows whether the gap lasts.
  • Target by response, not only by risk. The members most likely to lapse are not necessarily those the offer changes; a test that records who was offered lets later analysis find where the uplift is largest.

Testing a programme, not only an offer

A stamp card is harder to test than a message, because customers talk and see each other’s cards. Options include giving the card in the app to a random half of new members (the other half see the app as now), or switching it on at one café for a few months and comparing with the other, with the weakness that the cafés differ anyway. Either is better than launching it everywhere and comparing members who use the card with those who don’t, which repeats the notifications problem: the members who collect stamps are the ones who were coming anyway.

Common mistakes

  • Counting every redemption as a customer saved

    Most customers who use a win-back offer would have come back anyway. In the pastry test 41 returned, but only about 19 more than the holdout’s rate implies. Report the difference from a holdout, not the redemptions.

  • Comparing members with non-members of a programme

    People who join a programme, turn on notifications or collect stamps are already the keener customers. Their higher spending is no evidence the programme works. Randomise who gets it.

  • Repeating the 5% claim as a law

    “5% more retention nearly doubles profit” comes from an article about service companies in 1990, and the effect depends on margins and retention: five points add 11% to a customer’s value at 60% retention and 33% at 90%, before the cost of achieving them.

  • Reading a significant test as a profitable one

    A p-value below 0.05 says the effect is probably not zero. Profit needs the effect to exceed the break-even uplift. Compare the confidence interval with the break-even point.

  • Running a test too small to find the effect

    Detecting a rise from 5% to 7.5% with 80% power needs about 1468 customers a group; with a few hundred, a real effect will often look “not significant”. Work out the size before the test, or accept a wide interval and say so.

  • Expecting a programme to make customers unusually loyal

    Double jeopardy and the programme studies show loyalty mostly follows a brand’s size. A programme can reward and speed up buying among members, but large gains in loyalty beyond what penetration brings are rare. Judge it on what it adds, measured.

In practice

Fictional case

Ana’s note on retention, February 2028 (the test is fictional): “The pastry offer to members who hadn’t visited for two to six months brought 4.4 more in every hundred back within a month than a random holdout who got nothing (95% CI 0.9 to 7.9 points). That is about 19 members, worth about $142 against $37 of pastries, so it probably pays, though the test was too small to be sure. We’ll keep sending it each month with a fifth of eligible members held out, so we keep measuring. On the stamp card: the research shows cards speed up visits as the reward approaches, but programmes rarely create much extra loyalty, so I want to test it before launching. From April, half of new members (chosen at random) will see a card in the app. I’m not using the notifications data as evidence: members chose them.”

In practice retention strategy is where marketing spends most freely on unmeasured promises. Programmes are launched because competitors have them; win-back campaigns are judged by redemptions; the customers who use a programme are compared with those who don’t. Each of these reports something real (customers did redeem, members did spend more) but none measures what the spending added.

The businesses that learn are the ones that build a holdout into every retention action as a matter of routine: a random slice of each mailing that receives nothing, a test region, a staggered launch. The holdout costs a little in foregone response, if the action works, and buys the one number that matters: what the action changed. Over time the tests also show for whom it works best, which is where targeting money should go.

The value side comes from the earlier lessons. A retention offer is worth paying for when the customers it keeps are worth more than it costs, counting the cost given away to those who would have stayed anyway. Lifetime value supplies the first number, the holdout the second, and the comparison of the interval with the break-even uplift the decision.

Summary

  • Loyalty programmes (stamp cards, points, tiers, apps) reward repeat buying; every reward given to a customer who would have bought anyway is a cost with no return.
  • Dowling and Uncles (1997) and Sharp and Sharp (1997) found typical programmes add little loyalty; double jeopardy (Ehrenberg, Goodhardt and Barwise, 1990) shows loyalty largely follows a brand’s size.
  • Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006): at a university café, purchases sped up by about 20% as a ten-coffee reward approached, and a 12-stamp card with two stamps filled was completed in 12.7 days against 15.6 for a plain 10-stamp card.
  • The 5% claim (Reichheld and Sasser, 1990) depends on the business: five points of retention add more value where retention is already high, before the cost of buying them.
  • A self-selected feature cannot show its effect: members with notifications on lapsed later (median 57 against 48 days), but they chose the setting.
  • A randomised holdout measures incrementality: the difference in response with a confidence interval; small effects need large groups (about 1,468 a group to detect 5% against 7.5% at 80% power).
  • An offer pays when the uplift exceeds the break-even (p̂_T k + c) ÷ V; compare the interval, not only the estimate, with it.

Terms introduced

Loyalty programme
A scheme that rewards customers for buying repeatedly, such as a stamp card, points, tiers or an app with offers.
Double jeopardy
The regular finding that a brand with a small market share has far fewer buyers than a large brand and that its buyers also tend to buy it less often.
Goal-gradient effect
The tendency for effort to increase as a goal gets closer; in reward programmes, customers buying more often as a reward approaches.
Break-even uplift
The smallest increase in response caused by an offer at which the value of the customers it changes covers the offer’s cost.
