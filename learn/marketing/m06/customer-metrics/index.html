Customer metrics

Turn a log of purchases into a customer table, one row per customer, as of a stated date: recency, frequency, monetary value and tenure for each customer, and the base’s average order value, repeat rate and active customers. Describe how unequal customers are, tell contractual from noncontractual settings, and clean the data before you trust any of it.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Build a customer table from transaction records as of a stated date and window, with each customer’s recency, frequency, monetary value and tenure.
  • Calculate average order value, purchases per customer, repeat rate and active customers for a customer base, and explain why average order value is total spend ÷ purchases.
  • Describe skewed customer distributions with medians, percentiles and the top 20%’s share of spend, treating the 80/20 pattern as something to check.
  • Distinguish contractual from noncontractual settings and explain why churn is observed in one and has to be inferred in the other.
  • Identify data problems that distort customer metrics (one customer with two IDs, missing IDs, returns) and correct for them.
  • Calculate customer metrics in Excel with COUNTIFS, SUMIFS, MAXIFS and MINIFS, and with a PivotTable.

Review first

  • Psychographic and behavioural segmentation — Behavioural segmentation (usage rate, loyalty status) is where this course starts: M06 measures behaviour from records instead of asking about it.
  • Customer value, satisfaction and lifetime value — Retention, churn and customer lifetime value are introduced there; this lesson builds the data they are calculated from.
  • Conditional sums, counts and averages — COUNTIFS, SUMIFS, MAXIFS and MINIFS build the customer table in Excel.
  • Duplicate records — Finding records that describe the same customer is the first step before counting customers.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

It is January 2028. Lantern’s loyalty app opened at both cafés on 1 March 2027, and by 31 December 2,584 people had joined: 877 at Harbourside and 1,707 at Millbrook. The app is set up at the till, so joining is itself a visit, and every later visit where the member shows the app is logged: 19,458 visits and $82,251.25 of spend in ten months. The café managers have questions Ana can’t answer from the till totals. How many members are regulars? Who has stopped coming? Are the members who spend most also the ones who come most often?

The app’s log is transactional data: one row per visit, with the member, the date, the café and the amount. A table like that answers questions about sales (how much was spent in October, at which café). Questions about customers need a different table, with one row per customer, built by summarising each customer’s rows. That customer table is the starting point of everything in this course: scores, segments, cohorts, retention, churn models and lifetime value.

Four numbers for every customer

Four summaries of a customer’s purchases do most of the work. Each is measured at an as-of date, the day the table describes, using the purchases in a stated window that ends on it (here, 1 March to 31 December 2027, as of 31 December).

Recency
Days from the customer’s last purchase to the as-of date: 0 if they bought that day. Low is good.
Purchase frequency
The number of purchases in the window, counting the first. For Lantern’s members, visits, including the join.
Monetary value
Total spend in the window. Some analysts use the average spend per purchase instead; say which you use.
Tenure
Days from the first purchase to the as-of date: how long the customer has been a customer.

Member A1694’s row in the customer table is therefore recency 15 days, frequency 4, monetary value $18.15 and tenure 118 days. Recency, frequency and monetary value are the three behaviours that the next lesson scores (RFM); tenure tells you how long the customer has had to build them up.

Numbers for the whole base

Summing over the customer table gives the base’s figures. Lantern’s 2,584 members made 19,458 visits, 7.53 purchases per customer. They spent $82,251.25, so the average order value is $82,251.25 ÷ 19,458 = $4.23. 2,109 members came back at least once after joining: a repeat rate of 81.6%. And 1,260 members (48.8%) had visited in the last 60 days, which is one way to count active customers.

Key idea: Average order value is total spend ÷ purchases

Averaging each member’s own average gives $4.42, not $4.23: it counts a member with one $7.50 visit as heavily as a member with a hundred visits. Average order value describes the typical purchase, so every purchase gets equal weight. The mean of members’ averages answers a different question (the typical member’s usual ticket), and is rarely what a report means.

Customers are very unequal

The typical member is nothing like the average member. 475 members (18.4%) have visited only once, the median member has made 4 visits, the mean is 7.53, and one Millbrook regular has made 111. Spend is skewed the same way: a median of $18.00 and a mean of $31.83. When a few large values pull the mean up, report the median beside it.

A quick way to describe the inequality is to ask what share of spend the top 20% of customers bring. Lantern’s top 517 members by spend (everyone with at least $47.05) account for 57.9% of members’ spend and 56.1% of their visits. The “80/20 rule” (the Pareto principle) says 20% of customers bring 80% of sales; it is a rule of thumb, not a law, and here it is closer to 58/20. Check it in your own data rather than assume it.

Did they leave, or have they just not come yet?

At Halverton Climbing Centre (fictional), members pay monthly and cancel by telling the centre. That is a contractual setting: the business sees the day each customer leaves, so churn is observed. Lantern’s cafés are a noncontractual setting: nobody tells Lantern they have stopped coming. A member silent for two months might have moved away, or might be on holiday. Churn can only be inferred from silence, and a count of “lost” members depends on how long a silence you decide counts as lapsed.

That is why recency matters so much in a noncontractual business: it is the strongest evidence a customer is still a customer. The rest of this course keeps the distinction in view. Retention and churn rates, survival curves and lifetime value are calculated differently in the two settings, and the lessons on retention and on survival show how a lapse definition turns silence into a churn date.

In depth

The as-of date and the window

A customer table is a snapshot. Recency and tenure change every day even if nobody buys, so a table without its as-of date can’t be read. State both ends of the window, too: frequency over ten months and frequency over the last 90 days are different numbers. Three rules keep the arithmetic consistent.

  1. Use the data’s last day, not today, as the as-of date for historical data. Lantern’s log runs to 31 December 2027; measuring recency from the day Ana opens the file (in January 2028) would add the same few days to everyone and make every member look less recent than the data show.
  2. Count days as date differences: recency = as-of date − last purchase date, so a purchase on the as-of date gives 0. Tenure = as-of date − first purchase date.
  3. Count purchases in the window, the first included. Probability models of repeat buying often use the number of repeat purchases, frequency − 1, instead; if you share figures with someone using those models, say which you mean.

Base metrics and what each one hides

Purchases per customer and average order value multiply to spend per customer: 7.53 × $4.23 = $31.83. That identity helps explain a difference between groups. Harbourside’s members spend $36.73 each against Millbrook’s $29.32, but they come less often (6.36 visits against 8.13): the gap is all in the ticket, $5.77 against $3.61. The groups here are by the café each member joined at; 6.0% of visits after the join were at the other café.

The repeat rate is the share of customers with at least two purchases. It is easy to read but blunt: a member who came back once in March and never again counts as a repeater. Active customers need a threshold: a member is active if their recency is at most L days. The count moves a lot with L:

A threshold should reflect how often customers usually buy. Of the 16,874 gaps between one visit and the same member’s next, half are 5 days or shorter and 90% are 25 days or shorter. A 60-day silence is therefore unusual for a member who is still coming. But those gaps are only the ones that ended in another visit: the silence of a member who has left never ends, so it never appears in the list.

Tenure shapes recency and frequency

A member who joined in March has had ten months to visit; one who joined in December has had at most a month. Comparing their frequencies says more about when they joined than about how loyal they are, and the December joiner’s recency is short almost by construction.

Two remedies are common: compare customers who joined at the same time (the cohort analysis lesson does this systematically), or divide frequency by tenure to get a rate. A rate has its own trap: a member who joined yesterday and visited twice has an enormous rate built on two days. Notice too that every November and December joiner counts as active: their join is a visit within 60 days.

Skewed distributions

Customer spend and frequency are almost always right-skewed: many small customers and a long tail of large ones. Describe them with percentiles as well as means. Lantern’s members’ spend has a 20th percentile of $6.93, a median of $18.00 and an 80th percentile of $47.02; the largest is $427.40. Frequency has heavy ties: 475 members share the value 1, and 382 share the value 2. Ties matter as soon as you cut customers into groups, as the next lesson shows.

The top-20% share is a concentration measure: 20% would be perfect equality, and the higher it is, the more the business depends on a few customers. It depends on the window (over a short window more customers look small), on the base (members only, or every customer?) and on the measure (spend or visits). Quote it with all three.

Contractual and noncontractual settings

Fader and Hardie (2009) organise customer-base analysis around this distinction. In a contractual setting (a phone contract, an insurance policy, a gym membership, software licensed by the month) the end of the relationship is recorded. Retention and churn can be counted directly, and the question is how long customers stay. In a noncontractual setting (a café, a supermarket, most online shops) the end is never recorded: the business has to infer from each customer’s purchases how likely it is that they are still a customer, and churn needs a definition, usually a lapse of L days without a purchase.

The same business can have both: a climbing centre selling day passes at the desk (noncontractual) and monthly memberships that members cancel (contractual). Analyse them separately, because the metrics mean different things. A contractual customer who hasn’t bought for 60 days but hasn’t cancelled (a member who doesn’t use the climbing wall) is still a customer and still paying; a noncontractual customer silent for 60 days may be gone.

Data problems that distort every metric

  • One customer, two IDs. A member who reinstalls the app, or a shopper who opens a second account, appears as two customers, each with half the purchases. The customer count rises, purchases per customer falls, and the repeat rate usually falls, though a split can turn one repeater into two; the older ID looks lapsed. Match IDs on a stable key such as an email address or phone number, as in B02’s lesson on duplicates, and decide which ID survives.
  • Missing IDs. Only visits where the member shows the app are in the log. B05’s café log has 58,091 transactions and $236,000.00 of sales from 1 March to 31 December; members’ logged visits are 19,458 of the transactions (33.5%) and $82,251.25 of the sales (34.9%). Everything in the customer table describes members, not café customers, and a member who forgets the app is recorded as visiting less often than they did.
  • Returns and refunds. A refund is a negative amount, not a purchase. Net it against monetary value; remove a fully returned order from frequency as well (decide and state the rule for partial refunds). Counting the refund as a purchase inflates frequency and makes recency look better than it is if the refund came after the last real purchase.
  • Records that aren’t customers. Staff cards, test accounts and the café’s own card used for wastage look like extreme customers. Check the largest values before reporting them; Lantern’s busiest member (111 visits, about four a week since joining in June) is plausible for a regular, but deserves a look.

In Excel

With the visit log in columns A to D (member, date, café, amount), a list of members in F and the as-of date in a cell, four conditional functions build the customer table: =COUNTIFS($A$2:$A$36,F2) for frequency, =SUMIFS($D$2:$D$36,$A$2:$A$36,F2) for monetary value, =MAXIFS($B$2:$B$36,$A$2:$A$36,F2) for the last visit and =MINIFS($B$2:$B$36,$A$2:$A$36,F2) for the first. Recency is then the as-of cell minus the last visit, and tenure the as-of cell minus the first. MAXIFS and MINIFS take the range to search first, then range–criterion pairs, like SUMIFS; they need Excel 2019 or later.

A PivotTable does the same in one step: put the member in Rows, and in Values the Count of date (frequency), the Sum of amount (monetary value) and the Max and Min of date (last and first visit, shown as dates once formatted). Base metrics come from the customer table: =SUM(H2:H9)/SUM(G2:G9) for average order value, =COUNTIF(G2:G9,">=2")/COUNT(G2:G9) for the repeat rate.

Common mistakes

  • “Average order value is the average of the customers’ average orders.”

    That weights each customer equally, so a one-visit member counts as much as a daily regular: $4.42 for Lantern against the true $4.23. Divide total spend by total purchases.

  • “Recency is the days since the last purchase.” Since when?

    Recency is always measured to an as-of date. Use the data’s last day for historical data, state it, and recompute when the date changes; otherwise every customer drifts older while the report looks the same.

  • “Members who joined in the spring are our most loyal: they visit most.”

    They have had longest to visit. March–May joiners average 9.34 to 9.95 visits and December joiners 1.97, mostly because of tenure. Compare customers who joined at the same time, or use a rate with care.

  • “20% of customers bring 80% of sales: that’s the Pareto rule.”

    It is a rule of thumb. Lantern’s top 20% bring 57.9% of members’ spend. The share also depends on the window, the base and whether you count spend or visits; measure it.

  • “A customer who hasn’t bought for three months has churned.”

    In a noncontractual setting nobody tells you they have left; churn is a definition you choose (a lapse of L days), and the count moves with L. In a contractual setting a customer who hasn’t used the service but hasn’t cancelled is still a customer.

  • “The export is the customer list.”

    Duplicate IDs split customers, returns pose as purchases and rows without IDs vanish. Clean before summarising, and report what share of transactions the customer table covers.

In practice

Fictional case

Ana now sends the café managers a one-page member report on the first Monday of each month. It opens with a definitions box: the base (members who have joined since 1 March 2027), the window (since 1 March), the as-of date (the last day of the previous month), and the rule for active (a visit with the app in the last 60 days).

She leads with medians and the active count. The January report says 1,260 of 2,584 members (48.8%) were active on 31 December, with a median of 4 visits and $18.00 of spend. The means are in a second row, with a note that a few heavy users lift them.

She splits by café only where the café explains something. Harbourside’s members spend more per head, $36.73 against $29.32, and the report shows that this is the ticket ($5.77 against $3.61), not more visits. The Millbrook manager had been told her members were “worth less”; the identity spend per member = visits per member × average order value showed her members come more often.

She keeps the denominators honest. The report states that members’ logged visits are about a third of the cafés’ transactions, so member figures can’t stand in for all customers, and that a member who forgets the app is undercounted. Duplicate members (a reinstalled app gives a new ID) are merged monthly on the phone number held by the app, and Ana reports how many were merged.

The managers asked for a list of “our best customers”. Ana could have given them the top 517 by spend, but that list is mostly members who joined before September (441 of the 517). Instead the next lesson scores every member on recency, frequency and monetary value relative to the others, so that a member who joined in October and comes every week can sit beside one who has been coming since March.

Summary

  • A customer table has one row per customer, built from transactions as of a stated date and window; without the as-of date, recency and tenure can’t be read.
  • Recency = as-of date − last purchase (0 = bought that day); frequency = purchases in the window, the first included; monetary value = total spend in the window (say if you use the average); tenure = as-of date − first purchase.
  • Average order value = total spend ÷ total purchases, not the mean of customers’ averages; spend per customer = purchases per customer × average order value.
  • The repeat rate and the active count are simple but depend on definitions: active needs a recency threshold, and the count moves with it.
  • Customer distributions are right-skewed with heavy ties in frequency: report medians and percentiles, and measure the top 20%’s share instead of assuming 80/20.
  • Tenure shapes recency and frequency: compare customers who joined at the same time.
  • Contractual settings record when customers leave; noncontractual ones don’t, so churn must be inferred from silence with a stated lapse definition.
  • Merge duplicate IDs, net returns, and say what share of transactions has no customer ID before you trust any metric.

Terms introduced

Customer table
A table with one row per customer summarising that customer’s transactions in a stated window as of a stated date: for example recency, frequency, monetary value and tenure.
As-of date
The date a customer table or score describes, from which recency and tenure are measured; usually the last day of the data’s window.
Recency
The number of days from a customer’s last purchase to the as-of date; 0 means they bought on that date.
Purchase frequency
The number of purchases a customer made in a stated window, counting the first.
Monetary value
A customer’s total spend in a stated window (some analysts use the average spend per purchase instead).
Tenure
The number of days from a customer’s first purchase to the as-of date.
Repeat rate
The share of customers in a base who made at least two purchases in the window.
Active customers
Customers whose most recent purchase falls within a stated number of days of the as-of date.
Contractual setting
A business relationship in which the end is recorded, for example when a subscriber cancels, so churn is observed.
Noncontractual setting
A business relationship in which customers can stop buying without telling the business, so churn must be inferred from the absence of purchases.
