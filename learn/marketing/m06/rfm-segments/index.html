RFM segments

Turn R, F and M scores into named segments on the R × FM grid, size each one in members and in spend, choose what to do with each (and whom to leave alone), and check the segments against what members did next: Lantern’s “Loyal” members turned out to be among the least likely to come back.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Classify scored customers into segments on the R × FM grid, naming them with a stated convention.
  • Calculate each segment’s size, share of members, share of spend, mean recency and mean frequency, in Excel with COUNTIFS, SUMIFS and AVERAGEIFS.
  • Choose an action for each segment, including whom not to target, and judge whether an offer can pay for itself once members who would have come anyway are counted.
  • Evaluate segments against later behaviour with return rates, lift and a two-proportion test, and explain why a frequent customer gone quiet is a poorer prospect than an infrequent one.
  • Explain the limits of RFM segments: backward-looking, silent on why, moving with the date, associations not causes, and open to regression to the mean.
  • Compare RFM segmentation with the needs-based segmentation of M05.

Review first

  • RFM scoring — This lesson starts from scored customers: R, F and M from 1 to 5 by quantile cut points, and FM, the rounded mean of F and M.
  • Profiling segments — Sizing segments in people and in spend, share of spend against share of people, and naming segments honestly.
  • Psychographic and behavioural segmentation — Behavioural segmentation and needs-based segmentation, which RFM is compared with here.
  • Proportions and chi-square tests — The two-proportion z test used to compare segments’ return rates.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

It is January 2028. Ana has every loyalty-app member who joined Lantern’s cafés in 2027, 2,584 of them, scored as of 31 December on recency, frequency and monetary value, each from 1 to 5. The scores say how each member compares with the others. They don’t yet say what to do. A score of 3-5-5 is a member who once came often and spent well but hasn’t been in for a while; 5-1-1 is someone who joined last week. Ana wants a handful of groups her café managers can act on, and a way to tell whether the groups mean anything.

An RFM segment is a group of customers with similar R, F and M scores, given a name and an action. Five scores on each of three measures give 125 possible cells, far too many to act on, so practitioners collapse them. The most common collapse keeps recency on its own and combines frequency and monetary value, because F and M usually move together: a member who visits often also spends more in total.

The R × FM grid

FM is the mean of the F and M scores, rounded to a whole number (halves round up): a member with F = 4 and M = 5 has FM=(4+5)÷2=4.5\text{FM} = (4 + 5) \div 2 = 4.5FM=(4+5)÷2=4.5, which rounds to 5. Plotting R (rows) against FM (columns) gives the R × FM grid, 25 cells. Each cell, or block of cells, then gets a name. The naming below is one widely used practitioner convention, not a standard: marketing software vendors name and draw the blocks differently (Braze’s documentation, for example, scores 0–3 and defines ten groups by its own rules), so always say which scheme you used.

Key idea: A segment is a description, not a diagnosis

A name like “At risk” describes a pattern of scores on one date. It says nothing about why the members behave as they do, nothing about what an offer would change, and it may not even predict what they do next. All three need other evidence; the last can be checked, as this lesson does below.

From segments to actions

Each segment suggests a different kind of contact, and some suggest none. The list follows the engine’s naming; the actions are common practice, not evidence that they work.

  • Champions: Reward them; ask for reviews and referrals.
  • Loyal: Upsell and invite them into a loyalty scheme.
  • Potential loyalists: Give them a reason for the next visit.
  • New customers: Welcome them; make the second purchase easy.
  • At risk: Contact them personally; find out what changed.
  • Can’t lose them: Win them back with the best offer, and talk to them.
  • Hibernating: Low-cost reminders only.
  • Lost: Very cheap reactivation, or let them go.

Two principles keep this honest. First, a discount should go where it changes behaviour, not where it rewards behaviour that was happening anyway: Champions visit without being asked, so a voucher to them mostly subsidises visits Lantern would have had. Second, every contact has a cost, in money and in attention: a member who has stopped coming and is pestered weekly is not won back, and an app’s notifications can be switched off. Deciding whom not to target is as much a part of the plan as deciding whom to target.

In depth

Who is in each segment

Segments are partly made by tenure. Recency and frequency both depend on how long a member has had to visit, so the segments sort members by when they joined as much as by how they behave. Of the 121 New customers, 95 joined in December; none of the 292 Lost members joined after July (the latest joined on 10 July). Comparing a March joiner’s frequency with a December joiner’s is comparing ten months with a few weeks. Cohort analysis, the next lesson, separates tenure from behaviour by comparing members who joined at the same time.

Scores are also relative to this customer base. Harbourside’s tickets are bigger ($5.77 a visit for members who joined there, against $3.61 at Millbrook), so the monetary score partly measures which café a member uses: Harbourside members are 34% of all members but 43% of those with M = 5 and 24% of those with M = 1. If the cafés are to be managed separately, score each café’s members against each other.

Checking segments against later behaviour

A segmentation that says something true about members should predict what they do next. Lantern can test that with data it already has: score the 1,664 members who had joined by 31 August as of 31 October 2027, using only what was known then, and see who came back in November and December. Nobody was sent anything, so these are the return rates with no campaign at all. Overall 566 came back, a base rate of 34.0%; a segment’s lift is its return rate divided by the base rate.

Champions passed the test: 77.4% came back (lift 2.28), and their Nov–Dec spend was 81.9% of these members’ total. Recency alone did most of the work. Return rates by R score alone ran 86.8%, 54.5%, 17.0%, 6.4%, 3.3% from R = 5 down to R = 1. But two names failed badly. Only 8 of the 106 “Loyal” members came back (7.5%), against 61.0% of Potential loyalists. And none of the 58 members of “Can’t lose them” returned, while At risk (2.5%) did worse than Hibernating (8.9%), the segment the naming treats as less valuable.

The gaps are not chance. A two-proportion z test (B04) of Loyal against Potential loyalists gives z = -8.4, p below 0.001; At risk against Hibernating gives z = -2.75, p = 0.006. The tests treat these members as a sample of the kind of members Lantern will have, which is the question Ana cares about.

Why the frequent and quiet are poor prospects

Lantern’s members, like most customers of a café or a shop, are in a noncontractual setting: nobody announces they have stopped coming, so being gone has to be inferred from silence. Silence means more for a member who used to come often. If a member who visited twice a week hasn’t been seen for two months, that silence is very unlikely if they were still coming; for a member who came once a month, two quiet months are unremarkable. So at the same recency, higher frequency is a worse sign. Fader, Hardie and Lee (2005) showed this pattern in their model of an online music retailer’s (CDNOW’s) customers, in the iso-value curves fitted to its recency and frequency, and called it the increasing frequency paradox.

Lantern’s data show it plainly. Among members whose last visit was 61–90 days before 31 October, 22% of those with one or two visits came back, against 2% of those with seven or more. Within R = 3, return rates fell as FM rose: 20.5%, 22.7%, 20.6%, 9.1%, 5.9% for FM = 1 to 5. That is exactly where the grid puts “Loyal”: R = 3, FM of 4 or 5. The grid assumes value and recency add up; in this setting they interact, and the names inherited the error.

Caution: Names are hypotheses

“Loyal” and “Can’t lose them” are labels from a convention written for typical businesses. On Lantern’s data, scored on 31 October, the Loyal segment came back at 7.5% and Can’t lose them at 0%. Check every naming against later behaviour before acting on it, and rename segments that fail.

Scores move with the date

A segment is a snapshot. Every day a member doesn’t visit, their recency grows by one; every day other members visit, the cut points move. Rescoring the same 1,664 members on 31 December put 78% in the same segment as on 31 October. Even among the 1,098 who never came back, 197 changed segment, 45 of them from Champions to Loyal. A campaign list should be drawn on the day it is sent, and a report should give its as-of date.

Limits of RFM segments

  • Backward-looking. RFM summarises the past. It forecasts only as far as the past repeats; Fader, Hardie and Lee show that using R, F and M as inputs to a probability model of buying gives a forward-looking forecast that simple scores miss.
  • No reason why. Scores say who is quiet, not whether they moved house, switched to a rival, or only come in summer. Contact, a survey or a needs study answers why.
  • Relative and dated. Cut points depend on the customer base and the as-of date. A “5” in December 2027 is not the same customer as a “5” in December 2028.
  • Associations, not causes. Champions have notifications on more often than Lost members (58% against 47%), but members chose notifications themselves; switching them on for a Lost member need not make a Champion. Only a randomised test shows what an action changes.
  • Regression to the mean. Low scores are partly bad luck in timing, and some low scorers come back on their own (below).

Regression to the mean in win-back campaigns

A member whose last visit happened to fall just before a quiet spell scores low on recency; some of them were never gone. Pick members because their scores are low, and some will look better next time without anything being done: regression to the mean. Barnett, van der Pols and Dobson (2005) note it is more noticeable when follow-up is limited to a group selected on its first measurement, which is exactly what a win-back list is. In Lantern’s data 11 of the 222 Lost members (5.0%) and 16 of the 179 Hibernating (8.9%) came back in November–December with no campaign. Had Ana mailed them a voucher, she might have credited it with those returns. The remedy is a holdout group: send the offer to a random part of the segment and compare its return rate with the rest. Loyalty and retention strategy shows how to analyse such a test.

RFM and needs-based segments

M05 segmented by needs: a survey asked what people want from coffee, and clustering found groups with similar answers. RFM is behavioural segmentation on a business’s own records: what each customer has done with Lantern. The two answer different questions. Needs-based segmentation explains why people buy, covers people who aren’t yet customers, and guides products and positioning; it depends on a survey and a typing tool to place individuals. RFM covers every customer automatically, updates daily and is cheap, but it says nothing about non-customers or about why. Used together, RFM decides whom to contact this month and the needs segments decide what to say; if some members answered the needs study, cross-tabulating their two segments shows which needs Lantern’s Champions share.

Common mistakes

  • “The segment names are standard definitions.”

    They are one practitioner convention; vendors define “At risk” or “Champions” with different cells and scales. State the scheme, the cut-point rule and the as-of date with any segment count.

  • “Loyal and Can’t lose them are the best win-back targets because they were valuable.”

    In a noncontractual setting, a frequent customer gone quiet is more likely gone. Scored on 31 October, Lantern’s Loyal members came back at 7.5% and Can’t lose them at 0%, against 34.0% overall. Past value may still justify a test, but not an assumption.

  • “Send the discount to the Champions; they respond best.”

    They “respond” because they come anyway: 77.4% returned with no offer. A discount to them is mostly a subsidy. Judge an offer by the extra returns it causes, measured against a holdout group.

  • “The win-back campaign brought back 5% of Lost members.”

    Without a control group that figure means nothing: 5.0% of Lantern’s Lost members returned with no campaign at all. Selecting on low scores invites regression to the mean; compare with a random holdout.

  • “Members with notifications on are Champions, so turn notifications on for everyone.”

    Members chose notifications themselves, and the members who choose them may differ in other ways. A segment’s profile is an association. Only a randomised test shows whether an action changes behaviour.

  • “A member in At risk today will still be At risk next month.”

    Segments move with the date even when the member does nothing: 197 of the 1,098 members who never came back changed segment between 31 October and 31 December. Draw lists on the day they are used.

In practice

Fictional case

January 2028. Ana meets Lantern’s two café managers to agree what the app should send members in the first quarter. She brings the 31 December segments and the 31 October check, and one rule: no segment gets a discount unless a random part of it is held back.

Champions get thanks, not discounts. 563 members, 48.5% of 2027’s member spend. The managers will invite them to taste new beans first and ask them to recommend the app to a friend; no voucher, because the October check showed most come back anyway.

New customers and Potential loyalists get a second-visit nudge. Recency is the strongest sign a member is still coming, and these members are recent but have little history. A message a week after joining, tested on a random half of January’s joiners, will show whether it lifts second visits.

At risk, Can’t lose them and the renamed Lapsed regulars (R = 3, FM 4–5) get one win-back message, with a holdout. Their past spend makes a small test worth running; their return rates without any offer (2.5%, 0 of 58 and 7.5% in the October check) say not to expect much, and suggest most of Can’t lose them are gone. Half of each segment, chosen at random, gets nothing, so any difference is the message’s effect and not regression to the mean.

Lost and Hibernating are left alone. 516 members, 4.5% of spend. Repeated messages cost attention and may lead members to switch notifications off; they will see only general news.

Café by café. Because Harbourside’s tickets are larger, the managers will also score each café’s members against each other, so that a Millbrook regular isn’t ranked low on M only for drinking cheaper coffee. Ana rescores on the first of each month, records each list’s as-of date and checks every named segment against the next two months, as she did in October. Predicting churn builds on that check with a model that uses recency, frequency and the members’ other details together.

Summary

  • RFM segments collapse 125 score cells into a few named groups; the common R × FM grid keeps recency separate and combines F and M as FM = their rounded mean.
  • The names (Champions, Loyal, At risk, Can’t lose them, Hibernating, Lost …) are a practitioner convention, not a standard; state the scheme, the cut-point rule and the as-of date.
  • Size each segment in members and in spend: Lantern’s Champions are about a fifth of members and nearly half of member spend.
  • Choose actions by what an offer would change, not by who spends most: a discount to customers who would come anyway is a subsidy, and some segments should be left alone.
  • Evaluate segments against later behaviour with return rates, lift and a two-proportion test; at Lantern, “Loyal” and “Can’t lose them” failed, because a frequent customer gone quiet is likely gone.
  • RFM segments are backward-looking, relative, dated and silent on why; their profiles are associations, and win-back results need a random holdout because of regression to the mean.
  • RFM is behavioural segmentation of existing customers; needs-based segmentation explains why and reaches non-customers. Use them together.

Terms introduced

RFM segment
A named group of customers with similar recency, frequency and monetary scores, defined by a stated rule on a stated date, such as a block of cells on the R × FM grid.
R × FM grid
A 5 × 5 table with the recency score as rows and FM, the rounded mean of the frequency and monetary scores, as columns, whose cells are grouped into named RFM segments.
Lift
A group’s rate of an outcome divided by the rate for everyone (the base rate): lift 2 means twice as likely as average.
Increasing frequency paradox
In a noncontractual setting, the finding that among customers with the same long silence, those who used to buy more often are less likely to buy again, because their silence is stronger evidence that they have stopped.
