Cohort analysis

Group customers by when they joined and follow each group month by month: build and read the cohort triangle, choose between share active, number active and revenue per joiner, tell a better cohort from a season or a change in who joined, and avoid the mix effects that make pooled figures mislead.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Construct a cohort table by joining month, left-aligned by months since joining, from transactions, and calculate months since joining in Excel.
  • Calculate share active, number active and revenue per joiner, and decompose revenue per joiner into share active, visits per active customer and spend per visit.
  • Interpret a cohort table down its columns (are newer cohorts better?) and along its rows, and test a cohort’s difference with a two-proportion test.
  • Distinguish a cohort effect from seasonality and from a change in the mix of customers who joined.
  • Explain why pooled curves and whole-base figures mislead when cohorts of different ages are mixed, including a Simpson’s-paradox reversal.

Review first

  • Customer metrics — Turning transactions into customer-level measures as of a date, and why “churn” has to be inferred for café members.
  • RFM segments — RFM segments mix members of different tenures; cohorts separate them.
  • Proportions and chi-square tests — The two-proportion z test used to compare one cohort’s share active with others’.
  • Customer value, satisfaction and lifetime value — Retention rate, churn rate and customer lifetime value, which cohort tables feed.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern’s loyalty app had 242 active members in March 2027 and 975 in December. The café managers read that as success. Ana isn’t sure: new members joined every day, so a rising total could hide members fading away as fast as new ones arrive. To see what happens to a member after joining, she needs to follow groups of members who joined at the same time.

Cohort analysis groups customers into a cohort, everyone who joined (or first bought) in the same period, and follows each cohort through time. Lantern’s cohorts are joining months: the March 2027 cohort is the 242 members who set up the app in March. Comparing cohorts at the same age, and the same cohort at different ages, separates two things a total mixes: how members behave after joining, and how many joined.

The cohort triangle

A cohort table has one row per cohort and one column per period since joining. A member is active in month kkk of their cohort if they made at least one visit in that calendar month, where month 0 is the joining month. Every Lantern member is active in month 0, because the join is a visit at the till. The share active in month kkk is the number active divided by the cohort’s size. Newer cohorts have been watched for fewer months, so the table is a triangle: the March cohort has ten months of data (March to December), the December cohort one.

This layout is left-aligned: every cohort starts in column 0, so a column compares cohorts at the same age. Read along a row and you follow one cohort as it ages: the March cohort fell to 60% active in its first full month, 33% by month 3 and 17% by month 9. Read down a column and you compare cohorts at the same age: in month 1, the March to September cohorts sat between 59% and 63%. Every row drops most steeply from month 0 to month 1 and then flattens, a typical shape: members who come back once are likely to keep coming for a while.

The same numbers can be laid out by calendar month instead. Then each cohort starts in its own joining month and a calendar month is a column; in the left-aligned table the same calendar month runs along a diagonal. The calendar view is the one to use for anything that happens on a date, such as a price change or a quiet summer.

Three measures

The triangle can hold three different measures, and each answers a different question. Share active asks whether members keep coming. Number active asks how many members are coming, which depends on how many joined. Revenue per joiner, the cohort’s spend in a month divided by the number who joined, asks what a joiner is worth month by month. It is the product of three parts: share active × visits per active member × spend per visit. In April, its month 1, the March cohort had 146 of 242 members active (60.3%), making 2.92 visits each and spending $4.00 a visit: $7.04 per joiner.

Key idea: Same age, same measure

Compare cohorts only at the same age (a column of a left-aligned table) and on the same measure. A cohort that is “worse” in its sixth month than another in its second is simply older, and a bigger cohort has more members active without being any better.

In a café, “active in month k” is not the same as “still a customer”. A member can skip a month and come back, so a row can rise as well as fall; nobody cancels. Many reports still call the share active “retention”. When a lesson needs the share still a customer, it has to define being lapsed, as Survival analysis does.

In depth

Reading down the columns: are newer cohorts better?

Two cohorts stand out in month 1. Only 49% of the 225 October joiners were active in November, and 70% of the 221 November joiners were active in December, against 60.7% for the March to September cohorts together. (The October cohort is B06’s 225 members, of whom 121 came back within 30 days of joining.) Each cohort has about 250 members, so a share active is uncertain by several points, and a difference needs testing before it is read.

A two-proportion z test (B04) of November’s month 1 (154 of 221) against March to September’s (1,176 of 1,936) gives z = 2.59, p = 0.010; October’s gives z = -3.30, p = 0.001. But nine cohorts were compared, and with nine looks one p near 0.01 is not surprising. A Bonferroni correction (B04) asks for p below 0.05 ÷ 9 = 0.0056: October passes, November does not. October’s weak start looks real; November’s strong one needs another month before Ana reads much into it.

Cohort quality or a change in who joined?

If November’s joiners do keep coming more, the next question is why. A cohort is better or worse because of the members in it, and the mix of members can change. November’s joiners were 74% Millbrook members (against 64% in March to September), 47% lived or worked within a mile (40%) and 61% had notifications on (55%). Millbrook members, near members and members with notifications on all come more often, so some of November’s lead could be a mix effect: the cohort’s average changing because the shares of different kinds of member changed. How much can be measured by direct standardisation: take March to September’s month-1 rate for each combination of café, near and notifications, and average those rates over November’s members. That predicts 61.7% for November, against 60.7% for March to September and the 69.7% November actually reached. The mix explains about 1 point of a 9-point lead.

The rest is most likely chance. Within Millbrook alone November still led (73% against 63%; z = 2.49, p = 0.013), but that p doesn’t pass the Bonferroni threshold either, and even within Millbrook November’s joiners were more often near (49% against 45%) and had notifications on more often (62% against 55%), which the standardisation already allows for. Nothing in the data marks November out; its lead is the kind of result that one cohort in nine produces by chance.

Even where a mix effect is real, it says nothing about what caused it. The members chose their café, where they live and whether to switch notifications on; a cohort’s composition and its behaviour are associations. If something Lantern did in November (a sign-up drive, a new barista, a promotion) changed who joined, the cohort table shows the result but can’t separate the action from everything else that happened that month.

Seasonality or cohort quality?

A cohort’s month 1 falls in a different calendar month for each cohort, so seasonality can look like cohort quality. The test is where the pattern sits: an effect of the calendar shows on a diagonal of the left-aligned table (every cohort affected in the same calendar month, whatever its age); an effect of the cohort runs along its row (the cohort different at every age). Lantern’s footfall is seasonal at Harbourside, on the waterfront: B05’s log shows July’s footfall at 1.34 times the year’s average and December’s at 0.75. Members visit more on busy days, so Harbourside’s active members made 2.82 visits each in July and 2.78 in August but 2.08 in December. Millbrook’s footfall is flatter (1.09 and 1.04), and so are its visits per active member (3.24, 3.36 and 3.14). On the diagonal, every cohort from March to September lost fewer points of share active from November to December than its average month-on-month loss before that; rows flatten with age anyway, so that is weak evidence, but nothing suggests December hurt the cohorts.

In a busy month the members who are still coming make more visits, so the season shows in visits per active member and revenue per joiner as well as in the share active. A cohort that joined in a busy month has a high month-0 and month-1 revenue partly because of the month. Compare such cohorts with the same calendar months a year later, once there are two years of data, or on a measure the season moves less.

Pooled curves and the mix of ages

The bottom row of the triangle is the pooled curve: for each month since joining, the members active divided by the members who joined, over every cohort observed that long. Each point is a weighted average of the cohorts, weighted by size. It is the natural summary, with one catch: the cohorts behind each point change. Month 1 averages nine cohorts; month 9 is the March cohort alone (17%). If early cohorts differ from later ones, the pooled curve’s tail describes different members from its head, and its shape mixes how members age with who the early members were.

Figures for the whole base mix ages too. Of the members who had joined before each month, 60.3% were active in April and 32.5% in December. That fall doesn’t mean members got worse: every cohort’s month 1 stayed near 60% (apart from October and November). It means the base got older, and older members are less often active. Meanwhile the total of active members rose from 242 to 975, because the base grew. A total, a share of the whole base and a cohort’s share answer three different questions.

Caution: Simpson’s paradox in customer data

When two groups of customers have different age mixes, one can lead within every cohort and still trail overall, because its customers are mostly from older cohorts. This is Simpson’s paradox: the comparison reverses when the cohorts are pooled, because cohort age is a confounding variable. Compare groups cohort by cohort, at the same age, or standardise them to a common mix of cohorts. The challenging example works one through.

The two cafés

Splitting the triangle by café shows two different businesses. Millbrook members keep coming more: the pooled share active in month 1 is 62.9% at Millbrook and 56.0% at Harbourside. But Harbourside’s tickets are bigger, so revenue per joiner in month 1 is $8.49 at Harbourside and $7.58 at Millbrook. Over their first six months (months 0–5), the 497 Harbourside joiners of March to July spent $40.09 each and the 874 Millbrook joiners $31.91. With fewer members per cohort, Harbourside’s rows are noisier: 4 times a Harbourside cohort’s share active rose from one month to the next (the April cohort went from 54% to 58%), against 0 in the combined table.

Which café gets more from a new member is a question about these two sets of customers, not about what would happen if a Harbourside customer were moved to Millbrook. The cafés’ customers differ in where they live, what they buy and when they come.

Common mistakes

  • “Active members are rising, so retention is fine.”

    A total grows with every joiner. Lantern’s active members rose from 242 to 975 while each cohort’s share active fell month by month. Follow cohorts, not totals.

  • “The March cohort is our worst: only 17% are active.”

    It is 9 months older than the newest cohort. Compare cohorts in the same column: in month 1 March (60%) was typical.

  • “The pooled curve shows how a member’s activity changes over ten months.”

    Its later points rest on fewer, older cohorts (month 9 is March alone), so its shape mixes ageing with who the early members were. Check the curve cohort by cohort before reading its tail.

  • “November’s cohort is better, so November’s marketing worked.”

    A cohort’s lead can be chance (test it, allowing for how many cohorts were compared), a season (look along the diagonal), or a change in who joined (standardise to its mix). Lantern’s November lead is mostly chance: its mix predicts 61.7%, against 69.7% achieved and 60.7% before. Even a real lead is an association; only a planned comparison shows what an action did.

  • “Online members are more active overall, so online sign-up is better.”

    If one group’s customers are mostly from older cohorts, its overall share active is lower even when it leads at every age: Simpson’s paradox. Compare at the same age or standardise to a common cohort mix.

  • “Months since joining is what DATEDIF counts.”

    DATEDIF counts complete months, so a member who joins on 31 March and visits on 1 April gets 0, not 1. Cohorts by calendar month need =(YEAR(C2)-YEAR(B2))*12+MONTH(C2)-MONTH(B2).

In practice

Fictional case

February 2028. Lantern’s owner asks Ana for one slide on the app for the quarterly review. The managers want to show the 975 active members of December; Ana wants the slide to say what a new member is worth and whether that is changing.

She shows the triangle, not the total. The slide has the share-active triangle with months since joining across the top and the pooled curve beneath it, and one sentence: about 60% of joiners come back in their first full month, about a third (33%) are still visiting in month 4, and the March cohort was at 17% after nine months.

She flags the two odd cohorts, with their uncertainty. October’s weak first month (49%) holds up after allowing for nine comparisons; November’s strong one (70%) doesn’t, and its mix of members explains only about a point of it. Both will be revisited when February’s data are in, and the managers have written down what was different about those months.

She values a joiner by café. Over the first six months a Harbourside joiner spent $40.09 and a Millbrook joiner $31.91, worth $27.66 and $22.02 in margin at the cafés’ 31% cost of goods (F03). Revenue per joiner, decomposed into share active, visits per active member and spend per visit, will be the app’s main measure; Customer lifetime value turns it into a value per member, and Acquisition cost and payback sets it against what a sign-up costs.

She sets up the comparisons for next year. From March 2028 every cohort can be compared with the same month a year earlier, which separates season from cohort. Any change to how members are signed up will be run so that its cohorts can be compared with cohorts signed up the old way, rather than read off a single good month.

Summary

  • A cohort is everyone who joined in the same period; cohort analysis follows each cohort through time, so behaviour after joining isn’t hidden by how many joined.
  • A left-aligned cohort table has a row per cohort and a column per month since joining (month 0 = the joining month); newer cohorts have fewer months, so it is a triangle. Calendar months run along its diagonals.
  • Share active, number active and revenue per joiner answer different questions; revenue per joiner = share active × visits per active member × spend per visit.
  • Read down a column to compare cohorts at the same age; test a cohort’s difference and allow for how many cohorts were compared.
  • A cohort can differ because of chance, the season (a diagonal pattern), a change in who joined (a mix effect) or genuine differences; all are associations.
  • Pooled curves and whole-base shares mix cohorts of different ages; comparing groups with different age mixes can reverse a comparison (Simpson’s paradox), so compare at the same age or standardise.
  • Lantern’s Millbrook members keep coming more often; Harbourside’s spend more per visit and are worth more per joiner over the first six months.

Terms introduced

Cohort
A group of customers who joined, or first bought, in the same period, such as a calendar month, followed together through time.
Cohort analysis
Comparing cohorts of customers at the same age, and following each cohort as it ages, to separate how customers behave after joining from how many joined.
Cohort table
A table with one row per cohort and one column per period, showing a measure such as the share of the cohort active; left-aligned by periods since joining it forms a triangle, because newer cohorts have fewer periods.
Left-aligned
Of a cohort table or chart: laid out by periods since joining rather than by calendar date, so that every cohort starts in the same column and a column compares cohorts at the same age.
Pooled curve
For each period since joining, the customers active divided by the customers who joined, over all cohorts observed that long: a size-weighted average of the cohorts’ curves.
Direct standardisation
Comparing groups by applying one set of rates for each kind of member (from a reference group) to each group’s own mix of members, or one common mix to each group’s rates, so that differences in mix are separated from differences in behaviour.
Mix effect
A change or difference in an overall average that comes from a change in the shares of different kinds of member in the group, rather than from any kind behaving differently.
