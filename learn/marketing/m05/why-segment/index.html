Why segment?

A market is rarely one crowd wanting one thing. Learn what a market segment is, how segmentation, targeting and positioning fit together, when segmenting pays and when it costs more than it earns, and how to judge whether a set of segments is usable. Then read Lantern’s needs study to decide whether Harbour City has one coffee market or several.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Define a market segment and market segmentation, and explain how segmentation, targeting and positioning (STP) form one process.
  • Explain why customers’ differing needs and responses make segmented marketing pay, and when mass marketing is the better choice.
  • Calculate the gain from tailoring offers to segments after the extra fixed costs, and the smallest segment a tailored offer can pay for.
  • Assess whether segments are usable with five tests: measurable, substantial, accessible, differentiable and actionable.
  • Distinguish a priori from post hoc segmentation, and consumer from business segmentation (firmographics).
  • Evaluate survey evidence on the spread of customers’ needs to decide whether a market is worth segmenting.

Review first

  • Customer value, satisfaction and lifetime value — Segments are groups of customers who weigh benefits and costs alike; that lesson defined customer value and the value proposition.
  • Competitive advantage — Positioning and Porter’s focus strategy, both built on segments, were introduced there.
  • How organisations buy — Segmenting business markets starts from how organisations buy and who is in the buying centre.
  • Questionnaire design — The needs study is a set of rating questions; reading it needs Likert items and the caveats of rating scales.
  • Sampling methods — The study comes from an opt-in panel with quotas, not a probability sample.

The idea

Fictional case

In May 2027 Lantern sells coffee through its catering, its cafés and its online shop, and it plans to launch Lantern Cold Brew ($3.49 a can) later in the year. Only 14% of the coffee drinkers Ana surveyed had heard of Lantern (M02’s launch plan assumed 12%), so every dollar of marketing has to work hard. To decide where to spend it, Ana paid a panel company $9,600 for the Harbour City coffee-drinker needs study: 640 adults in Harbour City who drink coffee at least once a week, each rating how much eight things matter when they choose coffee, from 1 (not at all important) to 7 (essential). Her first question is the one this lesson answers: does Harbour City have one coffee market, or several?

Every business sells to a market, the set of actual and potential buyers of an offering. The temptation is to picture that market as one crowd and to design one offer for its average member. The trouble is that buyers differ: in what they want, in what they will pay, in where they shop and in which messages reach them. An offer designed for the average can end up fitting nobody well.

A market segment is a group of buyers within a market who have similar needs or would respond in a similar way to a marketing offer, and who differ in those respects from other groups. Market segmentation is the work of dividing a market into such segments, using variables called segmentation bases: age or region, the benefits people seek, how much they buy, their attitudes. This module surveys the bases; module 2 builds segments from data.

Segmentation, targeting and positioning

Segmentation is the first stage of a process usually abbreviated STP: segmentation, targeting and positioning. Segmentation finds and describes the segments. Targeting judges how attractive each one is and how well the business can serve it, and chooses the target market: the segment or segments to serve. Positioning decides the place the offer should hold in those customers’ minds relative to rivals, which M01 introduced as positioning, and the marketing mix then delivers it.

The order matters. A position is always a position for someone: “café-quality cold brew” means something to a buyer who cares about taste and nothing to one who wants the cheapest caffeine. And a target can only be chosen from segments that have been described and sized. That is why M01’s marketing strategy, which customers to serve and how to compete for them, starts with segmentation.

Why segment at all?

Segmentation pays when two conditions hold: customers differ in what they want or in how they respond, and the business can offer different groups something different at a cost they will cover. Then a tailored offer wins more buyers, or wins them at a better price, than one offer for everyone. It also concentrates the budget on the people most likely to respond instead of spreading it across people who never will.

The alternative is mass marketing: one offer and one marketing mix for the whole market. It is not a mistake by definition. It is the right choice when buyers really do want much the same thing, when one offer is far cheaper to make and sell than several, or when the market is too small to divide. The question is never “segment or not?” in the abstract, but whether the extra sales from fitting offers to groups exceed the extra cost of running several.

The idea has a long history in marketing thought. Wendell Smith, writing in the Journal of Marketing in 1956, described market segmentation as “viewing a heterogeneous market … as a number of smaller homogeneous markets in response to differing product preferences …”. The words carry the logic of this whole course: a market is worth dividing when buyers differ, and each part should be more alike inside than the whole.

Read the means alone and the market looks bland: every need averages between 3.62 and 4.66, the middle of the scale. A product built on that profile would be moderately tasty, moderately cheap and moderately local.

But the spread tells a different story. Every need has a standard deviation of 1.28 to 1.67 points, and for every need some respondents call it essential and others say it hardly matters. No respondent is within one point of the average on all eight needs, though with eight needs that is less telling than it seems: even if the needs were unrelated, only about 2 of 640 would be. The stronger evidence is in how the needs combine.

The needs also come in bundles. Of the 146 respondents who rated a low price 6 or 7, 133 rated buying from a local, independent business 3 or lower; of the 110 who rated local 6 or 7, 69 rated price 3 or lower. Just 1 respondent rated both 6 or higher, against about 25 if the two needs were unrelated (146 × 110 ÷ 640). The figure shows the pattern: the ratings run along the diagonal from price-led to local-led, with a broad middle (286 respondents rate both needs 3 to 5) and an almost empty corner where both are very important.

Correlations across all 640 respondents say the same in numbers. Price and local correlate at −0.49: the more someone cares about price, the less they tend to care about local. Other needs move together: taste with local (0.65), convenience with a strong caffeine kick (0.68), sustainability with low sugar and calories (0.67). Bundles like these are what segments are made of. They suggest that Harbour City holds several kinds of coffee drinker, which is worth knowing before Lantern spends a dollar on Cold Brew.

Key idea: Evidence for segmenting, not yet segments

A wide spread and strong bundles say a market is worth dividing. They don’t yet say how many segments there are, who is in each, or whether any is worth serving. Module 2 forms the segments from these ratings with cluster analysis, and module 3 decides which to target.

In depth

What a segment is, and what it is not

A segment is defined by similarity in needs or in response to marketing, not by a label. “Women aged 30 to 44” is a demographic group; it is a segment only if its members want something in common that other groups don’t, or would react to an offer in common. Equally, a segment is not the same as a target market. Segmentation describes the options; targeting chooses among them, and many segments are described only to be left out.

Segments are also a model that the analyst builds, not groups waiting in the market to be found. The same market can be divided in many valid ways (by region, by benefits sought, by how much people buy), and each serves a different decision. A café chain choosing where to open needs a geographic view; one choosing a menu needs a view of tastes. The test of a segmentation is whether it helps a decision, not whether it is “true”.

Two kinds of variable play different roles. The bases are the variables the segments are formed on: in Ana’s study, the eight needs. The descriptor variables, or descriptors, are the variables used afterwards to describe the segments and to find and reach their members: age, region, where people buy coffee. The best bases are often hard to observe, such as needs and attitudes, while the best descriptors are easy, such as age and postcode. Much of segmentation is the craft of linking the two.

Is a segment usable? Five tests

OpenStax’s Principles of Marketing gives five tests of an effective segment, remembered as ADAMS; Kotler and Keller list the same five (France and Ghose, 2019). A segment that fails one may still be interesting, but it can’t yet be marketed to.

The five tests of a usable segment, with the question to ask of any segment Lantern considers
TestWhat it asksAsked of a Lantern segment
MeasurableCan the segment’s size and buying power be estimated with reasonable accuracy?Can Ana say how many Harbour City adults belong to it, and what they spend on coffee?
SubstantialIs it large or profitable enough to be worth an offer of its own?Would its members’ spending cover the cost of a tailored offer or campaign?
AccessibleCan its members be reached at an affordable cost?Is there a channel, medium or place where they gather that Lantern can use?
DifferentiableAre its members alike inside, and different from other segments, so they respond differently?Would a craft message move this group more than the others?
ActionableCan the business design and run an effective programme for it, with the resources it has?Can Lantern make, price and sell what this group wants?

The tests pull against each other. Making segments more alike inside (differentiable) usually means making them smaller (less substantial). Bases that are close to why people buy, such as needs, tend to fail “accessible”, because few media can be bought by need. The usual compromise is to form segments on needs and then make them accessible by describing them with variables that can be reached, as module 2’s profiling lesson shows. A sixth, informal test is worth adding: the segments should be stable enough to last the life of the plan.

A priori and post hoc segmentation

There are two broad ways to form segments. In a priori segmentation the basis and the groups are chosen before the data are analysed: by age band, by region, by heavy against light buyers. The data then size and describe groups that someone defined in advance. In post hoc segmentation, also called data-driven segmentation, the groups are formed from the data, usually by clustering respondents on many variables at once, such as the importance of eight needs. Its number and kind of segments emerge from the analysis and the analyst’s judgement.

The distinction goes back to the segmentation research of the 1970s. France and Ghose’s review traces it to Green and colleagues in 1977, and Wind’s 1978 review of segmentation research listed “traditional a-priori and clustering-based designs” as the established approaches, against newer flexible and componential ones. Each kind has its strengths, and they are often combined: a priori groups for planning and media, post hoc segments for understanding why people buy.

Consumer markets and business markets

The same logic applies to a business market, with different bases. Instead of demographics, business marketers start with firmographics: OpenStax lists industry, location, size, legal structure and performance. They add technographics (the technology a firm uses), its needs, its value to the seller (how much, how often and at what value it buys) and its behaviour, such as how it engages with the seller online. In a business market the buyer is usually a buying centre, several people with different roles, so a segment may be defined by the organisation and also by who decides.

Shapiro and Bonoma (1984) proposed a nested approach for industrial markets: five sets of criteria arranged like rings, from the outer ring, general characteristics such as industry, size and location that are easy to observe, through operating variables, purchasing approaches and situational factors, to the personal characteristics of the buyers at the centre, which are the hardest to assess. A marketer starts from the outside and works in only as far as the extra insight repays the extra effort. The B2B paragraphs of the next two lessons fill in the rings.

What segmenting costs, and over-segmentation

Segmenting is not free. The research comes first: Lantern’s study cost $9,600. Then every extra offer brings costs that one offer doesn’t: product variants, packs and stock, separate campaigns and creative work, staff trained to sell several things, and management attention. A new offer for one segment can also take sales from the business’s existing offers, a risk module 3 calls cannibalisation. The gain from segmenting is what remains after those costs, and the formula card puts it in one line.

The gain is worth calculating before any decision. Glenarrow Cycles, in the simple example below, wins $9,920 more contribution a year by offering commuters and sport riders separate servicing plans, but the second plan costs $9,000 to run, which leaves a gain of only $920. Turned round, the same arithmetic gives the smallest segment a tailored offer can pay for: the extra fixed cost divided by the extra contribution each member brings.

Over-segmentation is dividing a market into more segments than the business can use. The symptoms are segments too small to cover the cost of an offer (they fail substantial), too similar to respond differently (they fail differentiable), or so fine that they change from one survey to the next. Twelve tidy cells in a grid of industry by firm size look rigorous, but the challenging example shows how few of them can pay for a campaign of their own. The cure is to merge cells whose members want the same thing, and to keep separate only those that are large enough and different enough.

At the other extreme, some businesses tailor to individuals: recommendations in an online shop, or a salesperson’s proposal to one client. That works when tailoring costs little per customer, as it often does online, and module 3 treats it as micromarketing. Between one offer for everyone and one for each person lies the practical range where most segmentation sits: a handful of segments, each worth its own offer.

Common mistakes

  • “A demographic group is a segment.”

    Only if its members want something in common that other groups don’t, or respond alike to marketing. Age, region and income are often useful descriptors of segments, but people of the same age can want very different coffee. Test a group by its needs and responses, not by its label.

  • “The average customer tells us what to offer.”

    An average of different groups can fit nobody well. In Ana’s study the needs come in bundles that pull apart: 146 respondents rate a low price 6 or 7 and 110 rate local 6 or 7, but only 1 rates both that high. Look at the spread and at which needs go together before designing for the middle.

  • “More segments are always better.”

    Every segment that gets its own offer adds fixed costs. Segments too small to cover them, or too similar to respond differently, are over-segmentation. Calculate the gain after the extra fixed costs, and merge segments that want the same thing.

  • “Segmentation finds the true segments in the market.”

    Segments are a model built for a decision. The same market can be divided many valid ways, and the useful one depends on the decision being made. Judge a segmentation by whether it helps that decision and passes the five tests, not by whether it is “real”.

  • “Segmenting means a different product for each segment.”

    Segments can be served with different messages, channels, prices or pack sizes as well as different products. Often the cheapest response to a segment is a different message for the same product.

In practice

Fictional case

Tollmere Bank, a fictional regional bank, offers one current account to all its 18,000 small-business customers. Nadia Osei, its head of marketing, has watched it lose newer customers to online banks, and the board has asked whether the bank should offer several accounts instead.

She starts from the decision, not the data. The board’s question is which accounts to offer and to whom. That rules out segmenting by, say, customers’ favourite sports, however interesting, and points to bases that would change what an account should include: how the business pays and gets paid, how often it needs help, and how it likes to bank.

She tries the a priori split first. The bank’s records give a firmographic split for nothing: sole traders, firms with 1–9 staff and firms with 10–49. It is measurable and accessible, because the bank knows every customer’s size. But a survey of a sample of customers shows that needs vary as much within each size band as between them: some sole traders want a cheap app-only account, others want a named adviser.

She builds segments on needs, then makes them reachable. Clustering the survey answers (module 2’s method) gives seven groups. Each account variant would cost about $40,000 a year to run, so Nadia uses the gain formula with the purchase rates from a pilot, merges the groups whose members want the same features, and keeps 3 accounts out of the 7 possible. She then describes each of the three segments by size band, sector and how they bank today, so that the bank can assign existing customers and reach new ones.

She checks the five tests before the board meets. Each segment is large enough to pay for its account (substantial), recognisable from data the bank holds (measurable and accessible), different in what it values (differentiable), and something the bank can deliver with its systems (actionable). Her paper ends with the condition that makes the plan honest: the bank will rerun the survey in a year to see whether the segments have held.

Summary

  • A market segment is a group of buyers with similar needs or responses who differ from other groups; segmentation divides a market into such groups using segmentation bases.
  • Segmentation, targeting and positioning (STP) is one process: describe the segments, choose which to serve, then decide the position to hold with them and build the mix.
  • Segment when customers differ and offers can be tailored at a cost they cover. Mass marketing suits markets where buyers want much the same thing or one offer is far cheaper.
  • C = Σ N_s × b_s × m_s for each strategy; the gain from segmenting is C_tailored − C_one offer − F. The smallest segment a tailored offer can pay for is F ÷ (extra purchase rate × margin).
  • Usable segments are measurable, substantial, accessible, differentiable and actionable (ADAMS). Bases close to why people buy are often the hardest to reach.
  • A priori segments are defined before the analysis; post hoc segments are formed from data, usually by clustering needs. Business markets start with firmographics and work inward.
  • Over-segmentation creates segments too small, too similar or too unstable to act on; merge segments that want the same thing.
  • Lantern’s study shows wide spreads and bundles of needs that pull apart (price against local: 1 respondent rates both 6 or 7, against about 25 expected by chance), so Harbour City is several coffee markets, not one.

Terms introduced

Market segment
A group of buyers within a market who have similar needs or would respond similarly to a marketing offer, and who differ in those respects from other groups.
Market segmentation
Dividing a market into segments of buyers with similar needs or responses, using one or more segmentation bases.
Segmentation base
A variable on which segments are formed, such as region, age, the benefits sought, usage rate or attitude. Variables used only to describe or reach segments once formed are descriptor variables.
Descriptor variable
A variable used to describe and reach a segment once it has been found, such as age, region, income or where people buy, as opposed to the segmentation base used to form it.
STP
Segmentation, targeting and positioning: the process of dividing a market into segments, choosing the segment or segments to serve, and deciding the position to hold with each and the marketing mix that delivers it.
Mass marketing
Offering one marketing mix to the whole market, as if all buyers wanted the same thing; also called undifferentiated marketing.
A priori segmentation
Segmentation in which the basis and the groups are chosen before the data are analysed, such as age bands, regions or heavy against light users.
Post hoc segmentation
Segmentation in which the groups are formed from the data, usually by clustering people on many variables such as their needs; also called data-driven segmentation.
Firmographics
Characteristics of organisations used to segment business markets, such as industry, location, size, legal structure and performance: the business-market counterpart of demographics.
Over-segmentation
Dividing a market into more segments than a business can use: segments too small to pay for an offer of their own, too similar to respond differently, or too unstable to plan for.
