Evaluating segments

A segment is worth targeting only if it is attractive and you can win it. Score segments on size, spending, growth, competition and fit with a weighted scorecard, find the weight that would change the winner, and screen out targets that would be wrong to pursue.

About 85 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Distinguish a segment’s attractiveness (size, growth, profitability, competition, accessibility) from its fit with a company’s objectives and strengths.
  • Calculate a segment’s margin pool from its size, spend per head and margin, and project it over a planning horizon.
  • Convert factor measures to 0–100 scores and combine them in a weighted segment scorecard.
  • Find the threshold weight at which a scorecard’s winner changes, and judge how robust a choice of target is.
  • Assess competition in a segment from the distance between its ideal point and the nearest rival on a perceptual map.
  • Evaluate whether targeting a segment is ethical, using product harm and consumer vulnerability.

Review first

  • Profiling segments — The five segments, their sizes, spend per head and indexes are taken as given here.
  • Situation analysis — The weighted competitive-strength scorecard there uses the same arithmetic as a segment scorecard.
  • What-if analysis: data tables, Goal Seek, scenarios and Solver — Sensitivity analysis and Excel’s data tables test how much the answer depends on the weights.
  • Market orientation: putting customers at the centre — The societal marketing concept and the AMA’s ethical norms frame the ethics of targeting.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

It is June 2027. Module 2 found five segments in Ana’s needs study of 640 Harbour City adults who drink coffee every week, and named them from their profiles: Value routine (152 respondents, 23.8%), On the go (146 respondents, 22.8%), Explorers (129 respondents, 20.2%), Craft and local (109 respondents, 17.0%), Mindful (104 respondents, 16.3%). Ana now has to decide which of them Lantern’s cafés, beans and the planned Cold Brew should be built around. Every segment could buy from Lantern; she can’t afford to design for all of them.

In STP, segmentation describes a market; targeting chooses. Between the two sits an evaluation, which asks two different questions of every segment. Is it worth having? And can this company win it, and does it want to? A segment can be large, rich and growing and still be the wrong target for a firm that has nothing it wants.

Attractiveness and fit

Segment attractiveness is what makes a segment worth serving for any firm that could serve it well. The open textbook Principles of Marketing (BCcampus edition) lists the features of an attractive market: it is large enough to be profitable given the costs of serving it, it is growing, it is not already swamped by competitors (or the firm has a way to stand out), and it can be reached. Those give five factors to measure.

  • Size: how many customers there are.
  • Growth: whether the segment is getting larger.
  • Profitability: how much they spend, and what that spending would earn after the cost of serving it, at the firm’s margins.
  • Competition: how well rivals already serve the segment’s needs.
  • Accessibility: whether the firm can reach the segment through channels and media at a reasonable cost.

Segment fit belongs to the company, not the market. The same textbook asks whether the firm has the resources to compete and whether the segment fits its mission and objectives. A segment that values convenience above all will reward a café chain with outlets everywhere, not a small roaster. Ana’s own view of Lantern is that it is strong on taste and quality, sustainability, being local and independent, and novelty, and weak on price and convenience.

The two questions explain why rivals choose differently. Summit Brew, a premium national brand with wide distribution, may find the busy convenience buyers the best target in the city; Lantern, judged on the same data, may not. Attractiveness can be measured once for everyone; fit has to be judged firm by firm.

Size in money: the margin pool

Counting people is only half of size. A segment’s margin pool multiplies its people by their spend per head and by the firm’s margin: the margin the segment’s whole spending would earn if the firm won all of it. No firm wins all of it, so the pool is a ceiling, not a forecast; its use is comparing segments. (“Margin pool” is this lesson’s own label; the “profit pool” of strategy writing is a different idea.) Ana estimates (a fictional figure) that Lantern keeps 38% of café and bean sales after the direct costs of making and serving them, café staff time included: less than the 52% gross margin F02 found on the online shop, which carries no café staff.

Per 1,000 weekly coffee drinkers in the study’s mix, On the go spends enough each month to give a pool of $5,075 at that margin, Craft and local $4,958, Explorers $4,182, Mindful $2,455 and Value routine $2,442. The largest segment by headcount has the smallest pool. Grown for three years at Ana’s estimates, Craft and local’s pool becomes $5,905 and Explorers’ $5,416: fast growth from a smaller base doesn’t catch up within her horizon.

Keeping the two questions apart on a chart borrows an old idea from strategy. The GE–McKinsey nine-box matrix, which dates from the early 1970s, judges a business unit by two separate measures, the attractiveness of its industry and the unit’s competitive strength in it, rather than by one combined score. The chart below does the same for Lantern’s segments, scoring them as in the scorecard later in this lesson.

Key idea: Two questions, kept apart

Attractiveness is a property of the segment; fit is a relation between the segment and your company. Score them separately before you combine them, so a big number on one can’t hide a weakness on the other.

In depth

Measuring each factor honestly

Size. Lantern’s sizes are shares of 640 people from an opt-in panel with quotas on age band only (a quota sample, M04). Within an age band, the panel’s members may drink more or less coffee than the city’s, so a segment’s share of respondents is an estimate of its share of the market with an unknown bias. Comparing segments is safer than quoting their sizes as market facts, and a size that decides the choice deserves a check against other evidence.

Profitability. The margin pool uses one margin, 38%, for every segment, so its ranking is the same as the ranking of spending. A fuller analysis would use a margin for what each segment buys. M02 showed that a can sold through a supermarket loses 32% of its price to the retailer, while a café sale keeps it all; a segment that buys in supermarkets earns Lantern less per dollar it spends. The cost of serving a segment (delivery, staff time, discounts) belongs here too.

Growth. Ana’s growth rates are her own judgements, not data. Use them to project size over the planning horizon with compound growth, and treat them as the first candidate for the sensitivity test below: the less certain a factor, the more it matters to know whether it decides the answer.

Competition. A segment is contested when a rival already offers what it wants. Module 4’s lesson Perceptual maps builds the tool used here. A perceptual map places brands by how customers rate them, so that brands seen as alike sit close together; a segment’s ideal point is its members’ ideal coffee placed on the same map; and the straight-line distance between an ideal point and a brand says how far the brand is from what the segment wants. The distance from a segment’s ideal to the nearest rival is a measure of how much room is left.

Room is small for Mindful (0.48, Arcadia Coffee) and On the go (0.57, Corner Cup) and largest for Craft and local (1.52, Arcadia Coffee). Lantern itself sits 0.79 from the Craft and local ideal, nearer than any rival, but on the ratings of only 61 people who know it.

Treat room as a rough guide. The map’s two dimensions carry 89.9% of the differences between the brands, not of the ideal points: only 42% of Explorers’ ideal (its squared distance from the map’s centre) lies in the map’s plane, against 67% to 98% for the other segments. A brand’s position is everyone’s average rating of it, not the segment’s own view; the nearest rival ignores crowding (On the go also has Summit Brew at 1.50 and Daily Grind RTD at 1.59); and a large distance can be a gap that is empty for a reason. As a check, room measured across all eight standardised attributes gives distances of 1.44, 1.37, 2.28, 2.97, 1.25 and room scores of 48.4, 46.3, 76.8, 100.0, 42.3. With them Ana’s totals become 43.3, 60.0, 78.4, 88.8, 63.7: Craft and local still leads, and growth’s threshold weight (below) falls from about 7.5 to about 7.0.

Accessibility. All five segments live in Harbour City and buy coffee every week, so none is out of reach. The useful question is the cost of reaching them. Lantern’s cafés already meet the people who mostly buy coffee at a café: 61.5% of Craft and local and 44.2% of Explorers, against 7.9% of Value routine, who mostly brew at home (50%) or buy in supermarkets (35%). When a factor is all or nothing (a licence you lack, customers you can’t legally serve), treat it as a screen before scoring, as with the must-haves in M01’s weighted competitive-strength scorecard.

Fit. The fit score compares a segment’s needs with what the company is good at. Each segment centre from module 2 gives the segment’s mean on each need in standardised units (z-scores: 0 is the average respondent). Ana marks each need +1 where Lantern is strong, −1 where it is weak and 0 otherwise; a segment’s fit is the sum of its z-scores times those marks, divided by the number of marked needs. A positive fit means the segment cares more than the average respondent about what Lantern does well, or less about what it does badly.

The map gives a cross-check of Ana’s self-assessment: the distance from Lantern to each segment’s ideal. Lantern is nearest the Craft and local ideal (0.79) and Explorers’ (2.12), and farthest from Value routine’s (4.67), the same order as the fit scores at both ends. When a company’s view of its strengths and its customers’ view disagree, believe the customers and find out why.

From measures to scores

The factors are in different units: percentages, dollars, map distances, z-scores. Before they can be added they must be put on one scale, usually 0 to 100. The simplest rule for a factor where more is better is the ratio to the best: divide each segment’s value by the best segment’s and multiply by 100. The best scores 100 and the others keep their proportions, so a segment half as large scores 50.

Fit can be negative, so it needs a different rule: centre it on 50 (no better than average) and scale it by the largest fit in either direction, so the segment furthest from average scores 100 (or 0, if its fit is negative) and every other segment falls between. For Lantern the best fit is also the largest, so Craft and local scores 100. These are the rules the site’s targeting engine uses. For a factor where less is better, such as cost to serve, invert the ratio: the cheapest segment’s cost divided by each segment’s.

A common alternative, min–max scaling, sets the worst segment to 0 and the best to 100. It stretches small differences: Lantern’s segments range only from 16.3% to 23.8% of respondents, but under min–max Craft and local’s size becomes 10.4 and Mindful’s 0. Choose the rule before you score, and say which you used.

Weights come from objectives

The weights say how much each factor matters to this decision. They should come from the company’s objectives and be set before the scores are seen; weights chosen afterwards tend to be the ones that crown the segment someone already wanted. Weights are relative: the total is divided by their sum, so 4, 4, 2, 2, 4 gives the same result as 2, 2, 1, 1, 2. It is the arithmetic of the weighted-attribute model from M01, applied by the seller to segments.

Ana wants Lantern to grow its sales without becoming a different business, so she gives size, spend per head and fit 4 each. Growth and room get 2 each: they matter to her, but less than the money Lantern can earn in the next few years and the strengths it can build on. These weights are this lesson’s example, and the choice is hers to defend.

A weight is not the whole story: a factor’s pull on the totals is its weight times the spread of its scores. Lantern’s size scores run only from 68.4 to 100, a range of 31.6, while fit runs from 8.1 to 100, a range of 91.9. Weight × range is size 126, spend per head 259, growth 178, room 137, fit 368: size and fit both weigh 4, but fit can move a segment’s total about three times as far. When you ask what drives a ranking, look at these products, not only at the weights.

How sensitive is the winner?

A scorecard’s total looks precise, but it is built from judgements: the weights, the growth estimates, the fit marks. Sensitivity analysis (B03) asks how far each judgement would have to move to change the answer. For a weighted scorecard there is a neat way to find out. Take the leader A and a challenger B, and a factor on which B scores higher. Raising that factor’s weight by Δ changes A’s weighted sum minus B’s by Δ times the difference in their scores on it, so the two tie when Δ equals the gap in weighted sums divided by B’s advantage on that factor: the threshold weight.

For Lantern, Craft and local’s weighted sum beats Explorers’ by 183.8 points (before dividing by the total weight of 16), and Explorers scores 33.3 points higher on growth. Growth’s weight would have to rise by 183.8 ÷ 33.3 ≈ 5.5, from 2 to about 7.5, for Explorers to draw level: at 7 Craft and local still leads (83.5 against 82.7), at 8 Explorers does (83.5 against 82.7). On size, the only other factor where Explorers is ahead, the weight would have to reach about 18.0. Lowering fit, room or spend per head, even to zero, never puts Explorers in front.

Other judgements can be tested the same way. A firm that cared only about money size, size and spend per head weighted equally, would put On the go narrowly first (86.2 against 85.9). A firm that ignored fit and weighted size heavily (10, 4, 2, 2, 0) would choose Explorers (81.6 against 80.6). Under min–max scaling with Ana’s weights, Craft and local still leads (72.9 against 62.6). In Excel, a data table with the growth weight as its input cell shows the whole curve at once.

The conclusion is not “Craft and local scored 88.8”. It is: Craft and local leads unless growth matters nearly four times as much to Ana as she now says, or unless she both drops fit and weights size far above the rest. That statement tells her which judgement to examine (her growth estimates) and what evidence would change her mind.

What a scorecard can’t do

  • It is compensatory. A high score on one factor makes up for a low one. Screen out segments that fail an absolute requirement (legal, ethical, or beyond the company’s reach) before scoring.
  • It can count one thing twice. The margin pool is size times spend, so a scorecard with size, spend per head and the pool gives size two votes. Strongly correlated measures, such as fit and the share already buying at a café, do something similar: they reward the same difference twice.
  • Its precision is false. Scores built from estimates and judgements can’t separate segments a few points apart. Treat a lead that a reasonable change of weights reverses as a tie, and decide it on other grounds.
  • It can’t supply the objectives. The weights carry the company’s strategy. A scorecard makes a choice explicit and testable; it doesn’t make the choice.

Ethics: who should not be targeted, and how

Targeting means concentrating persuasion on chosen people, and that raises ethical questions that attractiveness ignores. OpenStax’s Principles of Marketing (section 5.7) puts the problem in who is targeted and why: choosing a group is not wrong in itself, but it can exploit people less able to judge a marketing message. Its examples include young children, older adults drawn by promises about health, and low-income consumers drawn by offers such as high-interest credit. On children it cites the American Psychological Association: until about seven or eight, children can’t recognise that an advertisement is trying to sell to them.

N. Craig Smith and Elizabeth Cooper-Martin (1997) found that the public’s unease about targeting rises with two things: how harmful the product is and how vulnerable the targeted consumers are; the concern was not confined to harmful products. A useful screen asks both questions. A sports drink aimed at club athletes raises little concern; high-interest loans aimed at people in financial difficulty raise a great deal, even if the segment scores well. The AMA’s Statement of Ethics, met in M01, asks marketers to “recognize the needs of, and commitments to, vulnerable market segments”, and the societal marketing concept asks them to weigh customers’ long-term interests.

Not targeting a segment is not unethical in itself: every targeting decision leaves people out. OpenStax warns about the opposite error too, excluding people on assumptions (that low-income buyers can’t afford a product, say) rather than evidence. Exclusion becomes a legal issue when it touches protected characteristics: in the United States, for example, in advertising housing, jobs and credit.

For Lantern the screen mostly passes: coffee is a mild product sold to adults. Two cautions remain. On the go values “a strong caffeine kick” more than any other segment, and a Cold Brew campaign built on energy, run on social media, would reach under-18s and people for whom caffeine is a health risk; claims about caffeine need care. And Value routine’s price sensitivity makes it the segment where a misleading discount would do most harm.

Common mistakes

  • “The biggest segment is the best target.”

    Size is one factor of five. Value routine is Lantern’s largest segment, yet it has the smallest margin pool and the worst fit. Size in people, size in money and fit can point in different directions; score them separately.

  • “The scorecard’s total is the answer.”

    The total depends on weights and estimates that are judgements. Report the leader together with the threshold weights that would change it; a lead that a small, reasonable change reverses is a tie.

  • “Set the weights once you see which segment you like.”

    Weights chosen after the scores tend to confirm a favourite. Set them from the objectives before scoring, write down why, and then test them.

  • “Add every measure you have.”

    Measures that overlap count the same thing twice: the margin pool already contains size and spend per head. Strongly correlated measures, such as fit and the share already buying at a café, reward the same difference twice too. Choose one measure per factor.

  • “Min–max scaling is the neutral choice.”

    It makes the worst segment 0 and the best 100 however close they are, which exaggerates small differences. The ratio to the best keeps proportions. Neither is neutral; pick one before scoring and say which.

  • “If the people are adults and the product is legal, any target is fine.”

    Ethical concern rises with the harm a product can do and with the vulnerability of the people targeted. Screen segments for both before scoring, and remember that how you target (the claims, the channels, who else the message reaches) matters as much as whom.

In practice

In a company, the output of this lesson is a recommendation a manager can challenge: the leading segment, the runner-up, the threshold weights that separate them, and the evidence that would move them. A good targeting memo puts the sensitivity in the first paragraph, not an appendix, because it tells the reader which disagreement matters.

Fictional case

Ana’s summary for Lantern reads like this. Craft and local leads with 88.8 out of 100, ahead of Explorers (77.3); it has the highest spend per head, the most room from rivals and the best fit, and 61.5% of it buys coffee mostly at a café, the channel Lantern already runs. Of the single-weight changes, only a growth weight nearly four times hers, or a size weight about 4.5 times hers, would put Explorers ahead (dropping fit does so only if size is also weighted far above the rest), so the next thing to test is her growth estimate for Explorers. On the go has the largest money size but fits Lantern poorly, and Corner Cup is already close to what it wants; Mindful comes third (62.3) and Value routine last. Whether Lantern should serve one segment or more, and with which offers, is the targeting strategy of the next lesson.

Two habits make the method trustworthy. First, separate the measured factors from the judged ones: size and spend per head come from the survey, growth and fit marks from Ana, room from a map on which Lantern’s own position rests on only 61 ratings. Second, test the judged ones hardest. If the decision survives every reasonable change to them, it is robust; if it doesn’t, the scorecard has told you where to spend the next research budget.

The same scorecard serves very different firms. A bank choosing between small-business segments might weight the cost to serve and the risk of default; a travel company might add seasonality. A software firm like Sorrelbank, in a fast-moving market, might put growth first. The factors change with the industry, and the weights with the company’s objectives; the discipline of scoring attractiveness and fit separately, screening first and testing the weights does not.

Summary

  • Evaluating a segment asks two questions: is it attractive (size, growth, profitability, competition, accessibility), and does it fit the company’s objectives and strengths?
  • The margin pool, people × spend per head × margin, measures size in money; it is a ceiling for comparing segments, not a forecast.
  • Put each factor on a 0–100 scale (the ratio to the best keeps proportions; fit is centred on 50), weight the scores from the objectives and divide by the total weight.
  • A threshold weight, the gap in weighted sums divided by the challenger’s advantage on a factor, shows how far a judgement must move to change the winner.
  • Competition can be read, roughly, from a perceptual map as the distance from a segment’s ideal to the nearest rival; check it against the map’s limits and on all the attributes.
  • Scorecards are compensatory and falsely precise: screen must-haves first, don’t count a measure twice, and treat fragile leads as ties.
  • Targeting is unethical when it exploits vulnerability: concern rises with product harm and consumer vulnerability, and with how the targeting is done.

Terms introduced

Segment attractiveness
How worth serving a segment is for any firm that could serve it well: its size, growth and profitability, how well rivals already serve it, and how easily it can be reached.
Segment fit
How well a segment matches a particular company’s objectives, resources and strengths: whether the company can win the segment and wants to.
Margin pool
The margin a segment’s whole spending would earn at a firm’s margin: customers × spend per head × margin. A ceiling for comparing segments, not a sales forecast.
Weighted segment scorecard
A comparison of segments in which each is scored 0–100 on factors of attractiveness and fit, each score is multiplied by the factor’s weight, and the weighted scores are added and divided by the total weight.
Threshold weight
The weight a factor would need, with every other weight unchanged, for a weighted scorecard to rank two options equal; beyond it the order changes.
Vulnerable consumers
People less able than most to judge a marketing message or to protect their own interests in a purchase, such as young children or people under financial or health pressure.
