Differentiation and repositioning

Choose how to make an offer different (product, services, channels, people, image), test which differences are worth promoting, and place the offer among the five value positions. Then plan a repositioning on a perceptual map with fixed axes, work out how far customers’ ratings would have to move, and track the result with a repeated survey.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Identify the ways a company can differentiate an offer: product, services, channels, people and image.
  • Evaluate which differences are worth promoting with the criteria important, distinctive, superior, communicable, pre-emptive, affordable and profitable.
  • Classify offers by value position (more for more, more for the same, the same for less, less for much less, more for less) and explain why some are hard to sustain.
  • Calculate how a planned change in mean ratings moves a brand on a perceptual map with fixed axes, and how far the move brings it towards or away from each segment’s ideal point.
  • Plan a repositioning with its triggers, risks and a tracking survey large enough to detect the change.

Review first

  • Perceptual maps — The map, its loadings and its ideal points are where a planned position is drawn.
  • Positioning statements — Differentiation and repositioning carry out a positioning statement, or change it.
  • Competitive advantage — Differentiation as Porter’s generic strategy, and why a low price needs a cost advantage behind it.
  • Distribution channels and decisions — Channel decisions are one of the main ways an offer is differentiated, and the slowest to change.
  • Comparing two means — Whether a brand’s mean rating changed between two surveys is a comparison of two means.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

On the go drinkers are 146 of the 640 respondents and account for 27% of their coffee spending, the largest share of any segment. 38% of them say they would probably or definitely buy Lantern Cold Brew. Yet on the map their ideal is 4.48 from Lantern, and Lantern is rated low on the two things they want most, convenience and energy. Ana’s question for this lesson: could Lantern be seen as more convenient without losing what Craft and local drinkers see in it, and what would it take?

M01 defined differentiation as one of Porter’s generic strategies: being unique in ways customers value, so that they will pay a premium. This lesson is about the tools: the ways a business can actually make its offer different, how to choose the differences worth promoting, and what to do when the position customers hold in their minds is no longer the one the business needs.

Five ways to differentiate

Kotler and Armstrong’s textbook lists five broad ways to differentiate an offer. Each works through something customers can see or experience; none works by assertion alone.

  • Product: features, performance, quality, design or style. For Lantern Cold Brew, single-origin Ethiopian coffee steeped for 20 hours.
  • Services: speed, convenience, delivery, installation, care. Pre-ordering in Lantern’s cafés, or delivery from its online shop.
  • Channels: coverage, expertise and performance of distribution. In M02’s market, convenience stores sell 30% of Harbour City’s canned coffee, impulse purchases of chilled singles.
  • People: hiring and training staff who are better than rivals’. Lantern’s catering clients named its baristas’ professionalism as a reason for choosing it (M01).
  • Image: symbols, atmosphere, events and the associations a brand carries. Lantern’s strongest is local and independent.

Which differences are worth promoting?

Not every difference deserves to lead. Kotler and Armstrong give seven criteria that a difference should meet before a business builds its positioning on it. The list overlaps with Keller, Sternthal and Tybout’s desirability and deliverability tests from the last lesson; it adds superiority, visibility and affordability for the buyer.

Value positions

Customers weigh what they get against what they pay. Kotler and Armstrong describe five value propositions on which a brand can be positioned, by its benefits and its price relative to rivals. This course calls them value positions, to keep “value proposition” for M01’s promise of benefits.

“More for less” is the most attractive to customers and the hardest to hold: unless the business has lower costs than its rivals, which M01 showed is what a lasting low price needs, its margins or its quality give way. Lantern Cold Brew at $3.49 against Summit Brew’s $3.79 would be more for less if customers judged the can as they judge Lantern (taste 5.92 against 5.29). Against Arcadia at $2.89 it would be more for more. Which comparison customers make depends on the frame of reference.

Repositioning

Repositioning is an attempt to move an offer to a different place in customers’ minds. The BCcampus open textbook calls it an effort to “move” a product in consumers’ minds, often so that it appeals to different segments; Lumen Learning describes it as changing the market’s perceptions of an offering so that it can compete better. It starts from an existing position, which is what makes it harder than positioning a new offer: customers already believe something.

A perceptual map lets a business plan the move before paying for it. Hold the map’s axes where the current brands put them, change the planned brand’s mean ratings, and project the new profile with the same standardisation and weights. Raise Lantern’s convenience rating from 3.15 to Summit Brew’s 5.41, keeping every other rating, and Lantern moves from (3.20, 0.29) to (2.49, 0.59). It ends 0.62 from the Craft and local ideal (from 0.79) and 1.37 from the Explorers’ (from 2.12), but still 3.74 from On the go’s.

The arithmetic answers Ana’s question in two parts. Being seen as more convenient doesn’t cost Lantern its craft position: Craft and local drinkers want a little more convenience than Lantern offers, so the move brings Lantern closer to them, and closer to the Explorers. But convenience alone doesn’t bring Lantern near On the go drinkers, whose ideal also asks for more energy, a lower price and much less of the craft that defines Lantern.

Caution: A slider is not a customer

Moving a point on a map changes nothing in Harbour City. Ratings change only when customers experience something different: cans in the chiller of the shop they already pass, a café fridge by the door, a can that tastes as good as Lantern’s coffee. The map shows where those experiences would have to take Lantern; a repeated survey shows whether they did.

In depth

The arithmetic of a planned move

A brand’s score on a dimension is a weighted sum of its standardised ratings, so a change in one rating moves it in a straight line. Each rating point on attribute a moves the brand by the attribute’s weight on each dimension divided by the attribute’s standard deviation across the brands. The weight is the loading divided by the square root of the dimension’s eigenvalue; the formula card below gives the rule.

A convenience point moves a brand 0.31 towards the cheap and everywhere end and 0.13 up. Raising Lantern’s convenience from 3.15 to 5.00, a rise of 1.85 points, moves it by 1.85 × (−0.313, 0.132) = (−0.58, 0.24), to (2.62, 0.53). Energy works mostly on Dimension 2: a point moves a brand 0.80 up and 0.27 left.

The limits show at the extremes. Even rated 7 on both convenience and energy, the top of the scale, Lantern would sit at (1.21, 3.09): 3.14 from On the go’s ideal, still further than Corner Cup, Summit Brew, Daily Grind RTD or Harbour Basics, and 3.37 from Craft and local, now further than Arcadia (1.52). The map is saying that On the go drinkers want a different kind of coffee brand, not a more convenient Lantern.

Why the axes stay fixed, and when they don’t

Fixing the axes makes a planned move readable: everything else stays where it is, so the move is the plan’s alone. It is also an approximation. In a real repeat survey every brand’s means would be re-estimated, and with only six brands one brand’s move can tilt the axes themselves. The arrows would change too if Lantern broke the pattern that makes convenience and craft opposites on today’s map; a convenient craft brand would weaken the correlation between them, and the map would be re-drawn around it.

Compare two waves of a survey in both ways. First project the new wave’s brand means onto the old map’s axes, which shows each brand’s movement in the old picture, like for like. Then build the new map from the new wave, which shows whether the structure itself, which attributes go together, has changed. A brand that has truly repositioned shows up in the first; a market whose ideas have changed shows up in the second.

Tracking a position over time

A tracking survey repeats the same questions on the same kind of sample at intervals, so that changes can be told from differences in method. For a map that means the same attributes in the same wording and scale, the same brands, the same population and sampling design (the same panel and quotas), and the same rule for which brands each respondent rates. Change any of those and a moved point may be an artefact.

It also means enough ratings of the brand that matters. Lantern’s 61 convenience ratings have a standard deviation of 0.96, so a change between two waves of that size has a standard error of √(0.96² ÷ 61 + 0.96² ÷ 61) = 0.17. A change would have to exceed about 1.98 × 0.17 = 0.35 points to be statistically significant at 5%. To detect a change of 0.3 points with 80% power, B04’s sample-size rule gives 162 Lantern ratings in each wave.

At the study’s current rate (61 Lantern ratings from 640 respondents), 162 ratings would need about 1,700 respondents a wave, about $25,500 at the $15 a complete Ana paid in May (an estimate). A cheaper route is a booster sample of people who know Lantern, recruited by the same panel; it changes who rates Lantern, so it must be used the same way in every wave and reported with its base.

When to reposition, and what can go wrong

Lumen Learning’s reading suggests considering repositioning when there is a need or an opportunity to improve demand: falling sales, a shrinking target segment, new competitors or a rival’s innovation, a shift in the economy or in consumers’ tastes, or a change in the firm’s own leadership, strategy or technology. The process is the positioning process with a different starting point: research, a new positioning statement, the changes that will make it true, and tracking.

The same reading lists the pitfalls: too little research before the move; going “a bridge too far”, so that customers stop believing the claims; underestimating a return to basics; overpromising what the organisation can’t deliver; and a confusing message. One more risk matters most when the current position is valuable: losing the customers who chose the brand for what it was.

For Lantern that risk is concrete. Craft and local drinkers are 17.0% of respondents but 25.9% of their spending, at $76.61 a month a head, with the highest local-loyalty score of any segment (3.94 on the 1–5 scale, against 3.05 for all respondents). A move that made Lantern look like a convenience brand would put that at risk for a segment, On the go, that the map says Lantern can’t reach.

A business that wants to serve a segment far from its current position has other choices than moving its main brand: a separate product with its own name (a sub-brand), a different channel, or leaving that segment to rivals. Which segments to serve is the targeting decision of module 3; repositioning is how a business changes the position it has, and it should follow that decision, not make it.

Common mistakes

  • “Differentiation means adding features.”

    Features are one of five routes. Services, channels, people and image can differentiate as strongly, and are often harder to copy. Lantern’s strongest difference, local and independent, is an image and a fact about ownership, not a feature.

  • “If it is different, promote it.”

    A difference must be important to the target, distinctive, superior, communicable, pre-emptive, affordable and profitable. A difference that fails importance (electric cars to buyers who don’t care) or pre-emption (anything a rival can copy next month) is a weak foundation for a position.

  • “More for less is the best position.”

    It is the best for customers and the hardest for the business to hold. Without lower costs than its rivals, a business offering more for less gives up margin or quality until the position collapses. M01’s value stick shows why.

  • “Moving the slider shows what will happen.”

    The widget shows where a planned profile would sit if customers rated the brand that way, on today’s axes. Whether they will is a question about experiences, and whether they did is a question for a tracking survey large enough to detect the change.

  • “A repeated map with a moved point proves the repositioning worked.”

    Only if the method stayed the same and the change exceeds sampling error. With 61 ratings of Lantern a wave, a change in a mean rating must exceed about 0.35 points before it can be told from chance; and a changed sample design can move a point by itself.

  • “Repositioning is how we reach a new segment.”

    Sometimes; but moving the main brand towards a segment far away risks the customers who chose it for what it was. A separate product or brand, a different channel, or leaving the segment to rivals are often better. Choosing segments is targeting; repositioning follows it.

In practice

Repositioning decisions are rarely made at once. A brand manager states the target position as mean ratings on the map’s attributes, works out what the brand would have to do for customers to rate it that way, writes the success criteria before anything changes, and sets up the tracking survey that will judge the result. The plan is then reviewed after each wave, and abandoned if the costs of the move start to show in the ratings that matter most.

Fictional case

Ana’s plan for 2027–28. Keep the positioning statement from the last lesson: craft, local and independent, for Craft and local drinkers and the Explorers. Treat convenience as a point of parity to reach, through channels (a convenience-store listing for Cold Brew) and services (a café fridge by the door), with a target of Summit Brew’s 5.41. Don’t chase On the go with Lantern’s main brand: the map shows they are 3.74 away even at that target. Repeat the needs study in May 2028 with a Lantern booster.

The plan is deliberately modest. It changes the parts of the mix that customers experience, not the claims, and it measures the result with a survey designed for the purpose. If convenience rises and the craft ratings hold, Lantern has broken, for itself, the trade-off between convenience and craft that today’s map shows across the market. That would be a real difference, and one hard for rivals on either side of the map to copy.

The same sequence serves any repositioning: a gym adding classes, a bank trying to look modern without looking less safe, a travel company moving from cheap package holidays to tailored trips. Plan the target on the map, check every segment’s distance, list what customers would have to experience, set the criteria and the tracking sample before the money is spent, and be willing to stop.

  • A difference worth promoting is important, distinctive, superior, communicable, pre-emptive, affordable and profitable.

Terms introduced

Value position
Where an offer sits in the benefits it gives against the price it charges, compared with rivals; Kotler and Armstrong describe five: more for more, more for the same, the same for less, less for much less and more for less.
Repositioning
An attempt to move an existing offer to a different place in customers’ minds, by changing what they believe about it relative to competing offers.
Tracking survey
A survey repeated at intervals with the same questions, population and sampling design, so that changes over time can be told apart from differences in method.
Sub-brand
A separately named offer under a parent brand, used to serve a segment or carry a position the parent brand can’t credibly hold.
