Profiling segments

Turn a cluster solution into segments a business can use: profile them with indexes against the average, name them honestly, describe who they are and what they buy with cross-tabs and B04’s tests, size them in people and in spend, judge what an opt-in panel can say about sizes, and build a typing tool that assigns new customers from a few golden questions.

About 85 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate a segment’s index against the average and read an index table beside the segment means and centres.
  • Name segments from their profiles without over-claiming, and explain why tests on the segmentation variables prove nothing.
  • Describe segments by demographics and behaviour with cross-tabs, chi-square tests and Cramér’s V.
  • Calculate each segment’s share of respondents, share of spend and spend per head, in Excel with AVERAGEIFS and SUMIFS.
  • Assess what segment sizes from an opt-in panel with quotas can and can’t say about the population.
  • Build and test a typing tool that assigns new customers to the nearest segment centre from a few golden questions, measuring its accuracy on a hold-out.

Review first

  • Cluster analysis — The five segments, their centres in standardised units and the squared Euclidean distance come from there.
  • Analysing survey data — Cross-tabs percentaged within each category of the explanatory variable, and bases.
  • Proportions and chi-square tests — The chi-square test of independence and Cramér’s V describe how segments differ by age, income and the rest.
  • One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) — The F statistic ranks which needs separate the segments most.
  • Conditional sums, counts and averages — AVERAGEIFS, SUMIFS and COUNTIFS build every profile table here.
  • Sampling methods — Opt-in panels and quota samples, and why they are not probability samples.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana’s analyst has handed over five segments from the May 2027 needs study, lettered by size: 152, 146, 129, 109, 104 of the 640 respondents. To Ana they are five letters. Before she can choose which segments Lantern’s cafés, beans and the new Cold Brew should serve, she needs five answers about each: what it wants, what to call it, who is in it, how much it spends on coffee, and how to tell which segment a new customer belongs to.

Answering those questions has two parts. Profiling a segment describes it on the segmentation variables, the needs that formed it; describing a segment uses other information, its descriptor variables: demographics, behaviour, media use, awareness of the brand. Dolnicar, Grün and Leisch treat them as separate steps of a segmentation analysis, because they answer different questions: profiling says what a segment wants, describing says who its members are and how to reach them.

The index against the average

A segment’s profile is easiest to read as an index against the average respondent: the segment’s mean on a need, divided by the overall mean, times 100. Craft and local segment members rate “from a local, independent business” 6.28 on average, against 3.74 for all 640, an index of 6.28÷3.74×100=1686.28 \div 3.74 \times 100 = 1686.28÷3.74×100=168. An index of 100 means the segment is average on that need; above 100, the need matters more to it than to the average respondent; below 100, less. Analysts often treat indexes above about 120 or below about 80 as notable, but that is a convention, not a test.

A profile is a pattern of highs and lows, and the lows describe a segment as much as the highs.

Naming segments honestly

A name is the shorthand everyone will use for a segment for years, so it should say what the profile shows and nothing more. Name a needs segment by its highest indexes, in words customers would recognise, and check the name against the lows. Lantern’s five:

  • Value routine (152): a low price first, convenience second; well below average on novelty, local and sustainability. Highest indexes: price 142, convenience 104.
  • On the go (146): a strong caffeine kick and convenience. Highest indexes: energy 147, convenience 137.
  • Explorers (129): new and interesting flavours, with taste a little above average. Highest indexes: novelty 158, taste and quality 107.
  • Craft and local (109): local and independent, and taste and quality; above average on novelty and sustainability too. Highest indexes: local and independent 168, taste and quality 136.
  • Mindful (104): health and sustainability; low on energy. Highest indexes: health 168, sustainability 159.

Names go wrong when they claim more than the needs show. “Young explorers” would bake in an age that 33% of the segment don’t have. “Lantern loyalists” for Craft and local would be false: only 24.8% of them know Lantern. Names that judge (“coffee snobs”, “cheapskates”) colour every later decision about a group of customers. And no name should suggest a segment is a different kind of person rather than a group whose answers are similar, with edges that overlap.

Who they are and what they do

Cross-tabulating segment by each descriptor, and percentaging within each segment as Analysing survey data taught, turns the needs segments into groups Lantern can find. Explorers are young: 67.4% are aged 18–29, against 32.2% of all respondents. Craft and local are the café-goers (61.5% mostly buy from a café, against 31.9%) and the best-off (78.0% have household incomes over $80,000, against 41.9%). Half of Value routine (50.0%, against 28.8%) mostly brew at home. On the go buy from supermarkets and ready-to-drink cans (39.0%, against 27.5%) or at the office (30.1%, against 11.9%), and Mindful are more often families with children (38.5%, against 22.0%).

How big: people and spend

A segment has two sizes: its share of the respondents and its share of what they spend. The share of spend is the segment’s total monthly spend divided by everyone’s; spend per head is the segment’s total divided by its respondents. Craft and local are 17.0% of respondents but account for 25.9% of their spend, at $76.61 a head against $50.29 for everyone; Value routine are 23.8% of respondents and 12.8% of spend. Sizing tests whether a segment is substantial, one of the ADAMS criteria in OpenStax’s list (accessible, differentiable, actionable, measurable and substantial) that module 1 applied; the profiles and descriptors test the others.

In depth

Indexes, means and centres

A profile can be shown three ways, and each answers a slightly different question. Segment means (Craft and local’s 6.28 on local) stay on the questionnaire’s 1–7 scale. Indexes (168) compare with the average respondent. Centres in standardised units, the k-means centres of Cluster analysis, say how many standard deviations the segment sits from the average (+1.57 on local), which puts every need on the same footing.

Indexes are made for quantities with a true zero, such as spend, counts and shares: “Craft and local spend 152% of the average a head” means what it says. A 1–7 rating has no true zero, so the same gap in points gives a bigger index where the average is low: one point above average is an index of 128 on health (mean 3.62) but 121 on taste (mean 4.66). Read rating indexes beside the means or the centres, and compare them across segments on one need more readily than across needs.

Profiling is not testing

It is tempting to run a one-way ANOVA (B04) on each need and report that the segments “differ significantly”. They do: the F statistics run from 182.5 to 458.0. But k-means chose the segments to make these very means differ, so the test’s assumption, that the groups were fixed before the data were seen, fails, and the p-values mean nothing. The proof is the no-structure benchmark from Cluster analysis: on 640 made-up respondents with random ratings, k-means’ five segments give F statistics from 11.6 to 120.5 on the random ratings, every one with p below 0.000001.

The F statistics are still useful as a ranking of which needs separate the segments most: novelty (458.0), local (445.8), sustainability (348.2) and price (286.5) lead, and taste (182.5) separates them least. That ranking is a quick way to choose the golden questions, with a limit shown below.

Testing descriptors

Tests on descriptor variables are legitimate, because the segments were not formed from them. The test of independence of B04 on each cross-tab of segment by a descriptor gives p below 0.001 for all five here, and Cramér’s V measures how strong each link is: 0.35 for main source, 0.30 for age, 0.26 for income, 0.22 for household and 0.18 for region. They run from weak (region, below module 1’s rough guide of about 0.2, a cut-off that depends on the table’s size) to moderate (main source): associations, not rules.

Three cautions apply. The sample is not a probability sample, so a p-value says the pattern is unlikely to be chance among people like these respondents, not that it holds in Harbour City. Testing many descriptors invites false positives, as B04’s multiple-testing warning explained, so choose the few that matter before looking. And a strong link still leaves a segment mixed: 67% of Explorers are under 30, but Explorers are only 42% of the under-30s, so marketing to under-30s would mostly reach other segments. Demographics describe and help reach a needs segment; they rarely define it, which is why module 1 found needs harder to reach.

Sizing in people and in spend

The share of spend and spend per head come from the same totals: a segment’s share of spend divided by its share of respondents equals its spend per head divided by the average (1.52 for Craft and local, 0.54 for Value routine). Spend here is each respondent’s estimate of their monthly coffee spend, a recall question with the measurement error Survey error described, so the shares are better than the dollar totals. Per head, Craft and local spend $76.61 a month, nearly three times Value routine’s $27.05, though Value routine drink more cups (12.3 a week against 8.4).

Is the sample representative?

The 640 respondents came from an opt-in panel, filled to quota sample targets on age band. The quotas make the age mix match Ana’s targets; they do nothing for income, region or the needs themselves, and within each age band the people are those who joined a panel and chose to answer. The AAPOR task force on online panels advised researchers to avoid non-probability online panels when one of the objectives is to estimate population values accurately (Baker et al., 2010). A panel’s members may, for example, be keener on novelty than the city as a whole, which would overstate the Explorers.

So Ana can say “about a fifth of the coffee drinkers in our study were Explorers”, not “a fifth of Harbour City’s coffee drinkers are Explorers”. She can check the sizes against other evidence (Lantern’s own sales by café, income figures for Harbour City), weight to known population totals as Survey error showed, knowing that weighting can’t fix self-selection within a weighting cell, and treat any market size built from the shares, with Market sizing’s methods, as a planning range. The profiles are more robust than the sizes: what Explorers want is less likely to be distorted by the panel than how many there are.

The typing tool

A segmentation is only useful if new customers can be placed in it. A typing tool assigns a new respondent to a segment: transform their answers exactly as the study did, which for Ana means standardising with the study’s means and standard deviations, compute the squared Euclidean distance to every segment centre, and choose the nearest. Asking all eight needs in a café or a sign-up form is too long, so analysts use a few golden questions, chosen because they separate the segments best. Bock describes choosing them empirically, with a classification method such as discriminant analysis, and notes the trade-off: fewer questions, lower accuracy. Here the F ranking does the choosing, as a quick first cut.

Four questions (price, sustainability, local and novelty) place 92.5% of respondents, 592 of 640, in the same segment as all eight; the four needs with the lowest F statistics place only 83.6%. But ranking needs one at a time by F ignores how the needs overlap, and price, health and energy have nearly equal F statistics (286.5, 277.3 and 274.0). Of all 70 sets of four needs, the top-F four rank seventh; energy, health, local and novelty do best (94.1%). That is why Bock recommends a classification method that judges the questions together. Ana keeps the top-F four, close to the best and easy to explain.

Accuracy belongs on people the segmentation never saw, a test set in B02’s language, and nothing fitted to the tool may use them: not the segments, the means and standard deviations, or the choice of questions. Ana’s analyst segmented the first 480 respondents (the training set), standardised and ranked the needs on them alone (the same four questions came out on top) and typed the other 160: 140 of 160 (87.5%) landed in the same segment as in the segmentation of all 640. Standardising with all 640 and taking the four questions from the full analysis would have given 141 (88.1%), so the leak hardly matters here, and on all eight needs every one of the 160 matched: the centres themselves are stable. The figure to report for the tool is 87.5%.

Keep it in proportion. A hold-out of 160 is uncertain by about ±5.1 points, and which 160 are held back matters: across 40 random splits of 480 and 160, done the same way, the hold-out figure averaged 88.8% (standard deviation 4.4 points, from 79.4% to 94.4%), so Ana’s split is typical. The in-sample 92.5% flatters the tool, but only slightly here: on the same 160 respondents, the tool built from all 640 places 143 (89.4%). All these figures measure agreement with the full segmentation, not with any truth, and one research agency reports that typing tools’ accuracy ranges widely, from about 40% to 90% (Horn, n.d.). New customers answering at a till may also answer differently from panel members at home, so Ana will check the tool again on real customers.

Note: What Ana couldn’t see

Against the five planted kinds of coffee drinker in the case, typing the 160 held-back respondents on all eight needs with the first 480’s centres places 98.1% of them (157 of 160) in the kind they were generated from. No real study has that answer key.

The segment report

The usual deliverable is a one- or two-page report for each segment, built so that someone who never saw the analysis can use it. Dolnicar, Grün and Leisch stress that statistics are no substitute for the judgement of the people who will use the segments, and the report is what they judge from. Each report should give:

  • The name, and one sentence on what the segment wants, from its profile.
  • Its size in respondents and in spend, with the base and what the sample allows (shares of the study, not of the city).
  • The index profile on every need, the lows as well as the highs.
  • Who is in it and what they do: the descriptors that differ most, as percentages with the overall figure beside them.
  • How to reach it (where it buys, the media it uses, if asked) and how to recognise it: the golden questions and the typing tool’s accuracy.
  • What isn’t known: overlap with other segments, small bases, and the panel’s limits.

Common mistakes

  • “The segments differ significantly on every need, so they are real.”

    k-means formed the segments to differ on those needs, so the tests are circular: random ratings clustered the same way give F statistics from 11.6 to 120.5, all “significant”. Use the F statistics only to rank which needs separate the segments, and test descriptors instead.

  • “An index of 168 means the segment cares 68% more.”

    On a 1–7 rating scale with no true zero, an index exaggerates differences on needs with low averages. It means the segment’s mean rating is 1.68 times the overall mean; read it beside the means (6.10 against 3.62 for Mindful on health) or the centres.

  • “Name the segment after its typical member.”

    A segment is a group with similar needs, not a type of person. Names that add an age, an income or a loyalty the needs didn’t measure (“Young explorers”, “Lantern loyalists”) mislead: 33% of Explorers are 30 or older, and 75% of Craft and local don’t know Lantern.

  • “A fifth of the city are Explorers.”

    The shares come from an opt-in panel with age quotas: they are shares of the study’s respondents. Quotas fix age, nothing else. State the base, cross-check with other evidence, and treat population sizes built on them as ranges.

  • “The typing tool is 92.5% accurate.”

    92.5% is how often four golden questions agree with all eight needs on the study’s own respondents. On a hold-out that nothing in the tool was fitted to, it is 87.5%, uncertain by about ±5.1 points, and even that measures agreement with the full segmentation rather than truth; customers answering at a till may answer differently again.

  • “A new customer’s answers can be prepared however is convenient.”

    The tool must transform new answers exactly as the study did, or they won’t be comparable with the centres. For Ana that means standardising with the study’s stored means and standard deviations; centring a customer on her own average, or standardising against the day’s other customers, puts her on a different footing and can move her to the wrong segment.

In practice

Fictional case

Kestrow Bank, a fictional regional bank, segmented 2,400 current-account customers on 9 banking needs into 6 segments. Its customer-insight manager, Eilidh Bisset, had two months to turn the analysis into something the bank’s branch, digital and marketing teams would use.

She wrote one report per segment, to a fixed template. Each opens with the name and a sentence on what the segment wants, then the index profile with the lows as visible as the highs, then the descriptors from the bank’s own records: age band, products held, branch visits and app logins, as percentages beside the all-customer figure. Because the survey had been sent to a random sample of account holders and weighted to the customer base, she could give segment sizes as shares of customers, with margins of error; the bank’s records then gave each segment’s balances and fee income directly.

She tested the names with the people who would use them. Branch managers objected to “Branch traditionalists” for a segment whose highest index was face-to-face advice: many of its members also used the app every week. The segment became “Advice seekers”, which said what the profile showed and nothing about how the members bank.

She built the typing tool into account opening. 5 golden questions, chosen by how strongly each separated the segments, now appear in the online account-opening form, and each new customer is assigned to the nearest centre using the survey’s stored means and standard deviations. On a hold-out of 600 surveyed customers the tool agreed with the full segmentation for 88%; Eilidh reports that figure, the share of new customers whose two nearest centres are close, and a plan to re-run the segmentation every 2 years with the same specification.

Ana’s version is smaller: five one-page reports, a four-question card for café staff to offer customers who join Lantern’s mailing list, and a note on every size that it is a share of the study. With the segments profiled, named and sized, the next module asks which of them Lantern should serve.

Summary

  • Profiling describes segments on the segmentation variables; describing uses descriptor variables such as demographics, behaviour and awareness.
  • Index = segment mean ÷ overall mean × 100; above 100, the need matters more to the segment. Indexes suit spend and shares best; on ratings, read them beside the means or centres.
  • Name segments from their highest (and lowest) indexes in plain words, without adding ages, incomes, loyalty or judgements the needs didn’t measure.
  • Tests on the segmentation variables are circular; use their F statistics only to rank needs. Describe segments with cross-tabs, chi-square tests and Cramér’s V on descriptors, remembering a strong link still leaves segments mixed.
  • Size segments by share of respondents and share of spend; spend share ÷ respondent share = spend per head ÷ the average.
  • Sizes from an opt-in panel with quotas are shares of the study, not of the population; profiles are more robust than sizes.
  • A typing tool standardises a new respondent’s golden answers with the study’s means and SDs and assigns the nearest centre; measure its accuracy on a hold-out, and watch close calls.

Terms introduced

Profiling
Describing segments on the segmentation variables that formed them, for example as indexes against the average, to see what each segment wants.
Descriptor variable
A variable used to describe segments after they are formed, such as age, income, spend or where people buy, as distinct from the segmentation variables that formed them; also called a descriptor.
Index
A segment’s mean on a variable divided by the overall mean, times 100: 100 is average, above 100 the segment is higher than average. Also called an index against the average.
Share of spend
A segment’s total spend divided by the total spend of everyone in the base; compared with its share of people, it shows whether the segment spends more or less than average a head.
Spend per head
A group’s total spend divided by the number of people in it: the mean spend.
Typing tool
A rule or small model that assigns a new respondent or customer to an existing segment, usually from a few questions, for example by the nearest segment centre.
Golden questions
The short set of questions a typing tool uses to assign people to segments, chosen because they separate the segments best.
