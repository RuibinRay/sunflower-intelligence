Cluster analysis

Segment customers on their needs with data: choose and standardise the variables, run k-means (and Ward’s method as a check), pick the number of segments from the elbow, the silhouette and what the segments mean, and test whether the answer is stable. Lantern’s 640-person needs study is the data.

About 95 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Choose the segmentation variables for a needs-based segmentation and explain why they are standardised first.
  • Calculate squared Euclidean distances and segment centres, run k-means by hand for two iterations and calculate the within-cluster sum of squares.
  • Explain why k-means needs k-means++ starts and restarts, why it always returns k groups, and how Ward’s hierarchical clustering and its dendrogram differ.
  • Choose the number of segments from the elbow, the mean silhouette and whether the segments mean something a business can act on, and justify the choice.
  • Assess a segmentation’s stability with restarts, the agreement between Ward’s method and k-means (the adjusted Rand index) and a split-half check.

Review first

  • Normalisation and standardisation — Standardising (z-scores) and why distance-based methods such as k-means need it are used here without being derived again.
  • Psychographic and behavioural segmentation — Benefits sought and needs as a segmentation base, and why they are harder to reach than demographics.
  • Questionnaire design — The eight needs are rating items on a 1–7 scale; their wording and scale come from questionnaire design.
  • Sampling methods — The study comes from an opt-in panel with age quotas, a non-probability sample.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

In May 2027 Ana Lim bought a needs study from a panel company for $9,600: 640 Harbour City adults who drink coffee at least once a week, recruited from an opt-in panel with quotas on age band. Each rated how much 8 needs matter when they choose coffee, from 1 (not at all important) to 7 (essential): taste and quality, a low price, convenience, sustainability, a strong caffeine kick, health (low in sugar and calories), buying from a local independent business, and new flavours. Module 1 showed that the ratings spread widely. Ana now wants to know whether the 640 fall into groups that want different things, and if so how many groups and who is in each.

OpenStax defines needs-based segmentation as dividing a market into smaller groups of people with approximately the same needs. When the groups are formed from survey answers rather than decided in advance, it is post hoc segmentation, or data-driven, as module 1 explained. The tool is cluster analysis: a family of methods that sort observations into groups, called clusters, whose members are more alike than members of different groups, judged by a measure of distance. This lesson forms the groups; Profiling segments describes, names and sizes them.

Caution: A cluster analysis always returns groups

Give k-means any data, even ratings drawn at random, and it returns the number of groups you asked for. The groups are a model of the market that you build, choose and check, not segments that were waiting to be discovered. Dolnicar, Grün and Leisch, whose open textbook on segmentation analysis this lesson draws on, report that natural segments, clearly separated in the data, are rare in consumer data.

Six decisions

Every needs-based cluster analysis rests on six decisions, and two analysts given the same data can reach different segments by making them differently. Write them down before you run anything, so that they can’t be chosen to flatter the result.

  1. Segmentation variables: which answers form the segments. Here, the eight needs; who people are and what they buy come later, to describe the segments.
  2. Scale: standardise each variable, so that each counts equally in the distances.
  3. Distance: how far apart two respondents are; k-means uses squared Euclidean distance.
  4. Algorithm: k-means, started well and restarted many times; Ward’s hierarchical method as a check.
  5. Number of segments: from the elbow, the silhouette and what the segments mean.
  6. Stability: whether the segments survive restarts, another method and another half of the sample.

Respondents as points

Think of each respondent as a point with one coordinate per need: 8 coordinates for Ana’s study, two in the small examples below. After each need is standardised to a z-score (B02’s Normalisation and standardisation), one unit on every axis means one standard deviation. The squared Euclidean distance between two respondents adds up the squared differences on every need: two people who differ by one standard deviation on all 8 needs are 8×12=88 \times 1^2 = 88×12=8 apart, and two who differ by two standard deviations on price alone are 22=42^2 = 422=4 apart. Respondents who are close want similar things; a segment is a group of respondents close to one another.

k-means in four moves

k-means clustering sorts respondents into k groups, where k is a number you choose, so as to make the total squared distance from respondents to their group’s centre as small as possible. Each group’s segment centre (its centroid) is the mean of its members on every variable. The method repeats two moves until nothing changes:

  1. Start: choose k starting centres (k-means++ chooses them well; see In depth).
  2. Assign: put every respondent with the nearest centre, by squared Euclidean distance.
  3. Update: move each centre to the mean of the respondents assigned to it.
  4. Repeat the assign and update moves until no respondent changes group.

The total it makes small is the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS): every respondent’s squared distance to their own centre, added up. Each assign move and each update move can only lower it or leave it unchanged, so k-means always stops. Pellmark Cycles’ eight customers, worked below, show the moves on a scale small enough to do by hand: from a poor start the WCSS falls from 82 to 21.07 after one iteration and to 8.75 after two, when one customer, G, switches group and nothing moves again.

Key idea: Ana chooses five segments

Two segments fit best by the silhouette, but they are one broad split: people who put price, convenience and caffeine first against people who care more about taste, sustainability, health, local businesses and new flavours. Five segments sit at the elbow and a local peak of the silhouette, and each differs from the others in a way Lantern could act on. Statistics narrowed the choice to a few values of k; what the segments mean decided it. The applied example below works the decision through and checks that the five segments are stable.

In depth

Choosing the segmentation variables

The segmentation variables, module 1’s segmentation base, are the answers that form the segments. For a needs-based segmentation they are the needs or benefits that drive the choice the business cares about, worded so that respondents can answer them, as Ana’s eight are. Leave out who people are (age, income, region) and what they do (cups, spend, where they buy): those describe the segments afterwards, and mixing them into the clustering produces groups that are part need, part demographic and hard to interpret or reach.

Three further rules keep the variables honest. Don’t include two items that ask the same thing (“a low price” and “inexpensive”): the need they share counts twice in every distance. Leave out items that almost everyone rates alike, which add noise but no separation. And keep the number of variables in proportion to the sample: Dolnicar, Grün and Leisch’s simulations led to a guideline of at least 100 respondents per segmentation variable (an earlier study had suggested 70).

By that guideline Ana’s 8 needs call for 800 respondents, and she has 640: enough by the older figure of 560, short by the newer one. That doesn’t make the study useless, but it is one more reason to check that the segments are stable before relying on them. Ratings carry one more trap: some people rate everything high and others everything low, a habit of answering rather than a need. Standardising each variable, the next step, doesn’t remove it; analysts sometimes centre each respondent’s ratings on that respondent’s own mean, at the cost of losing whether someone cares about coffee at all. Whatever the study does, a tool that later places new customers in the segments must transform their answers in exactly the same way.

Standardising first

Distances add squared differences, so a variable with a wide spread dominates them. Standardising each variable to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (B02) makes every need count equally. Ana’s needs share one 1–7 scale and similar spreads (standard deviations from 1.28 to 1.67), so here standardising changes little: k-means on the raw ratings gives almost the same five segments, with an adjusted Rand index (defined below) of 0.99 against the standardised ones.

It matters as soon as the scales differ. Add each respondent’s monthly coffee spend, in dollars, to the eight needs without standardising: spend’s variance (867.7) is 50 times the eight needs’ variances added together (17.2), 98.1% of the total. k-means then sorts people into spend bands ($6–32, $33–53, $54–78, $80–115 and $117–215 a month) whose agreement with the needs segments is 0.11, close to none. Its mean silhouette, 0.47, is higher than any needs solution’s, because one widely spread variable makes tidy bands: a silhouette can’t be compared across different variables or scalings. Standardised, spend becomes one variable in nine and the segments barely move (agreement 0.95), although it would still be a behaviour mixed into a needs segmentation.

Distance

k-means works with squared Euclidean distance because the mean is the point that makes the sum of squared distances to a group smallest: the update move is exactly the step that lowers the WCSS most for the current groups. Other distances exist (city-block distance adds absolute differences; others suit yes-or-no answers), and other algorithms use them, but k-means and Ward’s method as taught here are built on squared Euclidean distance. The silhouette, below, uses the ordinary Euclidean distance, the square root.

Local optima, k-means++ and restarts

k-means improves the solution it starts from, so it stops at a local optimum: a grouping that no single assign or update move can improve, though a different grouping would be better. Arthur and Vassilvitskii (2007) note that standard k-means is guaranteed only to find a local optimum, which can be quite poor. Their k-means++ start picks the first centre at random and each further centre with a probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest centre already chosen, so the starting centres spread out. Analysts then run k-means from many such starts, called restarts, and keep the solution with the lowest WCSS.

Restarts matter on Ana’s data. Of 100 runs from a single k-means++ start each at k = 5, 60 reached the best solution (WCSS 1,767.9); the other 40 stopped at local optima between 2,015.9 and 2,201.7, 14% to 25% worse, and the worst agreed with the best solution only 0.64 on the adjusted Rand index. Even 20 restarts are a heuristic: 200 find the same solution at k = 5 but slightly lower sums at k = 6, 7 and 8 (1,677.6, 1,596.4 and 1,526.0, less than 0.1% below the table). Report how many restarts you ran.

k-means always returns k groups

To see what no structure looks like, the lesson made up 640 respondents whose 8 ratings were each drawn at random from 1 to 7, independently, and ran the same analysis with 100 restarts. k-means still returned five segments, of 121 to 138 people. Their mean silhouette was 0.095, against 0.305 for Ana’s data, and the within-cluster sum of squares fell smoothly, by 345, 251, 211, 198, 163 and 144 from one k to the next, with no elbow.

Uniform ratings don’t keep each need’s own spread, but shuffling each need’s real answers independently, which does, gives much the same silhouette (0.10). A stricter benchmark keeps the needs’ correlations with one another but builds in no clusters: normal data with Ana’s correlation matrix give 0.14 to 0.15 in three draws. Benchmarks like these, data of the same size and scale with no clusters built in, are a quick way to read what a silhouette means for your data.

Ward’s hierarchical clustering and the dendrogram

Ward’s method builds segments from the bottom up. It starts with every respondent as a group of one and, at each step, merges the two groups whose merger increases the within-cluster sum of squares least, until one group remains (Ward, 1963, proposed merging to lose least by an objective the investigator chooses; the error sum of squares is the usual one). Merging groups A and B adds nAnBnA+nB\frac{n_A n_B}{n_A + n_B}nA​+nB​nA​nB​​ times the squared distance between their centres to the WCSS, so small groups with close centres merge first. The record of merges is drawn as a dendrogram, a tree whose branches join at the height of each merge; cutting it at any height gives that many segments.

Ward’s method needs no k in advance and shows every number of segments at once, but it never undoes a merge, so an early merge that later looks wrong stays, and it compares every pair of groups, which grows slow for tens of thousands of respondents. k-means needs k and a good start but can move respondents freely. The two are often used together: Ward’s tree to see which values of k look natural, k-means to refine the groups. On Ana’s data, Ward’s five groups and the k-means segments agree closely: 624 of 640 respondents, 97.5%, are in matching groups, and 16 differ.

Comparing two segmentations: the adjusted Rand index

Two segmentations of the same people can label their segments differently, so they are compared over pairs of respondents: does each pair sit together in both, or apart in both? The adjusted Rand index of Hubert and Arabie (1985) counts that agreement and corrects it for chance: 1 means identical groupings (whatever the labels), about 0 means no more agreement than random labels would give, and it can be negative. Ward against k-means on Ana’s data scores 0.93.

Choosing k: the elbow, the silhouette and meaning

The elbow method plots the within-cluster sum of squares against k and looks for the bend after which extra segments buy little. The bend is often soft, so read the falls, not only the picture. With standardised data the total sum of squares is (n−1)p=639×8=5,112(n - 1)p = 639 \times 8 = 5,112(n−1)p=639×8=5,112, so Ana’s five segments leave 65.4% of the variation in the needs between segments and 34.6% within them.

The silhouette of a respondent, proposed by Rousseeuw (1987), compares a, their average distance to the other members of their own segment, with b, their average distance to the members of the nearest other segment: s = (b − a) ÷ max(a, b). It runs from −1 to 1. Near 1, the respondent sits well inside their segment; near 0, between two segments; below 0, nearer another segment than their own. The mean silhouette over all respondents summarises the solution, and Rousseeuw suggested it might help select the number of clusters. A widely quoted rule of thumb from Kaufman and Rousseeuw’s book on cluster analysis (1990, as reproduced by Emamat and colleagues) reads a mean of 0.71 or more as strong structure, 0.51 to 0.70 as reasonable structure, 0.26 to 0.50 as weak structure that could be artificial, and 0.25 or less as no substantial structure.

Read the size of a silhouette, not only its peak. Ana’s 0.305 at k = 5 is about three times the 0.095 of random ratings and about twice the 0.14–0.15 of correlated data without clusters, so the segments are not an accident of the method; on Kaufman and Rousseeuw’s scale it is weak structure, far from the values near 1 of tight, well-separated groups. The segments overlap, and many respondents sit near a boundary. The silhouette often peaks at a small k when one split dominates the data, as here: at k = 2 it is highest, but the two groups are 312 people who put price (index 120 against the average respondent), convenience (119) and caffeine (118) first, and 328 who rate taste, sustainability, health, local and novelty above average: true, and too coarse to plan five different offers around.

Meaning settles it. At k = 4, most of segment D (local and taste first) and most of segment C (novelty first) merge into one group of 229, although a café that sells itself on Harbour City roots and a brand that launches new flavours every month are different propositions. At k = 6 (the best of 200 restarts), segment C divides between a group of 76 (74 of C’s members and 2 from D; novelty index 165, with taste and local above average) and one of 55 (novelty 148, every other need below average): the same need at two strengths, not a new one. Dolnicar, Grün and Leisch put it plainly: statistical criteria are no substitute for the judgement of the people who will use the segments.

Stability

A segmentation worth acting on should not depend on the luck of one run. Three checks are quick: whether many restarts reach the same best solution (60 of 100 single starts did), whether a different method finds similar groups (Ward against k-means: an adjusted Rand index of 0.93), and whether another half of the sample gives the same segments. For the last, Ana’s analyst segmented the first 320 respondents and the last 320 separately, assigned all 640 to each half’s nearest centres, and compared the two assignments: an adjusted Rand index of 0.97.

Dolnicar and Leisch go further, re-running the segmentation on many bootstrap samples (samples of the same size drawn with replacement) and measuring how often each segment reappears. They distinguish natural segmentation, when the data hold real clusters; reproducible segmentation, when the data have structure that gives the same solution again and again without clear clusters; and constructive segmentation, when the data have no structure and the analyst and the business choose among many artificial solutions. Ana’s segments look reproducible: stable across starts, methods and halves, but overlapping. Profiling segments adds a fourth check, assigning held-back respondents to the segments.

Note: What Ana couldn’t see

The case was generated with five kinds of coffee drinker planted in it, a truth no real analyst has. Against that truth, the five k-means segments score an adjusted Rand index of 0.94; two segments score 0.38, four 0.76 and six 0.88. Following the highest silhouette would have recovered the planted kinds far worse than following the elbow and the meaning. In real data there is no answer key, which is why the stability checks matter.

Common mistakes

  • “The clusters are the segments that exist in the market.”

    k-means returns k groups from any data: 640 made-up respondents with random ratings still gave five segments. Clusters are a model you choose and check. Report how clear the structure is (the silhouette against a benchmark) and how stable it is, and call the segments what they are: groups of people with similar answers, with overlapping edges.

  • “Choose the k with the lowest within-cluster sum of squares.”

    The WCSS falls every time k rises and reaches 0 when every respondent is a segment of one. Look for where the falls shrink (the elbow), not for the lowest value.

  • “The highest silhouette decides the number of segments.”

    The silhouette often peaks at a small k when one split dominates the data: Ana’s peaks at two, a split too coarse to plan offers around. It can’t be compared across different variables or scalings either (raw spend bands score 0.47). Use it with the elbow, stability and what the segments mean.

  • “One run of k-means gives the answer.”

    k-means stops at a local optimum that depends on its start: 40 of 100 single starts on Ana’s data stopped at worse solutions. Use k-means++ starts, run many restarts, keep the lowest WCSS and report how many you ran.

  • “Put every variable in the survey into the clustering.”

    Demographics and behaviour mixed with needs give segments that are part need, part demographic, and hard to interpret. Duplicated items double-count a need, and every extra variable needs more respondents (about 100 each, by Dolnicar, Grün and Leisch’s guideline). Segment on the needs; describe with the rest.

  • “Standardising is a formality.”

    It decides what the distances measure. On Ana’s 1–7 ratings it changes little, but add spend in dollars unstandardised and spend is 98% of the variance: the segments become spend bands that agree with the needs segments only 0.11.

In practice

Fictional case

Tollwick Software, a fictional firm that sells scheduling software to small businesses, wants to rebuild its onboarding around what different customers need. Its customer-insight lead, Teodora Rafferty, surveyed 1,150 business customers on 7 needs, from easy set-up to integration with accounting tools, each rated 1–7.

She writes the specification first. Before looking at any results, Teodora records the segmentation variables (the 7 needs, with firm size, sector and plan kept for describing), standardisation, k from 2 to 7, k-means with k-means++ starts and 50 restarts, Ward’s method as a check, and the stability tests. With 1,150 customers she is above the guideline of 100 a variable (700).

She shows the team three solutions, not one. The silhouette is highest at three segments (0.31), with four close behind (0.29) and five lower (0.24). At four, the largest of the three segments splits into customers who want integrations and customers who want reports, and the product team points out that those two need different onboarding paths. Her split-half check gives an adjusted Rand index of 0.91 for four segments and 0.62 for five, so five is not stable enough to plan around.

She reports the choice with its evidence and its limits. The team adopts four segments (362, 301, 274, 213 customers), and her one-page summary gives the specification, the elbow and silhouette for every k, the stability results and a sentence on what the segments are not: “four groups of customers with similar needs, overlapping at the edges, not four kinds of customer with nothing in common.” She plans to re-run the segmentation each year with the same specification, so that a change in the segments reflects a change in customers rather than in the method.

Ana’s analysis followed the same pattern on a smaller scale, and her next job is the one Teodora’s team faced after choosing: profile each segment, give it an honest name, size it in people and in spend, and build a short questionnaire that assigns new customers to a segment.

Summary

  • Needs-based (post hoc) segmentation forms segments from customers’ needs with cluster analysis; demographics and behaviour describe the segments afterwards.
  • Choose a few relevant, non-duplicated needs, with about 100 respondents per variable, and standardise them so each counts equally in the distances.
  • k-means assigns each respondent to the nearest centre and moves each centre to its members’ mean until nothing changes, lowering the within-cluster sum of squares at every step.
  • k-means stops at local optima, so use k-means++ starts and many restarts, and remember it returns k groups even from data with no structure.
  • Ward’s method merges the two groups that raise the WCSS least, shows every k in a dendrogram, and is a useful check on k-means.
  • Choose k from the elbow, the mean silhouette (s = (b − a) ÷ max(a, b)) and whether the segments mean something the business can act on; Lantern’s data give five.
  • Check stability with restarts, a second method and a split-half test, comparing groupings with the adjusted Rand index (1 identical, about 0 chance).

Key formulas

  • Squared Euclidean distance between two respondents
  • Segment centre (centroid)
  • Within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS)
  • Silhouette of a respondent, and the mean silhouette
  • Ward’s merge cost

Terms introduced

Cluster analysis
A family of methods that sort observations into groups (clusters) whose members are more alike than members of different groups, judged by a chosen measure of distance. In marketing it forms segments from survey answers.
k-means clustering
A cluster analysis that sorts observations into k groups by repeatedly assigning each to the nearest group centre and moving each centre to the mean of its members, until no observation changes group; it makes the within-cluster sum of squares small.
Segment centre
The mean of a segment’s members on every segmentation variable; also called the centroid. In k-means each observation belongs to the segment with the nearest centre.
Squared Euclidean distance
The sum of the squared differences between two observations on every variable, Σ(x_j − y_j)²; the square of the straight-line distance.
Within-cluster sum of squares
The sum of every observation’s squared distance to the centre of its own cluster (WCSS); k-means makes it small, and it always falls as the number of clusters rises.
Local optimum
A solution that no small step can improve although a better solution exists elsewhere; k-means stops at one that depends on its starting centres.
k-means++
A way of choosing k-means’ starting centres: the first at random, each further one with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest centre already chosen, so the starts spread out (Arthur and Vassilvitskii, 2007).
Elbow
The point on a plot of the within-cluster sum of squares against the number of clusters after which extra clusters reduce it only a little; one guide to the number of clusters.
Silhouette
For one observation, (b − a) ÷ max(a, b), where a is its average distance to the other members of its cluster and b its average distance to the nearest other cluster; from −1 to 1, near 1 when it sits well inside its cluster (Rousseeuw, 1987). The mean silhouette summarises a solution.
Ward’s method
Hierarchical clustering that starts with every observation alone and repeatedly merges the two groups whose merger increases the within-cluster sum of squares least.
Dendrogram
A tree diagram of a hierarchical clustering, in which branches join at the height of each merge; cutting it at a height gives that many clusters.
Adjusted Rand index
A measure of agreement between two groupings of the same observations, counted over pairs and corrected for chance: 1 for identical groupings, about 0 for chance agreement (Hubert and Arabie, 1985).
