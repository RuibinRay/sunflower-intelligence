The marketing mix and customer value

Marketing turns an idea into an offer that people choose over the alternatives. Learn what customer value is, how product, price, place and promotion each create or capture it, and how to test whether a plan’s four decisions fit each other and the customers they are meant for.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain what marketing is and what customer value means, in your own words and in the American Marketing Association’s.
  • Classify marketing decisions as product, price, place or promotion decisions.
  • Analyse whether the four decisions in a plan are consistent with each other and with the target market and positioning.
  • Calculate how a price and a channel together determine what a business keeps per unit, and what a customer gives up to buy.
  • Compare the 4Ps with the 4Cs and the 7Ps, and describe how the mix differs between business and consumer markets.

Review first

  • No marketing background is needed. You should be comfortable with percentages, for example finding 32% of $3.49.
  • Customer value, satisfaction and lifetime value — Helpful, not required: M01 explains customer value, which every decision in the mix has to create, and what a customer is worth over time.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern Coffee Co. began as Ana Lim’s catering and barista-training business, then opened an online shop for beans, and now also runs its own cafés. Its next step is a product it will sell to people it has never met: Lantern Cold Brew, a ready-to-drink cold brew in 250 ml cans, to be launched in Harbour City. About 4.8 million cans of ready-to-drink coffee are sold there each year, three established brands already share the shelf, and only 12% of the people who buy the category have heard of Lantern. Every name and figure in this case is invented for teaching.

This course follows that launch. Each lesson teaches one group of marketing decisions, applies it to Lantern Cold Brew and borrows examples from other industries for breadth. By the end you will have built a complete launch plan and tested it in the Marketing Mix Simulator. The figures come from the course’s fictional market file, and every worked number in the lessons is calculated from it, so you can check each one yourself.

What marketing is

Ask people what marketing is and most will say advertising. Advertising is part of it, but a small part. In plain language, marketing is the work of finding out what a group of customers needs, creating an offer they value more than the alternatives, making it easy to get, telling them about it, and capturing enough of the value created to keep the business going.

The American Marketing Association (AMA), a professional association for marketers and marketing academics, maintains a formal definition. A panel of five marketing scholars reviews the AMA’s definitions regularly; the panel listed beside the current wording is the 2017 panel.

Marketing is the activity, set of institutions, and processes for creating, communicating, delivering, and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and society at large.

American Marketing Association, “Definitions of Marketing”

Three parts of that sentence are worth slowing down for. It speaks of offerings, not just physical goods: a workshop, a hotel stay and an app are offerings too. It names four activities, creating, communicating, delivering and exchanging, which you can read as a preview of this course: a business creates value with its product, communicates it with promotion, delivers it through distribution and exchanges it at a price. And it says the value must reach customers, partners and society. A launch that delights shoppers but loses money for the shops that stock it, or misleads people about what is in the can, fails the definition.

Customer value

Customers don’t buy products; they buy what products do for them, at a cost. Customer value is the customer’s judgement of the benefits an offer brings compared with everything it costs them to get and use it, set against the alternatives they could choose instead. Each part of that definition does work.

  • Benefits can be functional (cold coffee, ready to drink), emotional (a small treat on a hot afternoon) or social (being seen with a brand that says something about you).
  • Costs are more than the price. They include time (a detour to a café), effort (cold brew made at home has to steep overnight) and risk (paying for a brand you have never tasted, which may disappoint).
  • Judgement means it is the customer’s view that counts, not the producer’s. Lantern’s 20-hour steep is a benefit only if shoppers notice it and care.
  • Alternatives mean value is always relative. The comparison may be a competitor’s can, a café cold brew, coffee made at home or buying nothing at all.

A business can therefore raise customer value in two ways: increase the benefits the customer perceives, or reduce the total cost of getting them, which is not always the same as cutting the price. Putting cans in the chiller at a commuter’s station adds value by saving time. A money-back promise adds value by removing risk. Neither changes the price.

Who the mix is for: target market and positioning

Before any of Lantern’s launch decisions can be judged, two questions need answers. Who is the product for? The target market is the group of customers a business chooses to serve, described by their needs and behaviour as well as by who they are. And what should those customers think of it? Positioning is the place a business wants its offer to occupy in target customers’ minds, relative to the competing offers. The promise that gives those customers a reason to choose it is the value proposition: the benefits they get, why those benefits matter to them and why this offer delivers them better than the alternatives.

Fictional case

For this course Lantern starts from a working answer. Target market: Harbour City commuters and office workers who already buy coffee when they are out, care how it tastes and want something cold and quick. Positioning: café-quality single-origin cold brew in a can, priced below a café drink and close to the premium brand on the shelf. Value proposition: “The cold brew our cafés serve, ready when you are.” These are starting assumptions, not facts. The course on segmentation and positioning (M05) shows how to choose and test them.

Target market and positioning are not part of the mix; they are what the mix is for. Every decision in the rest of this lesson is judged against them.

The marketing mix

The marketing mix is the set of decisions a business controls and combines to create, communicate, deliver and capture value for its target market. The best-known way to organise those decisions is the 4Ps: product, price, place and promotion. “Mix” is the important word. Like ingredients in a recipe, the decisions work only in combination, and the right amount of one depends on the others.

In depth

Where the idea came from

The phrase comes from Harvard Business School in the 1940s. James Culliton, studying manufacturers’ marketing costs in a 1948 report, described the business executive as a “mixer of ingredients” who sometimes follows a recipe, sometimes adapts one and sometimes invents a new one. His colleague Neil Borden took up the idea. By his own account Borden used the term “marketing mix” in his teaching from the late 1940s; he spoke about it in his 1953 address as president of the American Marketing Association and set it out in an article in 1964.

Borden’s list had twelve elements for a manufacturer: product planning, pricing, branding, channels of distribution, personal selling, advertising, promotions, packaging, display, servicing, physical handling, and fact finding and analysis. He paired it with the market forces the mix has to respond to: consumers, the trade (wholesalers and retailers), competitors and government. He was modest about the idea. He did not present the mix as a scientific theory but as a checklist to guide judgement, and he expected the building of a mix to remain largely an art.

In 1960 E. Jerome McCarthy, in his textbook Basic Marketing: A Managerial Approach, grouped the decisions under four headings organised around a target market: product, price, place and promotion. Four Ps were easier to remember and to teach than twelve elements, and they became the standard structure of introductory marketing courses, this one included. The table shows one way Borden’s elements fit under McCarthy’s headings.

The four decisions, applied to Lantern Cold Brew

Product is what the customer gets: the goods, services and experiences on offer, with their features, quality, branding and packaging. For Lantern Cold Brew the main product decisions are the liquid and the range. The market file offers three liquids, called tiers: a Classic blend (taste score 6, costing $0.58 a can to make, fill and deliver to the distributor), a Single-origin Ethiopian (7.5, $0.74) and an Organic single-origin (8.5, $0.88). Lantern can launch one flavour, Original, or three: Original, Oat Milk Latte and Vanilla. The 250 ml can matches the rest of the category. Product is where most of the value is created, because it decides which benefits a customer can get at all. Lessons 2 and 3 cover it.

Price is what the customer gives up in money, and the terms on which they pay. It is the only P that brings in revenue directly; the other three mainly cost money. Competitors’ cans sell for $2.29 to $3.79, and the simulator’s default plan charges $3.49. Price does two jobs at once. It captures value: what Lantern keeps depends on it. And it communicates: shoppers who can’t taste a can before buying it read the price as a clue to quality, so a price out of line with the positioning undermines it. Module 2 covers pricing.

Place covers how the product reaches the customer: which channels sell it, how widely it is available and the logistics behind it. Lantern can sell through supermarkets, convenience stores, natural and specialty grocers, and its own cafés and online shop, and each channel changes both who can buy and what Lantern keeps. Supermarkets sell 45% of the category’s cans; there the distributor and retailer together keep 32% of the shelf price and charge $9,000 a year for each flavour stocked. In its own cafés and online shop Lantern keeps the whole price but pays $0.45 a can for packing and delivery, and reaches only its own customers. Module 3 covers place.

Promotion is how the business communicates with its target market and persuades it to buy: advertising, sales promotions, personal selling, public relations, and direct and digital marketing. Lantern’s first problem is attention. Only 12% of category buyers know its name, against 50% to 75% for the three established brands. The market file offers four promotion vehicles: social and search ads to build awareness, in-store sampling and displays to turn awareness into trial, trade promotions that fund temporary price cuts in stores, and PR and events. The default plan spends $120,000 a year across them. Module 4 covers promotion.

Key idea: How the four Ps divide the work

Product creates the benefits. Price captures value for the business and signals what to expect. Place makes the product available where and when the target customer wants it. Promotion makes sure the target customer knows about it, understands it and wants it. Customer value depends on all four: a great product nobody can find, or nobody has heard of, creates none.

Fit: the four decisions must agree

A mix is judged less by its individual decisions than by how well they fit together. Fit has two sides. Internal consistency means the four Ps send the same message and support each other. External fit means the mix suits the target market and positioning, stands up against competitors and is within what the business can afford and deliver.

Consider a plan that looks sensible decision by decision: the Single-origin liquid, because it tastes better than Arcadia and Daily Grind; a price of $2.29, matching Daily Grind, to win volume; and convenience stores only, because commuters buy there. Each choice has a reason. Together they contradict each other.

  • Product and price disagree. A taste score of 7.5 says premium; a $2.29 price says value brand, and a shopper who can’t taste the can will believe the price.
  • Price and place disagree. Convenience stores keep 40% of the shelf price, the highest share of any channel, so a low price leaves Lantern little: $2.29 × 0.60 = $1.374 a can, or about $0.63 after the $0.74 cost of the can and its contents. At $3.49 in the same stores Lantern would keep $1.354 a can after costs, more than twice as much. And the market file notes that convenience shoppers compare prices less than supermarket shoppers, so the low price gives money away where it is least needed.
  • Place and promotion disagree. A premium story (Ethiopian origin, a 20-hour steep) needs telling, but a convenience-store chiller is where people grab a familiar brand on impulse. With no tastings or displays, nothing at the shelf explains why this can is different.
  • The whole mix disagrees with the positioning. “Café quality, below café price” has become “a cheap can with an expensive liquid inside”. It confuses the target customer and makes poor money for Lantern.

There is more than one consistent alternative. A premium mix keeps the Single-origin (or Organic) liquid, prices close to Summit, sells where quality-minded shoppers buy (natural grocers, supermarkets and Lantern’s own cafés) and spends on sampling so people taste the difference. A value mix switches to the Classic blend, prices near Arcadia, chases volume in supermarkets and convenience stores and leans on trade promotions. Both could work, for different target customers. What can’t work is half of each.

Caution: Consistent is not the same as correct

A mix can be perfectly consistent and still fail: the target market may be too small, a competitor may already own the position, or the costs may not add up. Fit is necessary, not sufficient. Later lessons add the numbers that tell you whether a consistent mix also pays.

Criticisms and alternatives

The 4Ps are a way of sorting decisions, not a tested theory, and they have well-known weaknesses. Use them knowing what they leave out.

The first criticism is that the Ps describe the market from the seller’s side of the counter. In 1990 Robert Lauterborn, writing in Advertising Age, proposed four Cs seen from the buyer’s side instead. The 4Cs are consumer wants and needs (in place of product), cost to satisfy (in place of price), convenience to buy (in place of place) and communication (in place of promotion).

Turning the 4Ps round: the questions the 4Cs make Lantern ask
Seller’s view (4Ps)Buyer’s view (4Cs)The question for Lantern
ProductConsumer wants and needsWhat does a commuter want from a cold coffee, and does our can deliver it?
PriceCost to satisfyWhat does buying our can cost in money, time and risk, compared with a café or making it at home?
PlaceConvenience to buyIs a chilled can within reach at the moment of thirst?
PromotionCommunicationAre we listening to what buyers tell us, as well as talking to them?

The 4Cs don’t add new decisions: Lantern still has to choose a liquid, a price, channels and a budget. Their value is the change of viewpoint, which is why this course uses the 4Cs as a check on a 4P plan rather than as a replacement for it.

The second criticism is that the boundaries between the Ps are fuzzy. Packaging is part of the product, but on the shelf it is also promotion. A trade promotion is paid from the promotion budget but works by cutting the price shoppers pay. Customer service could be product or place. Where a decision is filed matters less than making sure no decision falls between the headings.

The third criticism is that the mix was built for mass-marketed consumer goods bought in single transactions. Christian Grönroos (1994) argued that treating marketing as the management of four Ps had become a “straitjacket” that encouraged toolbox thinking, and that in services and business markets the work that matters most is building and managing long-term relationships with customers, not tuning the mix for each sale.

Services raise a related problem. A hotel stay or a training course can’t be inspected before it is bought, is produced while the customer is there and depends on the people who deliver it. Bernard Booms and Mary Jo Bitner (1981) proposed adding three Ps for services: people, process and physical evidence. The resulting 7Ps are the subject of course M03; in this course services appear only as examples.

Business markets and consumer markets

The mix applies whether the customer is a household (business-to-consumer, or B2C) or an organisation (business-to-business, or B2B), but the decisions look different. Compare Lantern, selling cans to shoppers, with a fictional company, Ridgeway Energy Storage, which designs and installs battery-storage systems for warehouses and factories.

The same four decisions in a consumer market and a business market (both businesses fictional)
DecisionLantern Cold Brew (B2C)Ridgeway Energy Storage (B2B)
ProductA standard can, the same for everyoneA system designed for each site, with installation, monitoring and a performance warranty
PriceOne shelf price, paid on the spotA quoted, often negotiated price for each project; buyers compare costs over the system’s life
PlaceThrough distributors and retailers to thousands of shoppersSold and installed directly for a few dozen large customers
PromotionMass awareness and sampling at the shelfPersonal selling, technical proposals, case studies and site visits
Who decidesOne shopper, in secondsSeveral people (facilities, finance, operations), over months

Two lessons follow. First, in business markets the services around the core item (design, installation, maintenance, warranties) and the relationship itself often matter as much as the item, which is part of Grönroos’s argument. Second, Lantern is in a business market too. Before any shopper can buy a can, a distributor and a retailer’s buyer must agree to stock it, and they judge Lantern’s mix by what it is likely to earn them. The mix has to create value for them as well, just as the AMA definition says.

Common mistakes

  • “Marketing is advertising.”

    Advertising is one tool within one of the four Ps. Marketing starts earlier, with choosing which customers to serve and designing what to offer them, and it includes price and distribution. A launch with brilliant advertising and a can nobody can find in the shops fails; so does a great can that nobody has heard of.

  • “Customer value means a low price.”

    Value is benefits relative to total cost, judged against the alternatives. A $3.49 can can be better value than a $2.29 one for a shopper who cares about taste, and a café cold brew can be better value than either for someone who wants to sit down. Cutting the price is one way to raise value, and usually the most expensive one for the seller.

  • “Each P can be decided on its own.”

    The Ps interact. The channel changes what a price earns, the price changes what the product seems to be, and promotion works only where the product can be bought. Check every pair of decisions, as in the premium-liquid-at-a-value-price example, and use numbers where you can.

  • “If every decision is sensible, the mix is sensible.”

    A mix can be built from individually reasonable choices that contradict each other, because each came from a different idea of who the customer is. Judge each decision against the positioning and against the other three, not on its own merits.

  • “The 4Ps are the complete, scientific list of marketing decisions.”

    They are a memorable way to sort decisions. Borden saw the mix as a guide to judgement rather than a science; services need people, process and physical evidence as well; and the 4Cs remind you to look from the buyer’s side of the counter.

  • “The customer is the person who drinks the coffee.”

    Lantern has to win two kinds of customer: the shoppers, and the distributors and retailers who decide whether to stock the can at all. A mix that ignores what the trade earns never reaches the shelf.

In practice

Marketing managers use the mix most often as a consistency check on a plan before money is committed. A launch review at a consumer-goods company typically fits on one page. It states the target market and the positioning in a sentence each, lists the four decisions and then works through three sets of questions.

  1. Does each decision fit the positioning? For Lantern’s premium plan: is a taste score of 7.5 noticeably better than Arcadia’s 6, and is $3.49 believable beside Summit’s $3.79?
  2. Does each pair of decisions fit? Four decisions make six pairs. Price and place are checked with numbers: at $3.49 Lantern receives $2.37 from a supermarket sale ($3.49 × 0.68) and $3.04 from a sale in its own cafés or online shop ($3.49 − $0.45), in both cases before the $0.74 cost of the can and its contents. Place and promotion are checked for practicality: sampling needs shops that allow tastings.
  3. Does it work from the customer’s side? The 4Cs turn each P round: what does a commuter actually want, what does buying the can cost them, how convenient is it to buy, and is the conversation two-way?

The same review works far from coffee. Suppose an airline (a fictional one) adds a premium economy cabin. The seat must be clearly better than economy and clearly below business class, the fare must sit between the two, it must be sold through the channels its business travellers already use, and the message must justify the gap. If the seat is barely better than economy, no amount of promotion will hold the fare; if the fare is too close to business class, the new cabin pulls customers down from the more profitable one. A university launching an online master’s programme for working professionals faces the same test: do the programme’s design, its fee, its online delivery and the way it is advertised all speak to the same person?

Managers also know which decisions are cheap to change. A promotion budget can be moved within weeks; a price can be changed, although retailers may resist; a new channel takes months to negotiate; and a product change can take a year. That is why product and channel decisions are usually settled first and promotion is tuned last. It is also why the simulator is useful: it shows in seconds the kind of interaction between decisions that a real launch would take a year to reveal, as long as you remember that its numbers come from a model.

Summary

  • Marketing is creating, communicating, delivering and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, partners and society, while capturing enough value to keep the business going.
  • Customer value is the customer’s judgement of benefits relative to total cost (money, time, effort and risk), compared with the next-best alternative.
  • The target market and positioning come first: they are what the mix is designed to serve.
  • The marketing mix is the set of controllable decisions combined to deliver that value. McCarthy’s 4Ps (1960) organise them as product, price, place and promotion, simplifying Borden’s twelve elements.
  • A good mix is consistent: each decision fits the positioning and every other decision. Check pairs with numbers where you can, such as what a price earns in each channel.
  • The 4Ps are a framework, not a law. The 4Cs look from the buyer’s side, services need the 7Ps, and business markets put more weight on relationships, services and lifetime cost.

Terms introduced

Marketing
The activity of creating, communicating, delivering and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners and society (a paraphrase of the American Marketing Association’s definition).
Customer value
The customer’s judgement of the benefits of an offer relative to its total cost in money, time, effort and risk, compared with the alternatives.
Target market
The group of customers a business chooses to serve with a particular offer.
Positioning
The place a business wants its offer to occupy in target customers’ minds, relative to competing offers.
Value proposition
The promise of benefits that gives target customers a reason to choose an offer over the alternatives.
Marketing mix
The set of controllable decisions a business combines to create, communicate, deliver and capture value for its target market.
4Ps
Product, price, place and promotion: the four headings E. Jerome McCarthy (1960) used to organise the marketing mix.
4Cs
Robert Lauterborn’s (1990) buyer’s-side alternative to the 4Ps: consumer wants and needs, cost to satisfy, convenience to buy, and communication.
7Ps
The 4Ps plus people, process and physical evidence, proposed by Booms and Bitner (1981) for marketing services.
