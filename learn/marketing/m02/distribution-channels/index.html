Distribution channels and decisions

A product sells only where shoppers can find it. Learn what intermediaries do, how channels are structured, how much reach costs in margin and fees, and how to choose channels that fit the product, its price and its positioning.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain the functions intermediaries perform and why removing an intermediary does not remove its work.
  • Describe channel levels and distinguish direct, indirect, multichannel and omnichannel distribution.
  • Choose a distribution intensity (intensive, selective or exclusive) that fits a product’s class and positioning.
  • Calculate a price build-up from shelf price to net price and gross margin per unit, including trade margins, fulfilment costs and listing fees.
  • Identify horizontal and vertical channel conflict and explain where channel power comes from.
  • Evaluate channel options for a launch by comparing reach, margin per unit, fixed fees and fit with the positioning.

Review first

  • Price, demand and pricing tactics — You need to know how shoppers respond to price, because each channel changes the price they see and the price Lantern keeps.
  • Pricing approaches — The price build-up uses contribution per unit, fixed costs and the difference between margin and markup.
  • Working with percentages: finding a percentage of an amount and dividing by (1 − a percentage).

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern Coffee Co. has its product: a 250 ml can of single-origin cold brew, planned to sell for $3.49. Now it needs shelves. Harbour City’s shoppers buy about 4.8 million cans of ready-to-drink coffee a year, and almost all of them are bought somewhere Lantern doesn’t control: 45% in supermarkets, 30% in convenience stores and 15% in natural and specialty grocers. Only the last 10% are bought through the kind of outlet Lantern already runs, its own cafés and online shop. Ana Lim, Lantern’s founder, can’t drive cans to hundreds of stores, negotiate with each store’s buyer and chase each invoice herself. She needs other businesses to do that, and they will want to be paid.

That is the place decision in the marketing mix: how the product gets from the producer to the customer, and through whom. It matters as much as the product and the price, because a can that isn’t on the shelf can’t be bought, however good it is. It also differs from the other decisions in one important way. A price can change next week; a channel is a set of relationships and contracts that takes months to build and is costly to undo.

What a marketing channel is

A marketing channel (also called a distribution channel) is the set of organisations that work together to make a product available to the people who use it. The businesses in the middle of the channel are intermediaries: firms that buy, sell, store, move or promote the product between the producer and the final customer. The main kinds are wholesalers, which buy in bulk and resell to retailers and other businesses; distributors, which do the same but usually work closely with the producers they represent, often holding stock and selling the product to stores on their behalf; retailers, which sell to final consumers; and the agent or broker, which arranges sales for a commission or fee without ever owning the goods. The words overlap in practice: in many industries “wholesaler” and “distributor” are used for the same kind of firm.

Why intermediaries exist

Intermediaries survive because they make the whole system cheaper. Imagine 20 drinks brands selling to 400 stores. If every brand dealt with every store, the market would need 20×400=8,00020 \times 400 = 8{,}00020×400=8,000 separate relationships: 8,000 sets of negotiations, deliveries and invoices. If all of them went through one distributor, it would need 20+400=42020 + 400 = 42020+400=420. Each relationship is larger, so it is worth doing well, and the distributor can put Lantern’s cans on the same truck as everyone else’s.

Intermediaries also close the gap between what producers make and what customers want. A producer makes a narrow range in large quantities; a shopper wants one can, today, chosen from many brands. Somebody has to break the pallets into cases and the cases into single cans, and assemble the assortment the shopper sees on the shelf.

The work intermediaries do can be grouped into three families of functions. Transactional functions are buying, selling and bearing risk: a distributor that buys Lantern’s cans owns them, and loses money if they don’t sell or are damaged. Logistical functions are handling, storing, keeping inventory and transporting the goods, and breaking large quantities into small ones. Facilitating functions are financing (a retailer might pay 30 days after delivery, so Lantern is lending it money in the meantime, or a distributor pays Lantern before the shops pay it) and information: intermediaries see what sells, where and at what price, and they pass on what customers say.

Key idea: You can remove an intermediary, not its functions

If Lantern sells a can through its own online shop, nobody takes a trade margin, but somebody still has to store the stock, pick and pack the order, deliver it, take the payment and answer the customer who says the box arrived dented. In Lantern’s figures that work costs $0.45 a can plus $6,000 a year to run the shop. Cutting out the middle of a channel moves its costs; it doesn’t make them vanish.

Channel levels

Channels are described by their length. Each layer of intermediaries between the producer and the final customer is one channel level. A direct channel has no intermediary at all: a zero-level channel, such as Lantern selling through its cafés and online shop. Every other channel is an indirect channel. In a one-level channel a retailer buys from the producer and sells to the consumer; in a two-level channel a distributor or wholesaler sits between the producer and the retailer; a three-level channel adds another layer, for example an agent who represents the producer to distributors in a new region.

Longer is not worse. Each level is a business that performs some of the functions above and charges for them. A longer channel suits products that are cheap per unit, standardised and bought in many places, like canned drinks; a shorter one suits products that are expensive, customised or technical. A company installing home battery-storage systems, for example, usually sells and installs directly or through a few trained installers, because every sale needs a site survey and after-sales support that a general retailer can’t give.

In depth

Direct, indirect, multichannel and omnichannel

Selling direct to consumers, often called direct-to-consumer or DTC, gives the producer the whole price, full control over how the product is presented, and data on every customer. It also gives the producer every function to perform and only the customers who come looking. Indirect channels give reach the producer could never build alone, at the price of a share of every sale and less control over the shelf.

Most producers do both. Multichannel distribution means selling through more than one channel at once: Lantern’s first plan uses supermarkets, natural grocers and its own cafés and online shop. Omnichannel goes further: the channels are joined up from the customer’s side, so that prices, promotions, stock information and loyalty rewards are consistent wherever the customer meets the brand, and the customer can move between channels, for example ordering online and collecting a case at a café. Verhoef, Kannan and Inman (2015) describe this shift as retailers taking a broader view of channels, managing them together rather than as separate businesses. Omnichannel needs shared systems and data, so it costs money; for a canned drink the practical omnichannel questions are mostly about consistent prices and joined-up customer data.

Distribution intensity

The second big decision is how many of the outlets that would stock the product to use. With intensive distribution the product is placed in as many outlets as possible; with selective distribution it goes to more than one but fewer than all of the outlets that would take it; with exclusive distribution only one or a very few outlets in each area may sell it.

The table follows a classic link with product classes. Convenience products, bought often and with little effort, need intensive distribution; shopping products, compared on quality and price before purchase, suit selective distribution; speciality products, which buyers will make a special effort to get, can be sold exclusively. But positioning can override the product class. A premium brand may deliberately stay out of discount outlets even though its category is sold everywhere, to protect its image and the margins of the stores that do carry it.

Intensity also changes over a product’s life. A new brand often starts selectively, in outlets where its likely buyers shop and where it can afford the fees and the in-store support, and widens distribution once it has shown that it sells. A ready-to-drink coffee is bought like a convenience product, which argues for wide availability in the long run; Lantern’s premium single-origin positioning, its 12% awareness and its limited budget argue for a selective start.

Channel economics: trade margins and the price build-up

Intermediaries are paid out of the price the shopper pays. The trade margin is the share of the shelf price that the intermediaries keep: in Lantern’s market file it is the distributor’s and the retailer’s share together. What is left is Lantern’s net price, the amount it actually receives per can. A price build-up traces the price from the producer to the shelf, or back from the shelf to the producer, showing who keeps what at each level.

The same shelf price earns Lantern very different amounts. A can sold in a convenience store leaves Lantern $1.35; the same can sold through its own online shop leaves $2.30, but only to a customer who already knows Lantern well enough to order online. This is the central trade-off of place: reach costs margin. The channels that put the product in front of the most shoppers are the ones that take the largest share, because they provide the most valuable thing a new brand lacks, which is access to people who aren’t yet looking for it.

Listing and slotting fees

Shelf space is scarce, and a retailer that gives a new product a slot takes the risk that it won’t sell. Retailers therefore charge suppliers for access. A slotting fee (or slotting allowance) is a payment a supplier makes to a retailer to have a new product placed on its shelves or in its warehouse. The US Federal Trade Commission, studying grocery retailers in 2003, described slotting allowances as one-time payments made as a condition of initial placement, and found that both how often they were paid and how much varied widely across product categories. Retailers also charge for continuing support such as displays and promotional space.

Lantern’s market file simplifies all of this into one yearly listing fee per SKU per retail channel, covering slotting, listing and merchandising. Because the fee is per SKU, every flavour pays it separately.

Three flavours raise it to 16,532 cans before the extra production cost, and those cans have to come from shoppers who would otherwise have bought Summit Brew, Daily Grind or Arcadia, which already have the shelf and far higher awareness.

Channel conflict and channel power

The members of a channel share an interest in selling the product but disagree about how the money is divided. Channel conflict is disagreement between channel members that gets in the way of the channel working. Vertical conflict is between levels: if Lantern’s online shop sold a 12-pack at a price that worked out at $2.99 a can, supermarkets charging $3.49 would see Lantern competing with its own retailers, and might give the can a worse slot or drop it. Horizontal conflict is between members at the same level: if one supermarket chain cut Lantern to $2.79 in a price promotion, a rival chain might demand the same support or refuse to promote the brand.

How conflicts end depends on channel power. A large retailer controls access to its shoppers, and its shelf is a scarce resource every brand wants; a strong brand has shoppers who will ask for it or switch stores to find it, so retailers need it as much as it needs them. Distributors have power from their relationships and trucks. The member that coordinates the channel, sets terms and resolves disputes is sometimes called the channel captain. A new brand with 12% awareness has almost no power: it accepts the retailer’s terms and earns influence by proving that its product sells. Producers reduce conflict with clear and consistent pricing across channels, by giving channels different jobs or products (singles in stores, multipacks and subscriptions online) and by treating retail partners’ margins as part of their own plan, not as a leak.

Logistics in proportion

Behind every channel is logistics: planning and controlling the flow of goods, from the co-packer to the shopper. Lantern’s cans are shelf-stable (a fact of this fictional case), so they don’t need refrigerated trucks or warehouses; convenience stores chill them for sale. Three logistics decisions matter at launch. Inventory: enough stock to avoid empty shelves without tying up cash in cans that sit for months. Demand is seasonal: the category sells about 1,500,000 cans in the third quarter and 1,020,000 in the first, so stock has to be built before summer. Storage: in the retail channels the distributor’s warehouse holds stock; for the online shop Lantern must hold it itself or pay a fulfilment firm. Transport: the distributor’s trucks deliver to stores along with other brands; online orders go by parcel, which is why each can sold direct costs $0.45 to fulfil. An empty shelf loses more than one sale: it tells the retailer the brand can’t be relied on.

Common mistakes

  • “A 32% trade margin means the store adds 32% to Lantern’s price.”

    The margin is a share of the shelf price. To find the shelf price for a target net price, divide by (1 − margin): $2.37 ÷ 0.68 ≈ $3.49. Multiplying by 1.32 gives about $3.13, and at that price the trade would keep only 24% of the shelf price.

  • “Margins along the chain add up.”

    When each level quotes a margin on its own selling price, the margins compound. A 20% retail margin and a 15% distributor margin take 1 − 0.80 × 0.85 = 32% of the shelf price, not 35%. Work level by level, or use the combined-margin formula.

  • “Selling direct means keeping the whole price.”

    The intermediary disappears but its work doesn’t: storage, packing, delivery, payments and customer service. Lantern’s online shop costs $0.45 a can and $6,000 a year, and it reaches only people who already know Lantern. Direct selling raises margin per unit and lowers reach.

  • “More channels always mean more profit.”

    Each channel adds units, but also fees, lower margins in some channels, thinner in-store support and a risk of channel conflict. Lantern adding convenience stores would add $4,000 of fees per flavour, the lowest margin per can and a third less sampling per store. Compare contribution, not units.

  • “Listing fees are a launch cost you pay once.”

    In this course’s market the listing fee is charged every year, for every SKU, in every retail channel, so a three-flavour range pays three times. Real-world slotting allowances are often one-off payments for initial placement, but retailers also charge for continuing display and promotional support. Always ask what the fee buys and for how long.

In practice

Channel decisions are made by people with spreadsheets and appointments. Lantern’s sales manager would build a comparison like the one below before meeting any retail buyer, because the buyer will ask how the brand plans to support the product in store, and the answer depends on how much margin each channel leaves for that support.

Read across the rows and the logic of a selective launch appears. Supermarkets are the price of being a real brand: almost half the category is bought there, and the gross margin per can is the best of the retail channels. Natural grocers cost little to enter and their shoppers value exactly what a single-origin can offers. The direct channel earns the most per can and gives Lantern its customer data, so it pays to keep it even though it is small, as long as its prices don’t undercut the stores. Convenience stores are the closest call: the simulator says they add profit even in year 1, and the case for holding them back, or piloting them in one chain, rests on the execution risks the model leaves out (see the applied example).

The same reasoning runs through other industries. A city hotel that sells a $200 room through an online travel agency charging a hypothetical 18% commission keeps $164; selling the room on its own website, after a hypothetical $12 of payment and marketing costs, it keeps $188. Hotels still use the agencies, because the agencies bring guests the hotel could not reach on its own, but they work hard to win repeat guests back to the direct channel. Airlines face the same choice between their own websites and travel agents, and a clothing brand faces it between wholesaling to boutiques and selling online. In each case the professional question is the same: what does each channel deliver that the others can’t, and what does it cost per unit and per year?

Note: What the professional watches after launch

Once the can is on shelf, the number that keeps it there is sales per store per week. Retail buyers review slow sellers regularly, and a listing that doesn’t earn its space can be removed. That is why channel, promotion and logistics plans are made together: a listing without in-store support and reliable deliveries is a fee paid for nothing.

Summary

  • A marketing channel is the chain of organisations that makes a product available; intermediaries exist because they cut the number of transactions and perform transactional, logistical and facilitating functions more cheaply than producers can alone.
  • Removing an intermediary moves its functions and costs to someone else; it doesn’t remove them. Lantern’s direct channel pays $0.45 a can and $6,000 a year in fulfilment.
  • Channels range from zero-level (direct) to three-level and beyond; multichannel firms use several, and omnichannel firms join them up from the customer’s side.
  • Distribution intensity (intensive, selective, exclusive) should fit both the product class and the positioning; new brands often start selective and widen later.
  • Net price = shelf price × (1 − trade margin); gross margin per unit = net price − cost of goods. At $3.49 Lantern keeps between $1.35 (convenience) and $2.30 (direct) a can: reach costs margin.
  • Trade margins are quoted on selling prices and compound along a chain; to price back from a target, divide by (1 − margin) at each level.
  • Listing and slotting fees are fixed costs per SKU; divide them by gross margin per unit to see the volume each channel must deliver.
  • Channel conflict can be vertical (between levels) or horizontal (within a level); power comes from controlling access to shoppers or from shoppers’ demand for the brand.

Terms introduced

Marketing channel
The set of organisations that work together to make a product available to the people who use it; also called a distribution channel.
Intermediary
A business between the producer and the final customer that buys, sells, stores, moves or promotes the product.
Wholesaler
An intermediary that buys goods in bulk and resells them to retailers and other businesses rather than to consumers.
Distributor
A wholesaler that works closely with the producers it represents, usually holding their stock and selling and delivering it to stores. The terms are often used interchangeably.
Retailer
An intermediary that sells goods to final consumers.
Agent or broker
An intermediary that arranges sales for a producer in return for a commission or fee without taking ownership of the goods.
Channel level
One layer of intermediaries between the producer and the final customer. A direct channel has zero levels.
Direct channel
A channel with no intermediaries: the producer sells to the final customer itself, for example through its own shops or website.
Indirect channel
A channel that uses one or more intermediaries between the producer and the final customer.
Multichannel distribution
Selling the same product through more than one channel at the same time.
Omnichannel
Managing several channels as one system from the customer’s point of view, with consistent prices, promotions, stock information and service across them.
Intensive distribution
Placing a product in as many outlets as possible.
Selective distribution
Placing a product in more than one but fewer than all of the outlets that would stock it, chosen for their fit with the product.
Exclusive distribution
Allowing only one or a very few outlets in each area to sell a product.
Trade margin
The share of the final selling price kept by the intermediaries, usually quoted as a percentage of that price.
Price build-up
A calculation that traces a product’s price through each level of the channel, from what the producer receives to what the final customer pays.
Slotting fee
A payment from a supplier to a retailer to have a new product placed on its shelves or in its warehouse; also called a slotting allowance. Listing fees are similar charges for being stocked.
Channel conflict
Disagreement between members of a marketing channel that gets in the way of the channel working. Vertical conflict is between levels; horizontal conflict is between members at the same level.
Logistics
Planning and controlling the physical flow of goods, including inventory, storage and transport, from the producer to the customer.
