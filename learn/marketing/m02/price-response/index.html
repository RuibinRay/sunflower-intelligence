Price, demand and pricing tactics

How much do sales change when the price changes, and does the change pay? Measure price elasticity, predict what happens to revenue and to contribution, choose a launch price strategy, and use psychological, promotional, bundled and segmented prices within their evidence and their legal limits.

About 95 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate price elasticity of demand with the point and midpoint formulas and classify demand as elastic, inelastic or unit-elastic.
  • Determine the direction of a revenue change from the elasticity, and explain what makes demand more or less elastic.
  • Calculate the volume change a price change needs to keep total contribution unchanged, and explain why a price cut that raises revenue can lower profit.
  • Choose between skimming and penetration pricing for a new product and justify the choice with evidence.
  • Evaluate charm prices, reference prices, discounts, bundling and price discrimination against the research evidence and their legal and ethical limits.

Review first

  • Pricing approaches — You need contribution margin per unit, the contribution margin ratio and Lantern’s net price per can.
  • Percentage change between two numbers, and multiplying by 1 plus a percentage.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern’s sales team has a question that break-even analysis cannot answer. If Lantern Cold Brew sold at $3.14 instead of $3.49, about 10% less, how many more cans would it sell, and would the extra cans make up for the 35 cents? The answer depends on how shoppers respond to price, which Lantern has to measure rather than assume. The Lantern sales figures in this lesson come from a hypothetical in-store price test invented for teaching; they are not estimates of real demand.

A demand curve shows how many units customers would buy at each price over a period, with everything else held constant: the product, competitors’ prices, promotion, the season. It usually slopes downwards, because at a lower price some existing buyers buy more and some new buyers start. A change in the product’s own price moves you along the curve. A change in anything else, such as an advertising campaign, a competitor’s price cut or a heatwave, shifts the whole curve. Keeping the two apart matters when you read sales data: if Lantern cut its price in the same month as a sampling campaign, the extra sales could not be credited to the price alone.

Price elasticity of demand

The slope of a demand curve depends on the units used, cans per month or cans per year, dollars or cents, so marketers measure responsiveness in percentages instead. The price elasticity of demand is the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price that caused it:

E=% ΔQ% ΔPE = \dfrac{\%\,\Delta Q}{\%\,\Delta P}E=%ΔP%ΔQ​

Because quantity usually moves in the opposite direction to price, EEE is usually negative. Many economics textbooks, OpenStax’s among them, report its absolute value instead. This course keeps the sign, so an elasticity of −1.5 means that a 1% price rise reduces the quantity bought by about 1.5% and a 1% cut raises it by about 1.5%. Under either convention, demand is classified by the size of the number, ignoring the sign:

Elastic demand
The magnitude of EEE is greater than 1: quantity changes by a larger percentage than price.
Inelastic demand
The magnitude of EEE is less than 1: quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price.
Unit-elastic demand
The magnitude of EEE equals 1: the two percentage changes are the same size.

Elasticity and revenue

Revenue is price times quantity, so a price change pulls revenue two ways at once: each unit sold earns more or less, and fewer or more units are sold. Elasticity says which pull wins. For small changes, the percentage change in revenue is roughly the percentage change in price plus the percentage change in quantity. When demand is elastic the quantity effect is the larger one, so revenue moves in the opposite direction to price: a cut raises revenue and a rise lowers it. When demand is inelastic the price effect is larger, so revenue moves with the price. At unit elasticity revenue hardly changes.

The revenue rule

Elastic (magnitude above 1)Revenue fallsRevenue rises
Inelastic (magnitude below 1)Revenue risesRevenue falls
Unit-elastic (magnitude 1)Revenue unchangedRevenue unchanged

For example, if a 10% price rise reduces the quantity sold by 15%, then E=−15÷10=−1.5E = -15 \div 10 = -1.5E=−15÷10=−1.5. Demand is elastic, and revenue becomes 1.10×0.85=0.9351.10 \times 0.85 = 0.9351.10×0.85=0.935 of what it was, a fall of 6.5%.

What makes demand more or less elastic

  • Substitutes. The more close alternatives buyers can switch to, the more elastic demand is. This is why the demand facing one brand is usually far more elastic than demand for the whole category: a shopper can swap Lantern for Arcadia much more easily than give up coffee.
  • Share of the budget. A 10% rise is noticed more on a holiday or a car than on a box of matches; the bigger the share of spending, the more buyers search and compare.
  • Necessity. Necessities such as housing and electricity tend to have inelastic demand; restaurant meals and other purchases people can postpone or skip tend to have elastic demand.
  • Time. Demand is often less elastic in the short run than in the long run, because buyers need time to find alternatives and change habits.
  • Loyalty and switching costs. Buyers who trust a brand, or who would lose something by switching, such as the effort of learning a new system or the points in a loyalty scheme, respond less to its price.
  • How easily prices are compared. Lantern’s market file notes that supermarket shoppers compare prices on the shelf, while convenience-store buyers pick up chilled singles on impulse with less comparison. The same can is likely to face more elastic demand in a supermarket.

Use the point formula when you are given an elasticity and want to predict the effect of a change from today’s price; the prediction is approximate, and less reliable the larger the change. Analysts with many observations estimate elasticity by regression on the logarithms of price and quantity, a method covered in the analytics courses.

Elasticity is not the slope. Along a straight-line demand curve the slope is the same everywhere, but the same change in units is a smaller percentage of a large quantity, and the same change in price a larger percentage of a low price. So a straight-line demand curve is elastic at high prices and inelastic at low ones. One consequence matters in practice: no product has “an” elasticity. It has an elasticity at a price, over a range of prices, in a market and over a time horizon.

Profit is not revenue

Lantern’s test shows elastic demand, so cutting the price to $3.14 raises revenue. Whether it raises profit is a different question. Every extra can carries its own $0.74 cost of goods, and the lower price reduces the contribution margin on every can, including the cans that would have sold anyway. A price cut pays only if the extra cans bring in more contribution than the lower margin gives away.

New products: skimming or penetration

A new product’s first price has to be set before there is any sales history to measure elasticity from, and it is hard to raise once buyers have seen it. Two classic strategies frame the choice. Price skimming launches at a high price to sell to the buyers who value the product most, then lowers the price in steps to reach the next group. Penetration pricing launches at a low price to win as many buyers as possible early in the product life cycle, accepting a low margin per unit in exchange for volume.

When each strategy tends to fit
Skimming fits when…Penetration fits when…
Some buyers value the product highly and are not price-sensitiveDemand is elastic, so a low price brings in many more buyers
Competitors cannot copy it quickly (patents, know-how, a strong brand)Rivals can enter easily, so a large customer base is the best defence
Capacity is limited at first, so high volume could not be supplied anywayUnit costs fall as volume grows, so a low price becomes profitable with scale
A high price supports a premium position and signals qualityBuyers form habits, or the product becomes more useful as more people use it

Evidence on what firms actually do is a useful corrective. In a study of 663 digital cameras launched under 79 brand names, about 20% followed a skimming price path and 20% a penetration path, while the other 60% were launched at the market price; the authors found that market pricing dominates in practice (Spann, Fischer and Tellis, 2015). Skimming and penetration are the two ends of a range, and many launches sit near the going rate.

Psychological pricing

Psychological pricing sets prices with an eye to how buyers perceive numbers, not only to what the product is worth to them. The best-known form is the charm price, a price just below a round number: $2.99 rather than $3.00. Thomas and Morwitz (2005) explain why it can work. People read prices from left to right and give the leftmost digit most weight, so $2.99 can feel noticeably cheaper than $3.00. They found that this left-digit effect occurs only when the leftmost digits differ, and that it is more likely the closer together the two prices being compared are.

The evidence is not uniform. Sokolova, Seenivasan and Thomas (2020) find the bias is stronger when prices are judged side by side, as on a shelf, than when one of them is recalled from memory, and they note that the size of the effect varies substantially across published studies. Treat charm prices as a real but context-dependent effect, worth testing rather than assuming. For Lantern the research has a direct implication: $3.49 and $3.50 share a left digit, so the 49-cent ending is unlikely to do much on its own; the effect would matter in a choice between, say, $2.99 and $3.09. Round prices can work the other way: OpenStax notes that luxury goods are often given even prices, which suggest quality rather than a bargain.

Reference prices, discounts and promotions

Buyers judge a price against a reference price, the price they expect or regard as normal for the product. Reviewing the research, Kalyanaram and Winer (1995) set out three well-supported generalisations: buyers use reference prices when they choose between brands; they form them largely from the prices they have seen in the past; and they react more strongly to a price above their reference price, which feels like a loss, than to one the same distance below it, which feels like a gain.

Those findings explain the hidden cost of discounting. Each promotion adds a low price to shoppers’ memories and pulls their reference price down. A brand that is on deal every other week teaches its buyers that the deal price is normal, so the regular price starts to feel like a loss and full-price sales suffer. Frequent deals can also teach buyers to wait for the next one or to stock up during it, so part of the extra volume is borrowed from later weeks rather than created. Lantern’s default plan spends $20,000 on trade promotions. The case for them is trial by new buyers; the risk is a lower reference price among the buyers Lantern already has.

Sellers also supply external reference prices: “was $4.29”, “recommended retail price”, “compare at”. These must be genuine. OpenStax describes the US Federal Trade Commission’s view that comparing a price with a “suggested” price at which the product isn’t actually sold, or raising a price just before a “buy one, get one free” offer, is deceptive. Other jurisdictions have their own consumer-protection rules on price comparisons, so check the local law before advertising one.

Bundling

Bundling sells two or more products together for one price, usually less than the sum of their separate prices: a fast-food value meal, a hotel room with breakfast, a suite of software modules. Pure bundling sells only the bundle; mixed bundling offers the bundle and the separate items. Bundles can raise the amount each customer spends, and they can capture more of the value customers place on products when their tastes differ.

The bundle works because the two customers value the modules in opposite ways, so their values for the pair are closer together than their values for either module. For software an extra copy costs almost nothing, so the extra revenue is nearly all contribution. For physical goods, check that the bundle’s price still covers the variable costs of everything in it.

Price discrimination

Price discrimination, often called segmented pricing in marketing, charges different customers different prices for the same product when the difference is not explained by a difference in cost. Cinemas and museums charge students less; hotels and airlines charge different rates for the same room or seat depending on when you book, whether you can cancel and which day you stay or travel. It works when three conditions hold: the segments differ in price sensitivity; the seller can tell them apart, by a student card or by letting customers sort themselves through conditions such as advance purchase; and buyers paying the low price cannot easily resell to those paying the high one.

The conditions attached to cheaper rates are called fences. A non-refundable rate booked a month ahead suits a leisure traveller but not a business traveller whose plans may change, so each group chooses the rate meant for it. A fence that leaks, letting price-insensitive buyers take the cheap rate, gives revenue away. The applied example works through the arithmetic for a hotel.

Legal and ethical limits

Pricing freedom has limits that differ from country to country, so treat what follows as general principles, not legal advice. Agreements between competitors to set prices (price fixing) are prohibited by competition law in many jurisdictions. Advertised comparisons with former or recommended prices must be genuine. Selling below cost to drive a rival out of a market and then raising prices (predatory pricing) can break antitrust law. Charging competing business customers different prices for the same goods is restricted in some jurisdictions, and price differences based on characteristics protected by anti-discrimination law raise serious legal problems. OpenStax notes that many US states prohibit price gouging during declared emergencies.

The law is the minimum. Customers also judge prices for fairness, and OpenStax notes that customers who feel they are not getting a fair deal from price discrimination may stop being loyal. Two practical tests help: could you explain the price difference openly to the customers who pay more, and would you be comfortable seeing it described in the press?

Common mistakes

  • “Revenue went up, so the price cut worked.”

    Revenue ignores costs. In Lantern’s hypothetical test, a 10% cut raised revenue by about 2.3% and lowered contribution by about 2.8%. A cut pays only if volume rises by more than −ΔP÷(CM+ΔP)-\Delta P \div (CM + \Delta P)−ΔP÷(CM+ΔP); at a 30% contribution margin ratio, a 10% cut needs 50% more volume.

  • “Elasticity is the slope of the demand curve.”

    Slope is the change in units per dollar and depends on the units chosen; elasticity is a ratio of percentages. Along a straight-line demand curve the slope is constant but elasticity changes, from elastic at high prices to inelastic at low prices. Always say at what price, and over what range, an elasticity was measured.

  • “Divide each change by its starting value.”

    With two observed points, simple percentage changes give a different elasticity depending on which point you start from: about −1.37 or −1.08 for Lantern’s test. The midpoint formula gives about −1.22 both ways.

  • “Demand is inelastic, so we can keep raising the price.”

    An elasticity holds over the range of prices where it was measured. Along a straight-line demand curve its magnitude grows as the price rises; demand is often more elastic in the long run, as buyers find alternatives; and competitors can change the picture. Inelastic demand says a small rise will raise revenue, not that every rise will.

  • “Charm prices always add sales.”

    The left-digit effect appears when the leftmost digit changes ($2.99 against $3.00), is stronger when prices are compared side by side, and varies in size across studies. $3.49 and $3.50 share a left digit. Test a charm price before relying on it, and remember that round prices can signal quality.

  • “A discount only costs the discount.”

    Discounts also pull shoppers’ reference price down, can borrow sales from future weeks as buyers stock up, and invite competitors to match. Count those effects before judging a promotion by the extra units it sold that week.

In practice

Lantern’s launch: premium, penetration or the going rate?

Ana’s team argues the launch price both ways. The case for staying at $3.49, or moving closer to Summit’s $3.79, rests on value and position. Lantern’s taste score of 7.5 is close to Summit’s 8 and well above Arcadia’s 6; the single-origin coffee supports a premium story; and a low launch price would become shoppers’ reference price, which is hard to raise later. It also rests on arithmetic: at $3.49 the plan breaks even at about 117,000 cans.

The case for a penetration price of, say, $2.79 rests on awareness and trial. Only 12% of category buyers know Lantern, against 60% for Summit and 75% for Daily Grind, and a shopper who has never tasted Lantern has no reason to pay a premium for it. A lower price lowers the risk of trying it, and supermarket shoppers compare prices on the shelf. But the cost is steep. At $2.79, Lantern’s contribution falls to about $1.16 a can in supermarkets, $0.99 in natural grocers and $1.60 in its own channel, an average of about $1.18, and break-even rises to 197,000÷1.1846≈166,303197{,}000 \div 1.1846 \approx 166{,}303197,000÷1.1846≈166,303 cans, 42% more than at $3.49. (These use lesson 4’s simplifying assumption that cans sell in proportion to channel volume; the simulator, with its own channel mix and trade deals charged per can, gives about 167,000 at $2.79 against about 117,000 at $3.49.)

Argument alone cannot settle this. A defensible plan is to launch near the planned position, use sampling and a limited number of trade promotions to buy trial without teaching shoppers a permanently lower price, and run a controlled price test in a few stores to estimate elasticity from Lantern’s own data. The required-volume formula then turns the test into a decision: cut the price only if the measured volume increase beats the increase the lower contribution requires. The camera study suggests this middle course is common: most launches were priced at the market rate rather than skimmed or used for penetration.

Revenue management in hotels and airlines

Hotels and airlines practise segmented pricing on a large scale because their product perishes: an empty room or seat tonight cannot be sold tomorrow, and the variable cost of filling it is small compared with the fixed cost of the building or the aircraft. Revenue managers set several rates for the same room with different fences, forecast how many guests in each segment will book, protect enough rooms for late, higher-paying guests and sell the rest earlier at lower rates. The applied example shows why: a single rate either leaves rooms empty or charges business travellers less than they would pay.

A checklist before any price change

  1. Measure, don’t assume: estimate the elasticity from a test or from past price changes, holding promotion and season constant.
  2. Calculate the volume change the price change needs, using the contribution per unit the business actually receives.
  3. Ask how competitors will react: a cut that they match brings little extra volume and lowers everyone’s margin.
  4. Think about reference prices: a temporary cut can lower what buyers regard as normal.
  5. Check the legal limits, and how the change will look to the customers who pay more.

Summary

  • Price elasticity of demand = % change in quantity ÷ % change in price. It is usually negative; demand is elastic when its magnitude is above 1 and inelastic when below 1.
  • With two observed points, use the midpoint formula: it gives the same answer in both directions. Elasticity is not the slope and changes along a straight-line demand curve.
  • With elastic demand, price and revenue move in opposite directions; with inelastic demand they move together. Substitutes, budget share, necessity, time, loyalty and ease of comparison shape elasticity.
  • Profit is not revenue: a price change leaves contribution unchanged only if volume changes by −ΔP ÷ (CM + ΔP). Price cuts need large volume gains when margins are thin.
  • Skimming launches high and steps down; penetration launches low to build volume. In practice many launches sit at the market price.
  • Charm prices work mainly when the leftmost digit changes, and the effect varies; buyers form reference prices from past prices, so frequent discounts lower them.
  • Bundling and segmented pricing earn more when customers differ and fences hold. Price fixing, false reference prices and some forms of discrimination are illegal, and fairness matters beyond the law.

Terms introduced

Demand curve
The quantity of a product customers would buy at each price over a period, with everything else held constant. A price change moves along it; other changes shift it.
Price elasticity of demand
The percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. Usually negative; this course keeps the sign, while many texts report the absolute value.
Elastic demand
Demand whose elasticity has a magnitude above 1: quantity changes by a larger percentage than price, so revenue moves in the opposite direction to price.
Inelastic demand
Demand whose elasticity has a magnitude below 1: quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price, so revenue moves in the same direction as price.
Midpoint formula
A way of calculating elasticity between two observed points that divides each change by the average of the two values, so the answer is the same in either direction. Also called arc elasticity.
Price skimming
Launching a new product at a high price to sell first to the buyers who value it most, then lowering the price in steps to reach more buyers.
Penetration pricing
Launching a new product at a low price to win many buyers quickly, accepting a low margin per unit in exchange for volume.
Psychological pricing
Setting prices with regard to how buyers perceive numbers, for example charm prices just below a round number, or round prices that suggest quality.
Reference price
The price a buyer expects or regards as normal for a product, formed largely from past prices, against which an actual price is judged as a gain or a loss.
Bundling
Selling two or more products together for one price, usually less than the sum of their separate prices. Pure bundling sells only the bundle; mixed bundling also sells the items separately.
Price discrimination
Charging different customers different prices for the same product when the difference does not reflect a difference in cost. Also called segmented pricing.
