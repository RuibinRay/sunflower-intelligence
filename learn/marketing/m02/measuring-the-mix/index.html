Measuring promotion and bringing the mix together

Marketing numbers are easy to collect and easy to misread. Learn the media and digital metrics, why ROAS is not ROI, how to find out what promotion actually caused, and how to check that product, price, place and promotion work as one plan.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate reach, average frequency, gross rating points, CPM, CTR, conversion rate and CPA from campaign data.
  • Distinguish ROAS from marketing ROI and show how a campaign with a high ROAS can lose money.
  • Explain incrementality and design a holdout or A/B test that measures it.
  • Identify the bias in last-click attribution and in other correlational evidence from marketing data.
  • Choose a small set of dashboard metrics that match a plan’s objectives.
  • Evaluate a marketing mix for consistency across product, price, place and promotion.

Review first

  • The promotion mix and integrated marketing communications — You need the promotion tools and the objective-and-task budget whose results this lesson measures.
  • Distribution channels and decisions — Lantern’s gross margin per can in each channel is the margin that turns attributed revenue into profit.
  • Pricing approaches — Marketing ROI is built on contribution, not revenue.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Six weeks after launch, three reports land on Ana Lim’s desk (all figures hypothetical). The agency’s says the social and search ads delivered 6.2 million impressions with a click-through rate of 0.9%. A supermarket chain’s report says Lantern’s ads in its app returned $4.19 of sales for every $1 spent. The sampling team reports 38,000 tastings, and supermarket sales of Lantern are rising. Ana’s question is simple: which of this money worked? Every report is accurate, and none of them answers it.

Marketing metrics answer four different questions, and most mistakes come from using a number from one question to answer another. Did people see it? Exposure metrics count impressions, people reached and how often. Did they respond? Response metrics count clicks, sign-ups and purchases. Did it pay? Return metrics compare what came back with what was spent. And the hardest: did the promotion cause it? That needs a comparison with what would have happened without it.

Did people see it? Impressions, reach, frequency and GRPs

An impression is one display of an ad: one person seeing one ad once, or the same person seeing it twice counts as two. Digital impressions need care. The Media Rating Council, the US body that accredits audience measurement, distinguishes a served impression, counted when the ad is delivered, from a viewable one; its guidelines count a display ad as viewable only if at least half of its pixels are on screen for at least one continuous second (two seconds of play for video). Many served impressions are never seen.

Reach is the number of different people exposed at least once, often given as a percentage of the target audience. Frequency (average frequency) is the average number of times each reached person was exposed. Gross rating points (GRPs) combine the two into one measure of weight:

GRPs count exposures, not people: 300 GRPs could mean everyone seeing the ad three times or 30% of people seeing it ten times, which are very different campaigns.

The usual cost measure for exposure is CPM, the cost per thousand impressions (M is the Roman numeral for a thousand). If those 1,200,000 impressions cost $9,600, the CPM is 9,600÷1,200,000×1,000=89{,}600 \div 1{,}200{,}000 \times 1{,}000 = 89,600÷1,200,000×1,000=8 dollars. CPM lets a planner compare media, but a cheap impression nobody notices is not a bargain.

Did they respond? Clicks, conversions and cost per acquisition

Digital media add response metrics. CTR, the click-through rate, is clicks divided by impressions. CPC, the cost per click, is spend divided by clicks. Conversion rate is the share of clicks (or of visits; definitions vary, so always check) that end in the action the campaign wants, such as an order or a sign-up. CPA, the cost per acquisition or cost per action, is spend divided by conversions. The four are linked:

CPA=SpendConversions=CPM÷1,000CTR×Conversion rate\text{CPA} = \dfrac{\text{Spend}}{\text{Conversions}} = \dfrac{\text{CPM} \div 1{,}000}{\text{CTR} \times \text{Conversion rate}}CPA=ConversionsSpend​=CTR×Conversion rateCPM÷1,000​

The identity shows the three levers that lower the cost of a customer: cheaper impressions, more clicks per impression and more conversions per click. It also shows why chasing one of them can mislead: an ad that wins cheap clicks from people who never buy has a good CTR and a terrible CPA.

Did it pay? ROAS and marketing ROI

ROAS, return on ad spend, is the revenue attributed to a campaign divided by what it cost. It is quoted as a ratio (4.0, or 4:1) or a percentage (400%). Marketing ROI compares the profit the campaign added with its cost:

ROAS=Attributed revenueAd spendMarketing ROI=Incremental contribution−Marketing costMarketing cost\text{ROAS} = \dfrac{\text{Attributed revenue}}{\text{Ad spend}} \qquad \text{Marketing ROI} = \dfrac{\text{Incremental contribution} - \text{Marketing cost}}{\text{Marketing cost}}ROAS=Ad spendAttributed revenue​Marketing ROI=Marketing costIncremental contribution−Marketing cost​

Incremental contribution is the extra revenue the campaign caused, minus the variable costs of delivering it (in Lantern’s case cost of goods, trade margins and fulfilment); it is the contribution margin idea from the pricing lessons applied to a campaign. Variants exist: some companies use gross profit, some include fixed costs created by the campaign, and some count expected repeat purchases over a customer’s lifetime. Whichever version you use, say which, and keep it the same when you compare campaigns.

Key idea: ROAS and ROI are not interchangeable

ROAS divides revenue by spend; ROI divides profit by spend. A fictional online clothing retailer spends $20,000 and its ads are credited with $80,000 of sales: a ROAS of 4.0. If only 20% of each sale is left after the cost of the clothes, shipping and returns, the sales added $16,000 of contribution, and ROI is (16,000−20,000)÷20,000=−20%(16{,}000 - 20{,}000) \div 20{,}000 = -20\%(16,000−20,000)÷20,000=−20%. A campaign breaks even only when ROAS reaches 1÷0.20=51 \div 0.20 = 51÷0.20=5: the break-even ROAS is one divided by the contribution margin ratio.

In depth

Incrementality: what would have happened anyway?

Every return metric above depends on one assumption: that the sales credited to the campaign were caused by it. Incrementality is the part of an outcome that would not have happened without the marketing. The rest would have happened anyway: loyal customers who were going to buy, shoppers who already had the can in their basket, people searching for Lantern by name who would have found it without an ad.

This is not a theoretical worry. In large field experiments at eBay, Blake, Nosko and Tadelis (2015) found that paid search ads on the company’s own brand name had no measurable short-term benefit (people searching for a company by name can reach it through the free search results without the ad), and that the returns from paid search were a fraction of what the usual non-experimental estimates suggested.

The only reliable way to measure incrementality is to create a comparison group that didn’t get the marketing, chosen so that it is otherwise the same:

  • Holdout (control) group: withhold the ad, email or offer from a randomly chosen holdout group and compare its behaviour with the exposed group. The difference is the incremental effect.
  • A/B test: randomly split people (or stores, or weeks) between two versions, such as two messages or two discount levels, and compare outcomes.
  • Geographic experiments and test markets: run the campaign in some areas or store groups and not in comparable others. A test market launches a whole plan in a limited area before rolling it out.

Random assignment is the key. If Lantern sampled only in its twenty busiest supermarkets and compared them with the rest, the difference would mix the effect of sampling with the effect of being a busy store. Randomising which stores are sampled makes the two groups alike on average, so the remaining difference can be credited to the sampling.

Experiments have limits too. Sales vary a great deal from person to person and week to week, while the effect of one campaign is usually small. Lewis and Rao (2015), analysing 25 large online advertising experiments with US retailers and brokerages, found that measuring returns precisely could need samples of more than ten million person-weeks, and that the median confidence interval on ROI was over 100 percentage points wide. Good tests need planning: enough stores or people, a long enough period, and a decision rule set before the results arrive.

Attribution and its biases

Attribution is the set of rules that divides credit for a sale among the marketing touchpoints that preceded it. Suppose a customer sees a Lantern social ad, later reads a food blog’s review after the launch event, then searches “Lantern cold brew”, clicks a search ad and orders a $41.88 12-pack online.

Credit for one $41.88 order under three attribution rules (hypothetical path)
TouchpointLast clickFirst clickLinear (equal shares)
Social ad$0.00$41.88$13.96
Food-blog review (PR)$0.00$0.00$13.96
Search ad$41.88$0.00$13.96

PR coverage usually earns no clicks to track, so click-based rules rarely credit it at all.

Last-click attribution gives all the credit to the final click. It is simple, and it systematically favours the touchpoints closest to the purchase, such as search ads on the brand’s own name and retargeting ads shown to people who already visited the shop, which often capture demand that other activity created. Data-driven models, which share credit according to patterns in past conversion paths, are now common; Google Ads, for example, offers only last-click and data-driven attribution, having retired its first-click, linear, time-decay and position-based models. But every attribution model, however sophisticated, divides up credit for sales that happened. None of them can say which sales would have happened without any marketing. Gordon and colleagues (2019) compared 15 Facebook advertising experiments with the observational methods commonly used in the industry and found the observational methods often failed to reproduce the experimental results.

Caution: Correlation is not causation in marketing data

Stores with displays sell more Lantern: but big, busy stores are the ones that get displays. Weeks with more ad spending sell more: but spending is scheduled for the summer peak, when the category sells about 19% more in the third quarter than in the second. Customers who get emails buy more: but emails go to existing customers. Each pattern is real, and none shows the marketing caused the sales. Before believing a correlation, ask what else differs between the groups, and whether the outcome could have caused the spending instead of the other way round.

A dashboard: a few numbers that matter

A marketing dashboard is a short, regular report of the measures that show whether the plan is working. Its value comes from what it leaves out. Each number should be tied to an objective in the plan, defined the same way every period, and paired with a target. Leading indicators (awareness, distribution, trial) warn early; lagging ones (repeat purchase, contribution) say whether the plan is paying.

Bringing the mix together

The course began with the idea that the four Ps must fit each other and the target market. Measurement is where a misfit shows: a premium product that sells only on discount, ads that send people to stores that don’t stock the can, a range whose listing fees eat the margin. A consistency check asks one question for each pair of decisions.

A consistency checklist, applied to Lantern’s first plan

The simulator and the real world

The Marketing Mix Simulator turns a plan into simulated sales, contribution and share using a model with stated assumptions and invented coefficients. It is good for what models are good for: comparing plans on the same basis, seeing which decisions matter most, and finding trade-offs you hadn’t noticed, such as a trade-promotion budget that adds units but lowers contribution. It is not a forecast. Real shoppers, competitors and retailers respond in ways no teaching model captures: a rival cuts its price, a buyer refuses a listing, a heatwave doubles summer sales. A real launch would test its key assumptions before committing the whole budget, with a test market in part of the city, store-level experiments for sampling and A/B tests for messages, and would keep measuring after launch.

Common mistakes

  • “A ROAS above 1 means the campaign made money.”

    ROAS compares revenue with spend, but the business keeps only the contribution from that revenue. With a 20% contribution margin a campaign needs a ROAS of 5 just to break even. Convert ROAS to contribution, then to incremental contribution, before judging it.

  • “The channel that got the last click caused the sale.”

    Last-click attribution credits the final touchpoint, often a brand-name search ad or a retargeting ad that caught someone already on the way to buying. Experiments such as eBay’s brand-search test found such ads had no measurable short-term benefit. Credit is not causation; test with holdouts.

  • “Sales rose after the campaign, so the campaign worked.”

    Something else may explain the rise: the season (the category sells about 19% more in the third quarter than in the second), a competitor’s stock-out, new stores stocking the product. A before-and-after comparison can’t separate these; a comparison with a randomly chosen group that didn’t get the campaign can.

  • “Impressions tell you how many people saw the ad.”

    Impressions count displays, including repeats to the same person and served ads that never appeared on screen. Reach counts people; GRPs count weight. Report the measure that answers your question, and ask whether impressions are served or viewable.

  • “A higher click-through rate means a better ad.”

    CTR measures clicks, not customers. An ad that draws curious clicks from people who never buy can have a high CTR and a high CPA. Judge ads by the outcome the campaign exists for, ideally the incremental one.

  • “The simulator tells us what will happen.”

    A simulator applies stated assumptions consistently; that is why it is useful for comparing plans. Its coefficients are invented for teaching, and real markets react in ways no model captures. Use it to explore and compare, then test the key assumptions in the real world before committing the budget.

In practice

At Lantern’s quarterly review the marketing manager doesn’t present every number the agencies sent. She presents the dashboard, the tests that ran and what they changed. Suppose (hypothetically) the brand-name search ads show the highest last-click ROAS of any spending, but a two-week holdout in which half of searchers saw no ad found almost no difference in orders. The manager moves most of that money into sampling in more stocking stores, where the randomised test above showed a positive return over twelve weeks, and keeps a small, continuing holdout to check the decision. The budget hasn’t grown; it has moved from where credit was claimed to where effect was shown.

The same habits pay off in other industries. An airline that emails a fare sale to its whole loyalty list should hold out a random slice of members to learn how many of the bookings would have come anyway. An online retailer whose retargeting ads follow people who left items in their basket should test how many of those people would have come back on their own. A university that runs open-day ads can compare applications from regions where the ads ran with similar regions where they didn’t. In each case the professional skill is the same: name the question, pick the metric that answers it, and build in a comparison that separates cause from coincidence.

Finally, the mix is judged as a whole. A plan with an excellent ROI on its ads can still fail if the product is in the wrong channels or priced against its positioning. That is why Lantern’s review ends with the consistency checklist and the contribution line, and why the course’s project asks you to defend a complete plan, not a single campaign.

Summary

  • Separate four questions: did people see it (impressions, reach, frequency, GRPs, CPM), did they respond (CTR, conversion rate, CPA), did it pay (ROAS, ROI) and did the marketing cause it (incrementality).
  • GRPs = reach (%) × average frequency = impressions ÷ target audience × 100; CPM = spend ÷ impressions × 1,000; CPA = spend ÷ conversions.
  • ROAS divides attributed revenue by ad spend; marketing ROI divides incremental contribution minus cost by cost. Break-even ROAS is 1 ÷ contribution margin ratio, so a high ROAS can still mean a loss.
  • Incrementality is what would not have happened without the marketing; only comparisons with randomly chosen holdout or control groups, A/B tests and test markets measure it reliably.
  • Attribution models divide credit among touchpoints; last-click favours the touchpoints nearest the sale, and no attribution model measures causation.
  • Correlations in marketing data are often driven by store size, seasonality or targeting; ask what else differs before crediting the marketing.
  • A good dashboard has a few measures, each tied to an objective with a target, mixing leading and lagging indicators.
  • Check the whole mix pair by pair for consistency, use the simulator to compare plans, and test key assumptions in the real world before committing.

Terms introduced

Impressions
The number of times an ad is displayed. Repeat views by the same person count separately; a served impression is counted on delivery, a viewable one only if it could actually be seen.
Reach
The number or percentage of different people in the target audience exposed to a campaign at least once.
Frequency
The average number of times each person reached was exposed to the campaign: impressions ÷ reach.
Gross rating points
A measure of the weight of a campaign: reach (as a percentage of the target audience) × average frequency, or impressions as a percentage of the audience. Abbreviated GRPs.
CPM
Cost per thousand impressions: spend ÷ impressions × 1,000.
CTR
Click-through rate: clicks ÷ impressions.
CPC
Cost per click: spend ÷ clicks.
Conversion rate
The percentage of visits, or of ad clicks, that end in a chosen action such as an order or a sign-up. Which denominator is used varies between tools and reports, so always state it.
CPA
Cost per acquisition (or per action): spend ÷ conversions.
ROAS
Return on ad spend: revenue attributed to a campaign ÷ the campaign’s ad spend. It ignores costs and whether the revenue was caused by the ads.
Marketing ROI
Return on marketing investment: (incremental contribution − marketing cost) ÷ marketing cost. Variants use gross profit or lifetime value.
Break-even ROAS
The ROAS at which a campaign’s contribution just covers its cost: 1 ÷ the contribution margin ratio.
Incrementality
The part of an outcome, such as sales, that was caused by the marketing and would not have happened without it.
Holdout group
A randomly chosen group deliberately not exposed to a campaign, used as the comparison for measuring its incremental effect.
Attribution
Rules for dividing credit for a conversion among the marketing touchpoints that preceded it, such as last click, first click, linear or data-driven.
Test market
Launching a product or marketing plan in a limited area to measure results before a full roll-out.
