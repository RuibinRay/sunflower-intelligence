The promotion mix and integrated marketing communications

Promotion is how a business gets its offer noticed, understood and tried. Learn what each promotion tool does well and badly, when to push through the channel or pull through demand, how to make the messages work as one, and how to build a budget from objectives instead of guessing.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Describe marketing communication as a process and explain where messages fail.
  • Compare advertising, sales promotion, personal selling, public relations and direct and digital marketing by reach, cost per contact, persuasiveness and control.
  • Distinguish push and pull strategies and classify promotion activities as one or the other.
  • Evaluate response-hierarchy models such as AIDA and explain their limits.
  • Calculate promotion budgets with the affordable, percentage-of-sales, competitive-parity and objective-and-task methods, and compare them.
  • Plan an integrated launch campaign that is consistent with the product, price and channels.

Review first

  • Distribution channels and decisions — Push promotion works through the channel, and the trade margins and listing fees from that lesson decide how much support each channel needs.
  • The marketing mix and customer value — Promotion has to communicate the positioning and value proposition set out there.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern’s can is listed, priced and on its way to the shelves. One number in the market file shows the problem that remains: only 12% of Harbour City’s cold-brew buyers have heard of Lantern, from its catering work and cafés. The other 88% will walk past a can they don’t recognise, next to Daily Grind, which 75% of them know, and Summit Brew, which 60% know. Shoppers have to notice the can, understand why a single-origin cold brew at $3.49 is worth more than a $2.29 one, and try it. Lantern’s first plan sets aside $120,000 for that: $50,000 for social and search ads, $40,000 for in-store sampling and displays, $20,000 for trade promotions and $10,000 for PR and events. This lesson is about how a split like that should be decided.

Promotion is the communication element of the marketing mix: everything a business does to inform, persuade and remind the people whose decisions matter to it. For Lantern those people are not only shoppers. Supermarket and grocery buyers decide whether the can gets a shelf, journalists decide whether its launch is news, and café customers decide whether to recommend it. Each audience needs a different kind of communication.

Communication as a process

A message doesn’t travel from a company into a customer’s mind intact. A simple model, used in most marketing textbooks, breaks communication into parts. The sender (Lantern) has an idea to get across. Encoding turns the idea into words, images and sounds: a six-second video of coffee steeping overnight. The encoded message travels through a medium, such as a social feed, a shelf display or a salesperson. The receiver (a shopper) decodes it, giving it meaning from their own experience. Feedback tells the sender what happened: a click, a purchase, a question, a complaint. And noise is anything that interferes along the way: the thirty other drinks in the fridge, a scrolling thumb, a rival’s louder offer.

The model is simple, but it explains why so much promotion fails. Lantern means “worth paying more for”; a shopper may decode “fancy” or “overpriced”. The fix is to encode in the receiver’s terms, test how messages are understood, choose media where the target audience is paying attention, repeat the message, and watch the feedback.

The five tools of the promotion mix

The promotion mix is the combination of communication tools a business uses. Many textbooks list five; some, including OpenStax, treat direct marketing and digital marketing separately and list six.

Advertising
Paid, non-personal communication from an identified sponsor through mass or digital media: television, radio, outdoor posters, print, online video and social and search ads.
Sales promotion
Short-term incentives to buy or sell now. Consumer promotions target shoppers: coupons, free samples, temporary price cuts, multi-buy offers, contests. Trade promotions target intermediaries: allowances that fund retailers’ price cuts, payments for displays, extra margin for featuring the product.
Personal selling
Person-to-person communication aimed at making a sale and building a relationship, face to face or by phone or video call.
Public relations
Building good relationships with the company’s publics, including media, communities and investors, and earning favourable coverage: press releases, launch events, sponsorships, interviews.
Direct and digital marketing
Communication with carefully targeted individuals that invites a direct response: email, direct mail, messaging, the company’s website and app, and targeted online ads. Online and social media blur the line with advertising, which is why many textbooks group them together.

The tools differ along two dimensions that pull against each other: how many people a tool reaches for each dollar, and how persuasive each contact is. A salesperson spending an hour with a supermarket buyer reaches one person at a high cost, but can tailor every argument, answer objections and close the deal. A social ad reaches thousands for the same money, but each person sees it for a second and can scroll past. Public relations can be cheap and credible, because a journalist’s word is trusted more than an ad, but the company can’t control whether or how the story runs.

Push and pull

A product like Lantern’s has to travel through the channel from the previous lesson, and promotion can help it along from either end. A push strategy directs promotion at the channel: personal selling to distributors and store buyers, and trade promotions that give them reasons to stock, display and sell the product. The producer pushes the product to the next level, which pushes it on. A pull strategy directs promotion at final customers, through advertising, consumer promotions, PR and digital marketing, to create demand that draws the product through the channel: shoppers ask for it, so stores stock it.

For Lantern, the push is the sales manager’s meetings with supermarket and natural-grocer buyers, the $20,000 of trade promotions that fund temporary price cuts in store, and the displays and sampling days offered as part of the deal that wins the listing. The pull is the $50,000 of social and search ads, the PR launch and emails to Lantern’s café customers. In-store sampling sits on the boundary: Lantern negotiates it with retailers as part of its support package, so this course counts it with the push, but it acts on shoppers at the shelf, which is why some textbooks call sampling a consumer (pull) promotion. What matters is being clear about whom each activity is meant to move.

Key idea: Push and pull are partners, not rivals

A new brand needs push to get onto the shelf and pull to get off it. Without push, the advertising sends shoppers to stores that don’t stock the can; without pull, the retailer stocks a can nobody picks up, and drops it at the next review. Brands with strong pull also gain channel power, because retailers need them.

In depth

Response hierarchies: AIDA and its relatives

Why would a $50,000 ad campaign lead to sales? The oldest answer is a ladder of mental steps. AIDA says a buyer moves from attention (noticing the product) to interest (wanting to know more) to desire (wanting it) to action (buying). The formula is usually credited to the American advertising pioneer E. St. Elmo Lewis around 1898, on the strength of the psychologist Edward K. Strong’s account in 1925; a 2024 historical study by Akinori Iwamoto argues that there is no evidence Lewis devised it then, and that early sales writers shaped the formula. AIDA is best described as a long-standing model whose exact origin is uncertain.

Later models stretched the ladder. Robert Lavidge and Gary Steiner (1961) proposed six steps: awareness, knowledge, liking, preference, conviction and purchase, grouped into thinking, feeling and doing. Models of this family are called hierarchy-of-effects models. They are useful in two ways. They remind a planner that most people aren’t ready to buy the first time they hear of a product, and they let a campaign set a different objective for each stage, measured in a different way: awareness by surveys, interest by visits or sign-ups, action by sales.

The models also have limits, and a careful planner treats them as checklists, not laws. Vakratsas and Ambler (1999) reviewed more than 250 studies of how advertising works and concluded that there is little support for a fixed temporal sequence of effects. Real buying often skips or reverses steps. A canned drink is a low-involvement, low-price purchase: a shopper may grab Lantern because it is chilled and next to the till, and decide whether she likes it only after tasting it. For such products experience forms the preference, which is why sampling matters so much. The ladders also stop at the first purchase, while a brand like Lantern lives on the second and the tenth, and they treat each person as moving alone when many decisions are shaped by friends, reviews and what is on the shelf.

Caution: Frameworks are simplifications

AIDA, push and pull and integrated marketing communications are ways of organising decisions, not descriptions of how every buyer behaves. Use them to ask better questions (Which stage is our bottleneck? Who is this activity meant to move?), then check the answers against evidence from your own market.

Integrated marketing communications

Integrated marketing communications (IMC) means planning all the promotion tools together so that they deliver one clear, consistent message about the brand. Each tool is used for the job it does best, the timing is coordinated, and every touchpoint, from the can and the shelf display to the ads, the salesperson’s pitch and the café menu, tells the same story about the same positioning.

For Lantern, integration means that the claim “single-origin cold brew, steeped 20 hours” appears on the can, in the ads, in the sampling script and in the press kit; that ads run near the stores that stock the can, in the weeks when sampling teams are there; and that trade promotions are rare enough not to teach shoppers that a $3.49 can is really a $2.79 one.

IMC is easier to agree with than to achieve. Different tools are often run by different teams or agencies, each with its own budget and its own measure of success, so each optimises its own piece. Retailers run their own promotions, and a supermarket flyer may present Lantern as a bargain while Lantern’s ads present it as a treat. Short-term sales targets favour price promotions that undercut a premium message. Measurement credits each channel separately, which the next lesson shows can mislead. And integration is not uniformity: the same idea has to be adapted to a six-second video, a shelf tag and a conversation with a buyer, and every adaptation is a chance for the message to drift.

Setting the promotion budget

There is no formula that gives the right promotion budget, because the response of sales to promotion is uncertain and varies from market to market. Companies use four main methods, often in combination.

Four ways to set a promotion budget
MethodHow the budget is setStrengthWeakness
Affordable methodWhatever is left after other costs and the profit targetProtects cash; simpleIgnores what promotion could achieve; swings with profits; starves launches, which need spending before sales arrive
Percentage-of-sales methodA fixed percentage of past or forecast salesSimple; ties spending to the size of the business; easy to compare across yearsReverses cause and effect (sales decide promotion, not the other way round); no past sales at launch; cuts spending when sales fall, which may be when it is most needed
Competitive-parity methodMatch competitors’ spending, or their spending relative to salesAvoids being badly outspent; gives a market reference pointAssumes rivals know best and share your objectives; estimates of rivals’ spending are rough
Objective-and-task methodSet objectives, list the tasks needed to reach them, and cost each taskLinks money to results; makes trade-offs explicit; can be tested afterwardsNeeds estimates of how much effort produces how much result; more work; can be padded

The objective-and-task method is the one textbooks recommend and, by OpenStax’s account, one of the least used, because it demands the most thinking. Its real strength is that it turns the budget into a list of jobs with prices. If the total is too high, the company can see which objective it is giving up when it cuts a task, instead of trimming every line by the same percentage. In practice planners often build the budget by objective and task, then check it against what the business can afford and what rivals spend.

Note: What the simulator says about the $20,000 of trade promotion

In the Marketing Mix Simulator, with everything else at the first plan, cutting trade promotion from $20,000 to $0 raises Lantern’s profit after overhead from about $122,956 to $133,234. The deals add about 6,233 cans, but Lantern pays 20% of the price on all of the roughly 28,653 promoted cans, most of which shoppers would have bought anyway, so the extra gross profit (about $9,721) falls well short of the $20,000. The model’s trade money buys only temporary price cuts: it gives no value to the shelf position, features and retailer goodwill that a new brand’s promotion windows are often negotiated to win, and because the model has no repeat purchase, a shopper who first tries Lantern on a deal is worth one can. Whether those are worth about $10,000 a year is a judgement the model can’t make. Treat the trade budget as part of the price of winning and keeping the listings, keep it as small as the retailers will accept, and check whether the deals bring buyers back at the full price.

Common mistakes

  • “Promotion means advertising.”

    Advertising is one of five tools. For a new packaged product, personal selling and trade promotion often matter most at first, because without them the product never reaches the shelf the advertising points to. Ask which audience must act first, and choose the tool that moves that audience.

  • “If people see the ad enough times, they will buy.”

    Hierarchy models describe a tidy ladder, but evidence reviewed by Vakratsas and Ambler (1999) gives little support to a fixed sequence of effects. For low-involvement products, trial often comes before liking, so getting the product into people’s hands (sampling, availability) can matter more than repeating the message.

  • “Sales promotions build the brand.”

    A temporary price cut or coupon prompts action now, but it is a short-term incentive. Run too often, it teaches shoppers to wait for the deal and to see the discounted price as the real one, which undercuts a premium positioning. Use price promotions sparingly and for a clear purpose, such as trial at launch.

  • “Setting promotion at a percentage of sales is prudent.”

    It feels cautious, but it gets the cause and effect backwards: promotion is supposed to drive sales, not follow them. A new product has no past sales to take a percentage of, and the method cuts spending exactly when sales fall. Use it as a cross-check on an objective-and-task budget, not as the method.

  • “Integrated means the same ad everywhere.”

    Integration means one positioning and a consistent message, adapted to what each tool and medium does best. A shelf tag, a six-second video and a sales presentation can’t say the same words, but they should all say the same thing about the brand.

  • “Push and pull are alternative strategies; pick one.”

    Most launches need both. Push gets the product stocked and supported; pull gets shoppers asking for it. The balance shifts by industry and over time, but a new brand that relies on only one tends to fail at the other end of the channel.

In practice

A brand manager turns these ideas into three working documents. The first is a creative brief for the agency: who the audience is, the one thing the campaign must make them think, the evidence that supports it, and what every piece must include. Lantern’s brief would name category buyers who drink café coffee but haven’t tried ready-to-drink cold brew, the single proposition “café-quality single-origin, in a can”, and the 20-hour steep as the proof. The second is a media and activity plan that puts each task in a calendar, so that ads, sampling and stock arrive in the same weeks. The third is the trade plan agreed with each retailer: promotion windows, displays and how Lantern will support the product in store.

The budget meeting is where the objective-and-task method earns its keep. When Lantern’s finance adviser asks for a $30,000 cut, the brand manager can show which objective each cut gives up and propose the least damaging one, for example fewer impressions in the off-peak first quarter, rather than trimming every line equally. When the year’s results come in, the same task list says what to measure.

The right mix depends heavily on the business. A company selling battery-storage systems to factories and offices relies mainly on personal selling, supported by technical content and trade shows, because each sale is large, complex and made by a group of people. A university recruiting students mixes open days (events), search and social ads, and the credibility of rankings and press coverage, because applicants compare carefully over months. An airline uses fare sales and emails to fill seats that would otherwise fly empty, and advertising to keep its brand in mind for the next trip. An online clothing retailer leans on targeted digital ads and email, where each response can be tracked, and must watch that its frequent discount codes don’t become the price customers expect. In every case the same questions decide the mix: whom do we need to move, from which stage, through which part of the channel, and at what cost per contact.

Summary

  • Marketing communication passes through sender, encoding, message and medium, decoding and receiver, with feedback and noise; messages fail when they are decoded differently from what was meant or drowned out by noise.
  • The promotion mix has five tools: advertising, sales promotion (consumer and trade), personal selling, public relations, and direct and digital marketing. Reach per dollar and persuasion per contact trade off against each other.
  • Push promotion works on the channel (personal selling to buyers, trade promotions, displays); pull promotion works on final customers (advertising, consumer promotions, PR, digital). New brands need both.
  • AIDA and other hierarchy-of-effects models help set stage-by-stage objectives, but evidence doesn’t support a fixed sequence; for low-involvement products trial often comes before liking.
  • Integrated marketing communications plans the tools together around one positioning and consistent messages; it is hard because teams, partners and short-term incentives pull in different directions.
  • Budgets can be set by the affordable, percentage-of-sales, competitive-parity or objective-and-task methods; objective-and-task links money to results and makes trade-offs visible.
  • Lantern’s $120,000 first-year plan can be built task by task: $50,000 digital, $40,000 sampling and displays, $20,000 trade, $10,000 PR, which is $30,000 more than 15% of a hypothetical $600,000 forecast.

Terms introduced

Promotion mix
The combination of communication tools (advertising, sales promotion, personal selling, public relations, and direct and digital marketing) a business uses to communicate with its audiences.
Advertising
Paid, non-personal communication from an identified sponsor through mass or digital media.
Sales promotion
Short-term incentives to encourage buying or selling now, aimed at consumers (coupons, samples, price cuts) or at the trade (allowances, display payments).
Trade promotion
A sales promotion aimed at intermediaries, such as funding for a retailer’s temporary price cut or a payment for a display.
Personal selling
Person-to-person communication by a salesperson aimed at making sales and building customer relationships.
Public relations
Building good relationships with a company’s publics and earning favourable coverage, for example through press releases, events and sponsorships.
Direct and digital marketing
Communication with targeted individuals that invites a direct response, through email, direct mail, websites, apps and targeted online ads.
Push strategy
Directing promotion at channel members, through personal selling and trade promotions, so that they stock, support and sell the product.
Pull strategy
Directing promotion at final customers to create demand that draws the product through the channel.
AIDA
A response-hierarchy model in which a buyer moves through attention, interest, desire and action. Usually credited to E. St. Elmo Lewis around 1898, though the attribution is disputed.
Hierarchy-of-effects model
Any model that describes buying as a sequence of mental stages, such as awareness, knowledge, liking, preference, conviction and purchase.
Integrated marketing communications
Planning and coordinating all promotion tools so that they deliver one clear, consistent message about the brand. Abbreviated IMC.
Affordable method
Setting the promotion budget at whatever the business has left after its other costs and profit target.
Percentage-of-sales method
Setting the promotion budget as a fixed percentage of past or forecast sales.
Competitive-parity method
Setting the promotion budget to match competitors’ spending, in dollars or relative to sales.
Objective-and-task method
Setting the promotion budget by defining specific objectives, listing the tasks needed to achieve them and adding up the cost of those tasks.
