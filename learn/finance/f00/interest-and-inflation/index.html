Interest and inflation

Interest is what money earns over time; inflation is what time takes away. Learn simple and compound interest, why compounding frequency matters, how an APR differs from an effective annual rate, and how to turn a nominal return into a real one, so you can tell whether savings are really growing.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate simple and compound interest, and explain why compounding makes balances grow faster over time.
  • Calculate future values with more than one compounding period a year, and convert a quoted annual rate into an effective annual rate.
  • Distinguish an APR (a quoted annual rate) from an effective annual rate, and compare two rates quoted on different bases.
  • Explain inflation and purchasing power, and calculate an inflation rate from a price index.
  • Calculate a real return exactly and by approximation, and recognise when the approximation fails.

The idea

Fictional case

On 1 January 2026 Ana has $26,000 in a Coastline Bank savings account that pays 1.5% a year, credited once a year. It feels safe: the balance never falls. But the prices of the things she buys, from rent to coffee beans, are also rising, and she assumes they will rise by about 2.8% a year over the next two years. Two questions follow. How much does her money grow, and how fast? And is it growing faster than prices, so that it buys more next year than it does today, or more slowly, so that it buys less? The first question is about interest; the second is about inflation.

Interest: the price of time

Interest is what a borrower pays a lender for the use of money over time, usually quoted as a percentage of the principal per year. A saver who puts money in a bank account is lending to the bank, so the bank pays the saver interest. When Lantern borrows from Coastline Bank, Lantern pays the interest. The rate is the price of time: what it costs to have money now instead of later, or what you are paid for waiting.

There are two ways to calculate interest over more than one period, and the difference between them grows with time.

Simple interest
Interest is calculated on the original principal only. Each year’s interest is the same: principal × rate. Over ttt years the interest is I=P×r×tI = P \times r \times tI=P×r×t.
Compound interest
Interest is calculated on the principal and on the interest already added to the balance, so each year’s interest is larger than the last. Over nnn whole years the balance becomes P(1+r)nP(1 + r)^nP(1+r)n, the future value formula from lesson 1.

Lantern’s loan shows where simple interest arises in practice. Coastline Bank charges 6% a year on $10,000, and Lantern pays the interest every three months, $150 a time, rather than letting it build up. Because the interest is paid out as it falls due, the balance stays at $10,000 and the interest never earns interest. Over the two years Lantern pays 10,000×0.06×2=1,20010{,}000 \times 0.06 \times 2 = 1{,}20010,000×0.06×2=1,200 dollars, the same as eight payments of $150.

A savings account usually works the other way: the interest is added to the balance and left there, so next year the bank pays interest on it too. Put $1,000 in an account paying 10% a year and leave it alone. After one year it holds $1,100. In the second year the bank pays 10% on $1,100, which is $110, not $100: the extra $10 is interest on interest. After three years the balance is 1,000×1.13=1,3311{,}000 \times 1.1^3 = 1{,}3311,000×1.13=1,331 dollars, compared with 1,000+3×100=1,3001{,}000 + 3 \times 100 = 1{,}3001,000+3×100=1,300 under simple interest.

Over long periods the gap becomes large. At 5% a year, $10,000 grows to $15,000 in ten years with simple interest but to about $16,288.95 with compound interest; over thirty years the figures are $25,000 and about $43,219.42. Compounding is why starting to save early matters so much, and also why an unpaid credit-card balance can grow so quickly.

Compounding more than once a year

Interest is often added more often than once a year: every six months, every quarter, every month, even every day. The compounding frequency is how many times a year that happens. The bank quotes an annual rate, divides it by the number of periods to get the rate per period, and applies that rate each period. A 6% annual rate compounded monthly means 0.5% a month (6%÷126\% \div 126%÷12), applied twelve times.

The last column, the effective annual rate (EAR), is the rate that, compounded once a year, would give the same result. It is the honest way to compare rates quoted with different compounding.

Inflation: what time takes away

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general level of prices: not one product becoming dearer, but prices across the economy rising on average. It is measured with a price index, which tracks the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services over time. In the United States the best known is the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which the Bureau of Labor Statistics describes as a measure of the average change over time in the prices urban consumers pay for a market basket of consumer goods and services.

Inflation reduces the purchasing power of money: what a given number of dollars can buy. If prices rise by 2.8% in a year, $26,000 next year buys what about 26,000÷1.028≈25,291.8326{,}000 \div 1.028 \approx 25{,}291.8326,000÷1.028≈25,291.83 dollars buys today. The banknotes have not changed, but they stretch less far.

This is the point of Ana’s worry. Suppose she had kept all $26,000 in her account for a year. At 1.5% it would grow to 26,000×1.015=26,39026{,}000 \times 1.015 = 26{,}39026,000×1.015=26,390 dollars. But with prices 2.8% higher, $26,390 buys only what 26,390÷1.028≈25,671.2126{,}390 \div 1.028 \approx 25{,}671.2126,390÷1.028≈25,671.21 dollars buys today. In dollars she has gained $390; in what her money can buy, she has lost about $328.79.

Nominal and real returns

A nominal return is the percentage increase in the number of dollars: Ana’s 1.5%. A real return is the percentage increase in purchasing power: how much more, or less, the money can buy. The real return removes inflation from the nominal one:

1+rreal=1+rnom1+i⟹rreal=1.0151.028−1≈−1.26%1 + r_{\text{real}} = \frac{1 + r_{\text{nom}}}{1 + i} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad r_{\text{real}} = \frac{1.015}{1.028} - 1 \approx -1.26\%1+rreal​=1+i1+rnom​​⟹rreal​=1.0281.015​−1≈−1.26%

Ana’s real return is negative: her savings are losing purchasing power at about 1.26% a year. A quick approximation subtracts inflation from the nominal rate: 1.5%−2.8%=−1.3%1.5\% - 2.8\% = -1.3\%1.5%−2.8%=−1.3%. The two answers are close here because both rates are small. The approximation is easy to remember, but it is only an approximation, and the theory section shows when it goes wrong.

Key idea: Safe in dollars is not the same as safe in purchasing power

A bank deposit protects the number of dollars you have. It does not protect what those dollars can buy. Whenever the interest rate is below the rate of inflation, the real return is negative and the saver grows poorer in real terms each year, even though the balance only ever goes up.

In depth

Simple and compound interest: the formulas side by side

With principal PPP, annual rate rrr (as a decimal) and nnn whole years, simple interest gives a balance of P(1+rn)P(1 + rn)P(1+rn), which grows by the same amount each year: a straight line. Compound interest gives P(1+r)nP(1 + r)^nP(1+r)n, which grows by the same percentage each year: a curve that gets steeper. For one year the two agree. After that, compound interest is always ahead, and the gap widens every year, as the figure in “The idea” shows.

Simple interest appears wherever interest is paid out instead of added to the balance: Lantern’s loan, most bonds (which pay their interest to investors every six months or year) and some short-term loans. Compound interest appears wherever interest stays in: savings accounts, certificates of deposit that pay at maturity, unpaid credit-card balances and long-term investments whose income is reinvested.

A handy rule of thumb, the rule of 72, estimates how long compounding takes to double money: divide 72 by the percentage rate. At 6% a year money doubles in about 72÷6=1272 \div 6 = 1272÷6=12 years; the exact answer is about 11.9 years. The same rule works for prices: at 2.8% inflation, prices double in about 72÷2.8≈25.772 \div 2.8 \approx 25.772÷2.8≈25.7 years (exactly, about 25.1). It is an approximation that works well for rates between about 2% and 15%.

Compounding frequency and the effective annual rate

If a quoted annual rate rrr is compounded mmm times a year, the rate per period is r÷mr \div mr÷m and there are m×nm \times nm×n periods in nnn years, so the future value is P(1+r/m)mnP(1 + r/m)^{mn}P(1+r/m)mn. Over three years at 6%, $10,000 grows to $11,910.16 with annual compounding and to about $11,966.81 with monthly compounding (10,000×1.0053610{,}000 \times 1.005^{36}10,000×1.00536).

The effective annual rate converts any quoted rate into the equivalent once-a-year rate: EAR=(1+r/m)m−1\text{EAR} = (1 + r/m)^m - 1EAR=(1+r/m)m−1. Two rates can be compared fairly only once both are expressed as EARs. The comparison can reverse a first impression: worked example 2 shows a lower quoted rate that costs more.

APR, EAR and APY

The rate quoted on a loan is usually a nominal annual rate: the rate per period multiplied by the number of periods in a year, ignoring compounding. On loans and credit cards it is commonly called the APR (annual percentage rate). A card that charges 1.5% a month has an APR of 12×1.5%=18%12 \times 1.5\% = 18\%12×1.5%=18%, but because unpaid interest is added to the balance each month, its EAR is 1.01512−1≈19.56%1.015^{12} - 1 \approx 19.56\%1.01512−1≈19.56%. The APR understates what a balance left unpaid for a year really costs.

Standards: What the labels mean in US consumer finance

For closed-end consumer loans in the US, such as mortgages and car loans, the APR lenders must disclose under Regulation Z is broader than the interest rate: as the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau explains for mortgages, it also reflects points, broker fees and certain other charges, so it is usually higher than the interest rate. A credit card’s APR, by contrast, is its periodic rate times the number of periods in a year (Regulation Z, §1026.14), as in the 18% example above. For deposit accounts, US banks disclose an annual percentage yield (APY) under the Truth in Savings rules (Regulation DD): a percentage rate reflecting the total interest paid on an account, based on the interest rate and how often interest is compounded. An APY is therefore an effective annual rate. Other countries use other labels, such as AER (annual equivalent rate) for savings in the UK. Always check which rate a number is before comparing it with another.

This course uses the words this way: a quoted or nominal annual rate (an APR, in its simple sense) is the periodic rate times the number of periods; the effective annual rate includes compounding. F04 Mathematics of Finance develops both, including loans repaid in instalments, where APRs matter most.

Measuring inflation

A price index sets the cost of its basket in a chosen base period equal to 100 and expresses other periods relative to it. The inflation rate between two dates is the percentage change in the index, not the difference in index points. If a fictional index rises from 300.0 to 308.4 over a year, inflation is (308.4−300.0)÷300.0=2.8%(308.4 - 300.0) \div 300.0 = 2.8\%(308.4−300.0)÷300.0=2.8%, although the index rose 8.4 points. OpenStax’s Principles of Economics warns against the shortcut of subtracting index numbers for exactly this reason.

Different indexes measure different baskets. The CPI tracks what urban consumers buy. The Federal Reserve states its goal as inflation of 2% over the longer run, measured by the annual change in a different index, the price index for personal consumption expenditures (PCE). No index matches any one person’s spending exactly: Ana, who rents and buys a lot of coffee, may face a different inflation rate from the average household. That is one reason her 2.8% is an assumption, not a forecast.

Over several years inflation compounds just as interest does. At 2.8% a year for ten years, prices rise by a factor of 1.02810≈1.3181.028^{10} \approx 1.3181.02810≈1.318, about 31.8%, so $10,000 in ten years’ time would buy only what about 10,000÷1.318≈7,58710{,}000 \div 1.318 \approx 7{,}58710,000÷1.318≈7,587 dollars buys today. If Ana had left her whole $26,000 earning 1.5% for those ten years, it would have grown to about $30,174.06 in dollars but would buy only what about $22,893.00 buys today.

Real returns: the exact formula and the approximation

The exact relationship between nominal return, inflation and real return is often called the Fisher relation, after the economist Irving Fisher: (1+rnom)=(1+rreal)(1+i)(1 + r_{\text{nom}}) = (1 + r_{\text{real}})(1 + i)(1+rnom​)=(1+rreal​)(1+i). Multiplying out gives rnom=rreal+i+rreal×ir_{\text{nom}} = r_{\text{real}} + i + r_{\text{real}} \times irnom​=rreal​+i+rreal​×i. The approximation rreal≈rnom−ir_{\text{real}} \approx r_{\text{nom}} - irreal​≈rnom​−i drops the last term, the product of two small numbers.

When rates are small the dropped term is tiny and the approximation is good: at 8% nominal and 3% inflation the exact real return is 1.08÷1.03−1≈4.85%1.08 \div 1.03 - 1 \approx 4.85\%1.08÷1.03−1≈4.85%, against 5% by subtraction. When rates are large it fails badly. In an economy with 30% inflation, a deposit paying 40% has an exact real return of 1.40÷1.30−1≈7.69%1.40 \div 1.30 - 1 \approx 7.69\%1.40÷1.30−1≈7.69%, not 10%. Over long periods the annual error compounds: at 8% and 3% for 30 years the shortcut implies purchasing power multiplied by 4.32, against 4.15 exactly. Use the exact formula whenever inflation or interest rates are high, or the period is long.

The same logic gives the real interest rate on a loan, which is what borrowing really costs once inflation is taken into account. Inflation that turns out higher than expected when the rate was fixed helps borrowers: they repay in dollars that buy less than they expected. Inflation lower than expected helps lenders. Common mistakes

  • Using simple interest where interest is reinvested

    If interest stays in the account, it earns interest. Multiplying the yearly interest by the number of years (5% × 10 years = 50%) understates the growth: at 5% compounded, money grows by about 62.9% in ten years. Use simple interest only when the interest is paid out.

  • Comparing quoted rates with different compounding

    A 7.2% rate compounded monthly costs more than a 7.4% rate compounded annually. Convert both to effective annual rates before comparing, and check whether a figure is an APR, an APY or something else.

  • Dividing the annual rate by 12 but forgetting to multiply the periods

    With monthly compounding, use the monthly rate (r ÷ 12) and the number of months (12 × years). Using r ÷ 12 with the number of years, or r with the number of months, gives an answer that is far too small or far too large.

  • “My balance went up, so I am better off.”

    Only in dollars. If inflation exceeds the interest rate, as with Ana’s 1.5% account and 2.8% inflation, the money buys less each year. Judge savings by their real return.

  • Subtracting index points to get inflation

    An index rising from 300 to 308.4 has risen 8.4 points but 2.8%. Inflation is the percentage change in the index.

  • Trusting nominal − inflation when rates are high

    The shortcut drops the product of the two rates. At 40% nominal and 30% inflation it gives 10% instead of the true 7.69%. Use (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1 whenever rates are large or the period is long.

In practice

Interest and inflation run through ordinary financial decisions, at home and at work.

Setting a savings target in real terms. A financial planner helping a fictional teacher save for a home deposit in eight years starts by asking what the deposit is in today’s prices. She then plans in real terms: she projects the savings with a real return (the expected interest rate adjusted for expected inflation) and compares them with the deposit in today’s dollars, rather than mixing nominal savings growth with today’s house prices. If the account’s rate is below inflation, she points out that the teacher is losing ground every year and suggests looking at better-paying accounts, while keeping enough in easy-access savings for emergencies.

Pricing in a business. A fictional hotel signs a three-year contract to host a company’s training courses at a fixed $180 a room-night. If the hotel’s costs rise with inflation at about 3% a year, the fixed price is worth about 180÷1.032≈169.67180 \div 1.03^2 \approx 169.67180÷1.032≈169.67 dollars of today’s purchasing power at the start of the third year. The hotel’s sales manager therefore asks for a clause that raises the rate each year in line with a named price index, a common feature of long contracts, so that inflation does not quietly cut the real price.

Borrowing. The finance manager of a fictional battery-storage installer compares a fixed-rate loan with a variable-rate one. The fixed rate locks in the nominal cost; if inflation rises unexpectedly, the real cost falls, but if inflation falls, the business is left paying a high rate in real terms. She compares the offers on an effective-annual-rate basis, including fees, and then asks what the real rate would be under several inflation assumptions. Lenders do the same arithmetic from the other side, which is why rates tend to rise when expected inflation rises.

Summary

  • Interest is the price of using money over time. Simple interest is paid on the principal only (I = P × r × t); compound interest is paid on the principal and on earlier interest (FV = P(1 + r)^n).
  • Compounding makes balances grow faster every year; the gap from simple interest is small at first and large over long periods.
  • With m compounding periods a year, FV = P(1 + r/m)^(mn). More frequent compounding pays slightly more, with diminishing effect.
  • A quoted annual rate (an APR in its simple sense) ignores compounding; the effective annual rate includes it. Compare rates only as EARs. US deposit APYs are EARs; a US card’s APR is its periodic rate × periods, while APRs on US mortgages and car loans also include certain fees.
  • Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, measured as the percentage change in a price index such as the CPI. It erodes purchasing power.
  • Real return = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. Ana’s 1.5% against 2.8% inflation is about −1.26% a year: her balance rises while its purchasing power falls.
  • Nominal − inflation is a good approximation only when both rates are small; at 40% and 30% it gives 10% instead of 7.69%.

Terms introduced

Simple interest
Interest calculated on the original principal only, so it is the same every period: principal × rate × time.
Compound interest
Interest calculated on the principal and on interest already added to the balance, so the balance grows by the same percentage each period.
Compounding frequency
How many times a year interest is calculated and added to the balance: annually, semi-annually, quarterly, monthly or daily.
Effective annual rate
The interest rate that, compounded once a year, gives the same result as a quoted rate with more frequent compounding: (1 + r/m)^m − 1. Often shortened to EAR.
APR
Annual percentage rate. In its simple sense, a periodic rate multiplied by the number of periods in a year, ignoring compounding; a US credit card’s APR is calculated this way. For closed-end US consumer loans, such as mortgages and car loans, the disclosed APR is broader and also reflects certain fees.
Annual percentage yield
The rate US banks disclose on deposit accounts under the Truth in Savings rules, reflecting the total interest paid on the account including compounding. An effective annual rate. Often shortened to APY.
Inflation
A sustained rise in the general level of prices, measured as the percentage change in a price index.
Price index
A number tracking the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services over time, set to 100 in a base period.
Consumer Price Index
The best-known US price index, published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics: a measure of the average change over time in the prices urban consumers pay for a basket of goods and services. Often shortened to CPI.
Purchasing power
What a given amount of money can buy. Inflation reduces it.
Nominal return
The percentage change in the number of dollars an investment is worth, before allowing for inflation.
Real return
The percentage change in the purchasing power of an investment: (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1.
Real interest rate
An interest rate adjusted for inflation: what borrowing really costs, or lending really earns, in purchasing power.
