Value and valuation words

Book value, market value, market capitalisation, enterprise value, EBITDA and the multiples built from them: the words analysts use to say what a company is worth. Learn to calculate each one for Harbourline Roasters, to say what it leaves out, and to see why every valuation rests on assumptions.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Distinguish book value from market value, and explain why the two usually differ.
  • Calculate market capitalisation and enterprise value, and explain why enterprise value adds debt and subtracts cash.
  • Calculate EBIT and EBITDA from an income statement, and explain what EBITDA leaves out.
  • Calculate and interpret the EV/EBITDA and P/E multiples, matching each numerator with the right measure of earnings.
  • Estimate a value range from peer multiples and explain how the result depends on the assumptions chosen.

The idea

Fictional case

In February 2026, a few weeks before Lantern opens, Ana reads the annual report of Harbourline Roasters Inc., a listed coffee roaster that could one day supply her beans. One page says Harbourline’s shareholders’ equity is $300 million. A financial news site says the company is worth $765 million. A second site, comparing coffee companies, lists Harbourline at $930 million and “10.0× EBITDA”. All three are about the same company on the same day. None of them is a mistake: each answers a different question, and this lesson is about telling the questions apart.

A valuation is an estimate of what something is worth: a share, a whole company, a building, a café. Finance has a small vocabulary for doing it, and the words are used precisely. Some describe what the accounts record; some describe what the market is paying; some describe how much a business earns; and some, called multiples, relate a price to earnings so that companies of different sizes can be compared.

Book value and market value

The book value of equity is the shareholders’ equity recorded in the balance sheet: total assets minus total liabilities, measured by the accounting rules. Harbourline’s is $300 million (from the balance sheet in the previous lesson: assets of $660 million minus liabilities of $360 million). Accountants also speak of the book value of a single asset, meaning its cost less the depreciation recorded so far; in this lesson “book value” means book value of equity unless stated otherwise.

The market value of something is what buyers and sellers are paying for it now. For a listed company the market value of its equity is called its market capitalisation (market cap): the share price times the number of shares outstanding. Harbourline has 18 million shares trading at $42.50, so its market capitalisation is 42.50×18=76542.50 \times 18 = 76542.50×18=765 million dollars. Investor.gov defines it in the same way: the current market price of one share multiplied by the total number of shares outstanding.

Why is the market paying $765 million for equity the accounts record at $300 million? Because the two numbers look in different directions. The balance sheet mostly records what assets cost when they were bought, and leaves out valuable things the business built itself, such as its brand, its relationships with growers and the skills of its roasters. The share price reflects what investors expect the company to earn in the future, discounted for time and risk. Their ratio, the market-to-book ratio, is 765÷300=2.55765 \div 300 = 2.55765÷300=2.55: investors pay $2.55 for every dollar of recorded equity. Per share, book value is 300÷18≈16.67300 \div 18 \approx 16.67300÷18≈16.67 dollars against a price of $42.50.

Note: Market value is a price, not a fact about the company

Market capitalisation changes every time the share price does, often many times a day, while the business itself has not changed. It tells you what the marginal buyer and seller agreed to pay for a share at that moment. For a widely traded company it is often treated as the best available estimate of what the equity is worth, but it is still an estimate made by people with expectations.

Enterprise value: the price of the whole business

Market capitalisation prices only the shares. Suppose you wanted to buy all of Harbourline’s operations: its roasting plants, its brand, its customers. Buying every share would cost $765 million, but you would also take on its $210 million of debt, which the lenders will still expect to be repaid. In exchange, you would get the $45 million of cash in its bank accounts, which you could use straight away to pay part of that debt. The net cost of the business is its enterprise value (EV):

EV=765+210−45=930 ($ millions)\text{EV} = 765 + 210 - 45 = 930 \ \text{(\$ millions)}EV=765+210−45=930 ($ millions)

Think of buying a house that comes with a $200,000 mortgage you must take over and $5,000 left in a drawer. If you pay the seller $300,000, the house really costs you 300,000+200,000−5,000=495,000300{,}000 + 200{,}000 - 5{,}000 = 495{,}000300,000+200,000−5,000=495,000 dollars. Enterprise value applies the same logic to a company. The debt minus the cash, $165 million for Harbourline, is called net debt, so enterprise value is also market capitalisation plus net debt.

The figure shows the second way to read enterprise value: it is the value of the operating business, which is shared between everyone who financed it. Lenders and shareholders both have claims on it; cash is subtracted because it is not part of the operations and could be paid out tomorrow without changing what the business does. This is why enterprise value is the natural measure when comparing companies that are financed differently.

Measures of earnings: EBIT, D&A and EBITDA

To judge whether a price is high or low, you compare it with what the business earns. An income statement subtracts costs from revenue in stages, and finance gives names to some of the stages. EBIT (earnings before interest and taxes) is what the business earns before paying its lenders and the government. For a company with no income or costs outside its operations, such as Harbourline, EBIT equals operating profit; otherwise the two differ. D&A is depreciation and amortisation: depreciation spreads the cost of physical assets such as roasting machines over the years they are used, and amortisation does the same for intangible assets such as purchased software licences or customer contracts. Neither is a cash payment in the year it is recorded; the cash left when the asset was bought. EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation) adds D&A back to EBIT.

Multiples: price compared with earnings

A valuation multiple divides a measure of value by a measure of what the business earns, so that a large company and a small one can be compared. Two are used everywhere.

  • EV/EBITDA divides enterprise value by EBITDA. Harbourline: 930÷93=10.0930 \div 93 = 10.0930÷93=10.0. The whole business is priced at ten times one year’s EBITDA.
  • The P/E ratio (price-to-earnings) divides market capitalisation by net income, or equivalently the share price by earnings per share (EPS). Harbourline’s EPS is 38÷18≈2.1138 \div 18 \approx 2.1138÷18≈2.11 dollars, and its P/E is 765÷38≈20.1765 \div 38 \approx 20.1765÷38≈20.1. Shareholders are paying about twenty times one year’s earnings.

Key idea: Match the top and the bottom

A multiple must compare like with like. Enterprise value belongs to lenders and shareholders together, so it is divided by measures before interest (EBITDA, EBIT or revenue), which also belong to both. Market capitalisation belongs to shareholders only, so it is divided by net income, which is what is left for shareholders after the lenders have been paid.

In depth

Why enterprise value adds debt and subtracts cash: the careful version

Enterprise value aims to measure the value of a company’s operations, independent of how they are financed. Two companies with identical roasting plants, customers and costs should have the same enterprise value (ignoring the tax saving on interest) even if one borrowed heavily to build its plants and the other used shareholders’ money. Their market capitalisations will differ, because in the borrowing company the lenders own a larger slice of the same pie, but adding the debt back puts them on the same footing.

Cash is subtracted because cash is not an operating asset in this sense. A company holding $45 million of spare cash is worth $45 million more to its shareholders than an otherwise identical one without it, and that $45 million says nothing about how good its roasting business is. Strictly, analysts subtract only cash not needed to run the business day to day, and they add other claims on the business besides ordinary shares, such as preferred stock, lease obligations and the share of subsidiaries owned by outside shareholders (noncontrolling interests). Those refinements are left to later courses; the core idea is debt in, spare cash out.

What EBITDA leaves out

EBITDA is popular because it is a rough proxy for the cash the operations generate before interest, tax, replacement spending and working-capital needs, and because it is unaffected by financing, tax and depreciation choices. Those same features are its limits. Each letter in the name is something the business really does have to pay or replace.

  • Depreciation and amortisation stand for real spending. D&A is not a cash payment this year, but machines wear out and must be replaced. Harbourline spends about $35 million a year on new roasting equipment (its capital expenditure, from the course case). Its EBITDA of $93 million overstates what the business can pay out year after year; after that spending, 93−35=5893 - 35 = 5893−35=58 million dollars remain.
  • Interest and tax are paid in cash. A business with high EBITDA and heavy debt can still be unable to pay its lenders. That is why lenders look at interest payments as well.
  • Working capital needs cash. A growing roaster must buy more green coffee and wait longer for customers to pay. EBITDA ignores that money tied up in current assets.
  • EBITDA is not a GAAP measure. Neither US GAAP nor IFRS defines it, so companies can calculate it differently. Many report an “adjusted EBITDA” that adds back costs management calls unusual. The SEC staff’s guidance says a measure calculated differently from net income plus interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation should not be called EBITDA, and should carry a different title such as “Adjusted EBITDA”.

None of this makes EBITDA useless: it is a reasonable starting point for comparing similar businesses. It does make EBITDA a poor stand-in for cash flow or profit on its own. A careful analyst always asks what each company’s capital spending and working capital needs are, and how any adjusted figure was built.

Valuation depends on assumptions

There are two broad ways to estimate what a company is worth. Relative valuation uses multiples: find similar companies (comparables, or comps), see what multiples the market pays for them, and apply those multiples to the company being valued. Intrinsic valuation forecasts the cash the business will generate and discounts it to today using the time value of money from lesson 1; F05 Corporate Finance teaches that method. Both rest on judgements.

For relative valuation the judgements include which companies count as comparable, which year’s earnings to use (last year’s, called trailing, or next year’s forecast, called forward), whether to use reported or adjusted earnings, and where in the peers’ range the company deserves to sit. OpenStax’s Principles of Finance stresses that a single multiple means little without comparison to peers or history, that it is a snapshot in time, and that analysts should combine several measures. Worked example 2 shows how far the answer moves when the assumptions do.

For intrinsic valuation the judgements are the forecasts themselves (sales growth, margins, spending) and the discount rate. Small changes in either can move the answer a long way. Neither method produces the value of a company; each produces an estimate conditional on stated assumptions. A good valuation states those assumptions openly, so that a reader can disagree with them and see what happens to the answer.

Caution: Low multiple does not mean cheap

A company can trade at a low multiple because the market expects its earnings to fall, because it is riskier, or because its earnings this year are unusually high. A high multiple can reflect strong expected growth. A multiple is a summary of expectations, not a verdict; before calling a share cheap or expensive, ask what the market might know or expect that the multiple hides.

Common mistakes

  • “Book value is what the company is worth.”

    Book value is what the accounts record, mostly at historical cost, and it leaves out much of what makes a business valuable, such as its brand and customer relationships. Harbourline’s book equity is $300 million while the market values its shares at $765 million. Neither is “the” value, but book value is rarely a good estimate of what a buyer would pay. Some texts call a share trading above book value ‘overvalued’; for most healthy firms market value exceeds book because the balance sheet omits internally built value.

  • “Enterprise value subtracts debt, because debt reduces value.”

    It is the other way round. Enterprise value adds debt to market capitalisation, because a buyer of the whole business takes on the debt as well as buying the shares. It is when you go from enterprise value back to the value of the shares that you subtract debt (and add cash).

  • “EBITDA is the company’s cash flow.”

    EBITDA ignores the cash spent replacing equipment, the cash tied up in growing inventory and receivables, interest and tax. A business can report strong EBITDA and still run short of cash. Treat EBITDA as a starting point for comparing similar businesses, not as cash in the bank.

  • Mixing enterprise and equity measures in one multiple

    Dividing market capitalisation by EBITDA, or enterprise value by net income, compares numbers that belong to different groups of investors. The first makes heavily indebted companies look cheap; the second makes them look expensive. Pair EV with measures before interest, and market capitalisation with net income.

  • “A P/E of 12 is cheaper than a P/E of 20.”

    Only if everything else is equal, and it rarely is. A low P/E may reflect expected decline, higher risk or a one-off boost to this year’s earnings; a high one may reflect strong expected growth. P/E also depends on how much debt a company carries. Compare multiples between genuinely similar companies, and ask why they differ.

  • “The model gave the value.”

    Every valuation, by multiples or by forecasts, is the output of assumptions: which peers, which year’s earnings, which adjustments, which growth rate. Change them and the value changes, often by a lot. State the assumptions, and show what the answer would be under reasonable alternatives.

In practice

Valuation words appear wherever ownership of a business changes hands or money is lent against it. Here is how three professionals use them.

An equity analyst at a fund deciding whether to buy Harbourline shares builds a table of listed coffee and food companies with their market capitalisations, enterprise values, EV/EBITDA and P/E. She removes companies whose businesses differ too much (a café chain earns money differently from a roaster), checks how each company defines any adjusted figure, and notes which companies carry heavy debt, since their P/E ratios are not comparable with Harbourline’s. Harbourline’s 10.0× EV/EBITDA turns out to be in the middle of its peers. Whether that is attractive depends on her view of its growth and spending, which she sets out in a short note with the assumptions stated, so her colleagues can challenge them.

A corporate banker lending to a company looks at the same vocabulary from the lender’s side. Banks often express how much a company has borrowed as a multiple of its EBITDA, and loan agreements often limit it. On the lesson’s figures, Harbourline’s debt is 210÷93≈2.3210 \div 93 \approx 2.3210÷93≈2.3 times its EBITDA, and its net debt 165÷93≈1.8165 \div 93 \approx 1.8165÷93≈1.8 times. The banker also looks at enterprise value: debt of $210 million against a business valued at $930 million leaves a large cushion of shareholders’ value that would be lost before the lenders lost anything.

Fictional case

Ana uses the words too, on a small scale. In five years, if Lantern is profitable, a larger coffee or catering company might offer to buy it. The buyer would probably value Lantern at a multiple of its EBITDA, subtract Lantern’s debt, add its cash, and offer the rest to Ana for her shares. Knowing that, Ana can see now which decisions would raise that price (steady, well-documented earnings; equipment kept in good order; customer contracts that would survive a change of owner) and why her $20,000 of book equity may turn out to be worth much more, or less, than $20,000.

In each case the same discipline applies: calculate the measures correctly, pair numerators with the right denominators, know what each measure leaves out, and treat every value as conditional on its assumptions.

Summary

  • Book value of equity is what the accounts record (assets minus liabilities); market value is what investors are paying now. Harbourline: $300 million book, $765 million market, a market-to-book ratio of 2.55.
  • Market capitalisation is share price times shares outstanding: the market value of the equity.
  • Enterprise value = market cap + debt − cash: the net cost of buying the whole operating business, shared between lenders and shareholders. Harbourline: $930 million.
  • EBIT is earnings before interest and tax (equal to operating profit for Harbourline); EBITDA adds back depreciation and amortisation. Harbourline: $61 million and $93 million.
  • EBITDA is not cash flow and not a GAAP measure: it ignores replacement spending, working capital, interest and tax, and adjusted versions vary.
  • Pair EV with measures before interest (EV/EBITDA, 10.0× for Harbourline) and market cap with net income (P/E, about 20.1×). EV multiples compare differently financed firms more fairly.
  • Every valuation depends on assumptions: peers, earnings definitions, forecasts and discount rates. State them and show how the answer changes.

Terms introduced

Valuation
An estimate of what an asset, share or business is worth, made with stated methods and assumptions.
Book value of equity
Shareholders’ equity as recorded in the balance sheet: total assets minus total liabilities, measured by the accounting rules.
Market value
What buyers and sellers are paying for something now. For a listed company’s equity it is the market capitalisation.
Market capitalisation
Share price multiplied by the number of shares outstanding: the market value of a company’s equity. Often shortened to market cap.
Market-to-book ratio
Market capitalisation divided by the book value of equity (or price per share divided by book value per share). Also called price-to-book.
Enterprise value
Market capitalisation plus debt minus cash: the value of a company’s operating business, claimed jointly by its lenders and shareholders. Often shortened to EV.
Net debt
Debt minus cash. Enterprise value equals market capitalisation plus net debt.
EBIT
Earnings before interest and taxes: profit before paying lenders and tax. For a company with no income or costs outside its operations, EBIT equals operating profit; otherwise the two differ.
D&A
Depreciation and amortisation: the part of the cost of long-lived physical and intangible assets charged as an expense in the period.
Amortisation
The spreading of the cost of an intangible asset, such as purchased software or a customer contract, over the years it is used. The word is also used for the gradual repayment of a loan’s principal.
EBITDA
Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation: EBIT plus D&A. A widely used but non-GAAP measure of operating earnings.
Valuation multiple
A ratio of a measure of value (such as enterprise value or market capitalisation) to a measure of earnings or size (such as EBITDA or net income), used to compare companies.
EV/EBITDA
Enterprise value divided by EBITDA: a multiple that compares differently financed companies on the same footing.
Earnings per share
Net income divided by the number of shares outstanding. Often shortened to EPS.
P/E ratio
Price-to-earnings ratio: share price divided by earnings per share, or market capitalisation divided by net income.
