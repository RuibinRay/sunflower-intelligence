Capital structure

Does borrowing make a business more valuable? Work through Modigliani and Miller’s answer without and with taxes, then the costs of financial distress, the trade-off and pecking-order theories, agency costs and covenants, and what finance directors say they actually do.

About 85 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Compare debt and equity as claims on a business: priority, control, taxes, covenants and flexibility.
  • Calculate how financial leverage changes earnings per share and return on equity, and find the EBIT at which two financing plans give the same EPS.
  • Apply Modigliani and Miller’s propositions without taxes, and explain the homemade-leverage argument behind them.
  • Calculate the present value of the interest tax shield, the levered firm’s value VL=VU+TDV_L = V_U + T DVL​=VU​+TD, and the cost of equity with corporate tax.
  • Explain the costs of financial distress, the trade-off and pecking-order theories, and the agency costs of debt and equity, and evaluate them against survey evidence.

Review first

  • The weighted average cost of capital — The cost of equity, the after-tax cost of debt and the WACC are the rates whose behaviour this lesson explains.
  • Solvency and leverage — Leverage and coverage ratios, and Lantern’s 2027 debt, are measured there.
  • Governance and the agency problem — Agency costs, risk shifting and covenants are introduced there; this lesson adds what they mean for how much to borrow.
  • Perpetuities and growth — A permanent tax saving is valued as a perpetuity.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana has financed Lantern in three ways so far: her own $20,000 of shares, profit kept in the business, and borrowing (Coastline Bank’s loans, the line of credit, the brewer’s instalment plan and, if you count them, the cafés’ leases). At the end of 2027 its interest-bearing debt was $65,250 against equity of $62,818, a debt-to-equity ratio of 1.04. In module 3 she assumed a long-run debt share of 20% of Lantern’s value to work out a cost of capital. Where should a target like that come from? Is debt cheap money, as it looks, or does borrowing only move risk around?

A firm’s capital structure is the mix of debt and equity it uses to finance its assets. The question of this lesson is whether that mix changes how much the business is worth. The assets, the coffee, the cafés and the customers, produce the cash. Debt and equity are two ways of dividing that cash among the people who provided the money. Finance theory’s first answer is that the division should not matter; its later answers say why, in practice, it does.

Because lenders are paid first, debt is safer than equity and its required return is lower: in module 3 a lender would charge Lantern 7.5% while its owners need about 12.8%. That makes debt look cheap. The central lesson of capital-structure theory is that the look is misleading: every dollar of debt makes the remaining equity riskier, and the owners’ required return rises to match.

Leverage magnifies earnings per share

Brookvale Bakeries, a fictional listed chain, has no debt. Its 100,000 shares trade at $20, so the firm is worth $2,000,000. Its earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT) depend on the economy: $120,000 in a recession, $240,000 in a normal year, $360,000 in a boom, and the same pattern is expected for ever. Ignore taxes for now. The board is considering a recapitalisation: borrow $800,000 at 8% for good and use it to buy back 40,000 shares at $20. The assets and the EBIT are unchanged; only the financing differs.

Above it, borrowed money earns more than it costs and the shareholders keep the difference; below it they make up the shortfall. This is the leverage F03 measured with ratios, seen from the shareholders’ side.

Key idea: Higher expected EPS is not higher value

Leverage raised Brookvale’s expected EPS and also its risk. Whether the shares are worth more depends on whether the extra expected return is more than enough to pay for the extra risk. Modigliani and Miller showed that, in a market without taxes or other frictions, it is exactly enough and no more.

In depth

Modigliani and Miller without taxes

Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller, who both later received the Nobel prize in economics, asked what capital structure does to value in a perfect capital market: no taxes, no costs of bankruptcy or of issuing securities, investors and firms borrowing at the same rate, everyone with the same information, and the firm’s investment decisions fixed. Their answer comes in two propositions.

Proposition I. The value of a firm does not depend on its capital structure: VL=VUV_L = V_UVL​=VU​, where VLV_LVL​ is the value of the firm with debt (levered) and VUV_UVU​ its value without (unlevered). The value comes from the assets’ cash flows and their risk; dividing the cash flows between lenders and shareholders does not change their total.

The argument is that investors can borrow on their own account, which is called homemade leverage. Suppose levered Brookvale’s equity is fairly priced at $1,200,000. An investor with $12,000 can buy 1% of it (600 shares) and receive 1% of EBIT minus the interest, or she can buy 1% of the unlevered Brookvale (1,000 shares, $20,000) with her $12,000 and $8,000 borrowed at 8%:

Payoff ($)RecessionNormalBoom
1% of the levered firm’s equity: 1% × (EBIT − 64,000)5601,7602,960
1% of the unlevered firm (1% × EBIT) less 8% on $8,0005601,7602,960

Both cost the investor $12,000 of her own money.

The payoffs are identical in every state of the economy, so the two positions must cost the same. If levered shares sold for more, investors would make their own leverage with unlevered shares instead and nobody would pay the premium; if they sold for less, investors would buy them and “unlever” by lending. Any capital structure a firm can choose, an investor can copy or undo at no cost, so a firm cannot create value by choosing one.

Let rUr_UrU​ be the return investors require on the assets (the cost of capital of the unlevered firm), rDr_DrD​ the cost of debt and D/ED/ED/E the debt-to-equity ratio at market values. Proposition II is why “debt is cheaper” is not an argument for borrowing. The owners of levered Brookvale are not wrong to want 14.67% rather than 12%: their shares now carry all of the assets’ risk on a smaller base of equity. Module 3’s relevered beta says the same thing in CAPM’s language.

Note: Why a theory that “doesn’t hold” matters

No real market is perfect, so no one believes Proposition I literally. Its value is as a checklist: if capital structure does change value, it must be through something the propositions assume away, such as taxes, the costs of financial distress, information that managers have and investors don’t, or the incentives debt creates. The rest of the lesson takes those in turn.

Taken alone, the tax shield says a firm should borrow almost everything.

Three cautions keep the shield in proportion. It is worth T×DT \times DT×D only if the debt is permanent and the firm always has taxable profit to deduct the interest from; a firm with losses, or whose interest exceeds its EBIT, saves nothing on the extra interest. Many tax systems now cap the interest a company may deduct, so check the current rules before counting on it. And investors pay personal tax too: in many systems interest income is taxed more heavily than share returns, which shrinks the advantage, though in a survey of US finance directors only 4.5% said investors’ taxes were important to their debt decisions (Graham & Harvey, 2002).

The costs of financial distress

Financial distress is the condition of a firm that has trouble meeting its obligations to lenders. Its costs fall into two groups. Direct costs are the fees of a bankruptcy or a restructuring: lawyers, accountants, consultants, appraisers, auctioneers. Indirect costs are usually larger and start well before any bankruptcy: customers stop buying a product they fear will not be supported, suppliers demand cash on delivery, good staff leave, managers spend their time negotiating with banks instead of running the business, and assets are sold quickly at low prices.

Distress also changes owners’ incentives, as F00’s lesson on governance and agency showed. With the firm near default, shareholders gain from risky gambles (risk shifting), because the lenders bear most of a failure. They also lose interest in safe, valuable projects that would mainly rescue the lenders: if a new investment’s payoff would go largely to repaying existing debt, the shareholders who must fund it may turn it down. This is debt overhang, an underinvestment cost of debt. Lenders anticipate all of this and charge for it in advance, so the firm, and therefore its shareholders, bear the expected costs of distress through a higher cost of debt.

The trade-off theory

The trade-off theory puts the two forces together. Each extra dollar of debt adds tax shield; it also adds to the expected costs of distress, slowly at low debt and quickly as default becomes likely. The value of the firm is

The distress-cost assumption is invented for illustration; real distress costs cannot be read off a formula. With it, value peaks at debt of $375,000 (firm value $1,546,875, a debt share of 24.2%).

The trade-off theory predicts a target debt ratio for each firm, and predicts which firms should borrow more: those with steady, taxable profits and tangible assets that keep their value in distress (property, vehicles, utilities’ networks) can borrow more; those whose value lies in growth opportunities, brands, research or people, which melt away in distress, should borrow less. Ana’s assumed 20% for Lantern in module 3 is a judgement of this kind. The theory leaves puzzles too. Myers (1984) noted that it fits observed debt ratios only loosely, and that the most profitable firms in an industry, which have the most taxable profit to shield, often borrow the least.

The pecking order

The pecking order theory starts from asymmetric information: managers know more about their firm’s prospects than outside investors do. Investors know that managers acting for existing shareholders prefer to sell new shares when the shares are overpriced, so they read an equity issue as bad news and mark the price down; on average, a firm’s share price falls when it announces a share issue. Debt is less exposed to this, because its value depends less on what managers know. Myers (1984), drawing on his work with Nicholas Majluf, set out the resulting order of finance:

  1. Use internal funds first: retained profit and cash in hand. Dividends are set with investment needs in mind but are sticky, so internal funds rise and fall with profits.
  2. If outside money is needed, issue the safest security first: debt.
  3. Then hybrid securities such as convertible bonds.
  4. New shares only as a last resort.

In this view there is no target debt ratio. A firm’s leverage is the accumulated record of its need for outside money: a very profitable firm with few investment needs ends up with little debt, which is the pattern the trade-off theory struggles to explain. Myers reported that, for US non-financial companies over 1973–1982, internally generated cash covered on average 62% of capital spending, and net new share issues were never more than 6% of external financing.

Lantern’s first two years follow the order, for a small-firm reason as well as an information one: Ana kept most of her profit in the business, borrowed from her bank and a supplier, and sold no shares to anyone else, which would have meant sharing ownership and control. Many owner-managed firms finance the same way.

Agency costs: debt as discipline and as temptation

F00 described two agency relationships. Between managers and shareholders, the danger is that managers with spare cash spend it on growth for its own sake or on comforts; debt helps, because interest and repayments must be paid, leaving less spare cash and bringing in lenders who watch. Between shareholders and lenders, debt creates the risk shifting and underinvestment described above, and the temptation to pay out cash to shareholders before the lenders are paid. Debt therefore reduces one set of agency costs and creates another, and a firm’s best capital structure balances these as well as taxes and distress.

Lenders protect themselves with covenants. Affirmative covenants require things: audited accounts each year, insurance, upkeep of the assets. Negative covenants forbid things without the lender’s consent: more borrowing, selling major assets, paying dividends above a limit, merging. Financial covenants set ratios: a minimum interest coverage or current ratio, a maximum debt to EBITDA. A maintenance covenant must hold at every test date; an incurrence covenant is tested only when the firm takes an action, such as new borrowing. Breaking a covenant is a default that lets the lender demand repayment, so in practice it starts a renegotiation. Covenants make debt cheaper, because they limit the behaviour lenders would otherwise charge for, at the cost of the firm’s flexibility.

What finance directors say they do

Graham and Harvey surveyed 392 chief financial officers of US companies and published the results in 2002. On debt policy, the factor most often cited was financial flexibility, keeping room to borrow when opportunities arise, followed by the firm’s credit rating; they were not mainly trying to minimise their WACC. About 45% rated the tax advantage of interest as important or very important, but only 21.4% said the same of the costs of bankruptcy or distress. Of the firms, 44% had a strict or somewhat strict target debt ratio or range, 37% a flexible target and 19% none. On the pecking order the evidence was mixed: 46.8% said insufficient internal funds was an important reason to issue debt. Of CFOs who had seriously considered issuing shares, 69% named dilution of earnings per share as important, and about two-thirds said they were reluctant to issue shares they thought undervalued.

Read together, the theories are partial models rather than rivals with one winner. Taxes push towards debt; distress costs and lost flexibility push back; information problems make outside equity expensive; and agency costs cut both ways. Which force dominates depends on the firm: its taxes, the safety and tangibility of its assets, how much its managers know that investors don’t, and how much spare cash it generates.

Common mistakes

  • “Debt is cheaper than equity, so borrowing more lowers the cost of capital.”

    Debt’s required return is lower, but each dollar of debt raises the cost of equity (Proposition II). Without taxes the two effects cancel exactly; with tax the WACC falls only by the value of the tax shield, and distress costs push it back up beyond some point.

  • “Leverage raised EPS, so it raised the share price.”

    Brookvale’s expected EPS rose from $2.40 to $2.93 with no change in value: the extra expected earnings are payment for extra risk. A plan that raises EPS can lower the share price, and one that lowers EPS can raise it.

  • “The tax shield is always worth T × D.”

    T × D assumes permanent debt and a firm that can always use the deduction. Debt that is repaid in five years has five years of shields; a firm with losses or with interest above its EBIT gets no shield on the extra interest; interest-deduction caps can limit it further.

  • “Financial distress means bankruptcy fees.”

    Legal and advisory fees are the direct costs. The larger costs are indirect and start before any default: lost customers and suppliers’ credit, departing staff, fire sales, and owners who gamble or refuse good projects because the gains would go to the lenders.

  • “One capital-structure theory is right and the others are wrong.”

    Each is a model of one force: taxes, distress, information or incentives. Survey evidence supports parts of several: CFOs value flexibility and ratings, many have targets, and many also prefer internal funds and avoid issuing undervalued shares.

A finance director sets a target. The chief financial officer of a fictional listed food company works out how much debt her firm can carry in a bad year with its covenants and a credit rating she wants to keep, rather than how much would minimise the WACC on paper. She sets a range for net debt to EBITDA, holds the bottom of it in good years so that the firm can borrow quickly for an acquisition, and uses buybacks to stop debt falling far below the range. That is the trade-off theory with flexibility added, which is how the CFOs in Graham and Harvey’s survey describe their own policies.

An analyst reads a leveraged recapitalisation. When a fictional retailer announces it will borrow to buy back a fifth of its shares, an analyst separates the effects. Expected EPS will rise mechanically; that is not news. The tax shield adds value if the retailer has steady taxable profit. Against that, the retailer’s value lies in its brand and its leases, which would lose value fast in distress, so the expected distress cost is high. She also asks why managers chose this moment: a buyback can signal that they think the shares are undervalued, the mirror image of the pecking order’s equity issue.

A small private firm. For a business like Lantern, the questions are the same but the answers are shaped by size. A bank lends against tangible assets and steady cash flow, with covenants; outside equity means sharing ownership, control and information with an investor who will price the uncertainty. That is why small firms lean on retained profit, bank debt and leases, and why the cost of capital estimated in module 3 includes a premium for a small private firm. Any decision about how much to borrow, though, should be tested as Winterbourne was: in a bad year, against the covenants.

Summary

  • Capital structure is the mix of debt and equity. Debt has a fixed, prior claim, tax-deductible interest and covenants; equity has the residual claim and control.
  • Leverage raises expected EPS and ROE when the assets earn more than the interest rate, and widens their swings. Brookvale’s plans tie at EBIT of $160,000.
  • Modigliani–Miller without taxes: firm value and the WACC do not depend on capital structure (homemade leverage); the cost of equity rises with D/E: rE=rU+(rU−rD)D/Er_E = r_U + (r_U - r_D)D/ErE​=rU​+(rU​−rD​)D/E.
  • With corporate tax and permanent debt, VL=VU+TDV_L = V_U + TDVL​=VU​+TD and rE=rU+(rU−rD)(1−T)D/Er_E = r_U + (r_U - r_D)(1 - T)D/ErE​=rU​+(rU​−rD​)(1−T)D/E; the WACC falls as debt rises. Brookvale: $1,600,000, 13% and 11.25%.
  • The costs of financial distress, direct and indirect, including risk shifting and debt overhang, offset the tax shield: the trade-off theory gives a target debt level.
  • The pecking order: with asymmetric information, firms use internal funds, then debt, then equity as a last resort, and have no target ratio.
  • Debt disciplines managers but creates conflicts with lenders, which covenants control. Surveyed CFOs put financial flexibility and credit ratings first.

Terms introduced

Homemade leverage
Borrowing or lending by investors on their own account to copy or undo a firm’s capital structure; the argument behind Modigliani and Miller’s Proposition I.
Modigliani–Miller propositions
Proposition I: in a perfect capital market a firm’s value does not depend on its capital structure. Proposition II: the cost of equity rises with the debt-to-equity ratio, so the weighted-average cost of capital stays the same. With corporate tax, the value rises by the present value of the interest tax shield.
Interest tax shield
The tax a firm saves because interest is deductible: the tax rate times the interest paid. For permanent debt its present value is the tax rate times the debt.
Financial distress
The condition of a firm that has difficulty meeting its obligations to lenders. Its costs include direct costs (legal and advisory fees in bankruptcy or restructuring) and indirect costs (lost customers, suppliers and staff, forced asset sales, distorted investment).
Debt overhang
Underinvestment by a firm with heavy debt: shareholders turn down a valuable project because much of its payoff would go to existing lenders.
Trade-off theory
The theory that a firm chooses its debt level by balancing the tax advantage of debt against the expected costs of financial distress, giving a target debt ratio.
Pecking order theory
The theory that, because managers know more than investors, firms prefer internal funds, then debt, and issue new equity only as a last resort, so they have no target debt ratio.
Asymmetric information
A situation in which one party to a transaction, such as a firm’s managers, knows more than the other, such as outside investors.
