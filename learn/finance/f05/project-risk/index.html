Project risk

An NPV is only as good as the forecasts behind it. Learn to find the assumptions that matter with sensitivity analysis and a tornado chart, to test combinations with scenarios, to calculate accounting, cash and financial break-even volumes, and to see what simulation and real options add, and where they mislead.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate a project’s NPV with each input moved in turn (sensitivity analysis), and interpret the result as a tornado chart.
  • Build pessimistic, base and optimistic scenarios and calculate an expected NPV, explaining what scenarios add to sensitivity analysis.
  • Calculate the accounting, cash and financial (NPV) break-even volumes of a project, and the degree of operating leverage, and interpret them.
  • Explain what Monte Carlo simulation adds to scenario analysis and the dangers of false precision and ignored correlations.
  • Identify options to expand, abandon and wait in a project, and value a simple option to abandon.
  • Explain why raising the discount rate for every worry double-counts risk, and where each kind of risk belongs.

Review first

  • Project cash flows — Every test in this lesson reruns Cold Brew’s free-cash-flow model built there.
  • Net present value — You need the NPV rule and the idea that the discount rate is the return investors could earn elsewhere at the same risk.
  • Six principles behind financial decisions — The difference between systematic risk, which the rate prices, and risk that diversification removes is introduced there.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Cold Brew’s NPV at 11% is $25,737. Ana built it from forecasts: 30,000 cans in the first year rising to 60,000, a $3.49 shelf price, $0.74 a can from the co-packer, $93,000 of promotion over five years. None of these is known. The grocers might take fewer cans, a rival might force a price cut, the co-packer might raise its fee. Before she commits, Ana wants to know which of her assumptions the decision rests on, how bad things could get, and what she could do if they went badly.

A project’s NPV is a forecast of value, and the forecasts behind it are uncertain in two ways. There is forecasting risk: Ana’s numbers may simply be wrong, perhaps biased by her enthusiasm. And there is genuine uncertainty about the future: even a careful forecast is an average of outcomes, some good and some bad. Neither shows up in a single NPV figure.

This lesson gives you a toolkit for looking behind the figure:

  • Sensitivity analysis moves one input at a time and shows how far the NPV moves: which assumptions matter most. A tornado chart draws the result.
  • Scenario analysis moves several inputs together, into consistent stories of a bad, a normal and a good future.
  • Break-even analysis turns the question round: how low can volume (or price) go before the project stops paying its way?
  • Monte Carlo simulation draws thousands of combinations of inputs from probability distributions and shows the whole range of NPVs.
  • Real options recognise that managers can react as the future unfolds: expand if things go well, abandon if they go badly, or wait to learn more.

None of these tools changes the decision rule. A project is worth doing if its NPV, from expected cash flows discounted at a rate that reflects its systematic risk, is positive. The tools help you get the expected cash flows right, see how fragile the answer is, and design the project so that bad news costs less.

Key idea: Where risk goes

Risk that investors can’t diversify away (systematic risk, such as a recession hitting every business) belongs in the discount rate. Everything else (a bad launch, a lost listing, a forecast that is too rosy) belongs in the expected cash flows. Adding points to the rate for every worry double-counts, and penalises distant cash flows far more than near ones.

In depth

Sensitivity analysis and the tornado chart

Take the model, hold every input at its base value except one, move that one to a plausible low and high value, and record the NPV each time. Repeat for each input. Sorting the inputs by the width of the swing and drawing each as a horizontal bar of the change in NPV from the base, widest at the top, gives a chart shaped like a tornado.

Volume moves every year’s cans by the same percentage. A lower cost or less promotion raises the NPV, so for those inputs the low value gives the higher NPV.

Volume dominates: a 30% shortfall turns a $25,737 NPV into a loss of $29,657. The shelf price comes next, because every 10 cents of shelf price is 6.2 cents of net price on every can. The discount rate, so often argued over, matters least of the five over these ranges. That tells Ana where to spend her effort: on evidence about how many cans grocers will sell, not on refining the rate.

Two cautions. The ranges are judgements, and a tornado is only as fair as they are: an input given a narrow range will look unimportant. And one-at-a-time changes ignore the fact that inputs move together; if volume disappoints, Ana might also cut the price or spend more on promotion.

Scenario analysis

A scenario changes several inputs at once to tell a consistent story. Ana’s pessimistic case is that grocers take only 70% of the forecast, she cuts the shelf price to $3.29 to keep her space, and the co-packer’s fee rises to $0.80 a can. Her optimistic case is 120% of the forecast volume at $0.70 a can.

The expected NPV, weighting each scenario by its probability, is 0.25×(−46,683)+0.5×25,737+0.25×69,107=18,4740.25 \times (-46{,}683) + 0.5 \times 25{,}737 + 0.25 \times 69{,}107 = 18{,}4740.25×(−46,683)+0.5×25,737+0.25×69,107=18,474 dollars. It is lower than the base case because Ana’s bad case is worse than her good case is good: the base case is the most likely outcome, not the average one. When the downside is lopsided, an NPV from the most likely values overstates the project. And the pessimistic case is a loss of $46,683: Lantern must be able to survive it.

Scenario analysis is only as good as the scenarios. Three are a crude summary of a continuous range, the probabilities are judgements, and the “worst case” is rarely the worst that could happen.

Break-even analysis

Break-even analysis asks how far a key input can fall before the project stops paying its way, and “paying its way” can mean three things. For a project selling a constant quantity QQQ a year at net price PPP, with variable cost vvv a unit, fixed cash costs FFF a year and depreciation DDD:

Accounting break-even
The volume at which accounting profit (EBIT, and so net income) is zero: QA=(F+D)÷(P−v)Q_A = (F + D) \div (P - v)QA​=(F+D)÷(P−v).
Cash break-even
The volume at which revenue just covers variable and fixed cash costs, so operating cash flow before tax is zero: QC=F÷(P−v)Q_C = F \div (P - v)QC​=F÷(P−v). Below it the project drains cash every year.
Financial break-even
The volume at which NPV is zero: operating cash flow just repays the investment with the required return. Each year it must equal the investment’s equivalent annual cost.

Take a steady year of Cold Brew at the forecast’s eventual 60,000 cans. Each can brings in $2.16 (the $3.49 shelf price less the trade’s 38%) and costs $0.74, a contribution of $1.42. Fixed costs are $49,000 a year: $15,000 of promotion, the $2,000 listing fee, the $24,000 brand manager and the $8,000 of café margin lost. Depreciation, simplified to straight line, is $1,800 on the $9,000 of fridges. Then:

  • Accounting break-even: (49,000+1,800)÷1.4238=35,679(49{,}000 + 1{,}800) \div 1.4238 = 35{,}679(49,000+1,800)÷1.4238=35,679 cans a year.
  • Cash break-even: 49,000÷1.4238=34,41549{,}000 \div 1.4238 = 34{,}41549,000÷1.4238=34,415 cans a year.
  • Financial break-even: the fridges’ equivalent annual cost over five years at 11% is 9,000÷3.6959=2,4359{,}000 \div 3.6959 = 2{,}4359,000÷3.6959=2,435 dollars; operating cash flow must cover it, after allowing for the depreciation tax shield, so QF=[49,000+(2,435−0.25×1,800)÷0.75]÷1.4238=36,274Q_F = [49{,}000 + (2{,}435 - 0.25 \times 1{,}800) \div 0.75] \div 1.4238 = 36{,}274QF​=[49,000+(2,435−0.25×1,800)÷0.75]÷1.4238=36,274 cans a year.

A steady year needs only about 36,000 cans against a forecast of 60,000, which looks comfortable. It isn’t, and the reason is instructive. Cold Brew’s costs are front-loaded: $30,000 of promotion in year 1, the $6,000 launch, and working capital to build. Running the full five-year model instead, the NPV is zero when every year’s volume is 86.1% of the forecast, a margin of less than 14%. Break-even formulas for a steady state are a quick screen; for a project whose early years differ from its later ones, find the break-even on the full model (in a spreadsheet, Goal Seek does this: set the NPV cell to 0 by changing the volume cell). The same search gives a break-even shelf price of $3.17, only 32 cents below Ana’s $3.49.

The roaster is a cost-saving project with steady years, so its break-even is easier to read. It saves $21.53 on every kilogram roasted instead of bought, against fixed costs of an operator, maintenance and a kitchen corner. Its NPV is zero at about 2,158 kg a year, 90% of the 2,400 kg Lantern needs: a 10% fall in Lantern’s coffee sales would wipe out its value.

Operating leverage

Why is Cold Brew so sensitive to volume? Because most of its costs are fixed. Operating leverage is the degree to which a project’s costs are fixed rather than variable: high fixed costs mean that a change in volume produces a larger percentage change in profit. The degree of operating leverage (DOL) measures it at a given volume:

DOL=% change in EBIT% change in volume=Q (P−v)EBIT\text{DOL} = \dfrac{\%\ \text{change in EBIT}}{\%\ \text{change in volume}} = \dfrac{Q\,(P - v)}{\text{EBIT}}DOL=% change in volume% change in EBIT​=EBITQ(P−v)​

In Cold Brew’s steady year, contribution is 60,000×1.4238=85,42860{,}000 \times 1.4238 = 85{,}42860,000×1.4238=85,428 dollars and EBIT, counting the lost café margin, is 85,428−49,000−1,800=34,62885{,}428 - 49{,}000 - 1{,}800 = 34{,}62885,428−49,000−1,800=34,628 dollars, so DOL is 85,428÷34,628=2.4785{,}428 \div 34{,}628 = 2.4785,428÷34,628=2.47: a 10% fall in volume cuts EBIT by about 25%. DOL rises as volume approaches break-even, where EBIT is close to zero. Choices that swap variable costs for fixed ones (an in-house roaster instead of bought beans, a salaried sales team instead of commission) raise operating leverage, and with it the risk that a shortfall hurts.

Monte Carlo simulation, in outline

Simulation takes the next step: describe each uncertain input by a probability distribution, draw a value for each input at random, compute the NPV, and repeat thousands of times. The result is a distribution of NPVs: its average, its spread, and the chance of a loss. As an illustration, suppose Cold Brew’s volume is normally distributed around the forecast with a standard deviation of 20 percentage points (floored at 30%), and its shelf price around $3.49 with a standard deviation of 15 cents.

Cold Brew: 2,000 simulated NPVs at 11% (assumed distributions; fictional)
Volume and priceMean NPVChance NPV < 05th percentile95th percentile
Independent$25,48426.2%−$39,957$90,897
Correlated (0.8): low volume comes with low prices$27,37429.0%−$50,825$109,274

Each row uses the same 2,000 seeded random draws, so the difference comes from the correlation alone. The correlated mean is higher because revenue is volume × price: when the two move together, the expected value of their product rises, and the same effect stretches the good tail.

Two lessons stand out. First, the correlation matters: if a weak launch also forces discounting, the bad outcomes are worse (a 5th percentile of −$50,825 instead of −$39,957). Simulations that draw every input independently understate the tails when inputs that push NPV the same way move together. Second, the output looks precise (“29.0% chance of a loss”) but rests entirely on the assumed distributions and correlations, which for a new product are guesses. Garbage in, garbage out, with decimals. And each draw’s NPV is computed at the risk-adjusted rate, which already prices systematic risk; rejecting a project for a wide spread of NPVs on top of that counts the risk twice.

Real options

A forecast model treats the project as a fixed plan. Real managers react: they expand what works, cut what doesn’t, and wait when waiting teaches them something. The right to make such a decision later, after learning more, is a real option, and it has value because it lets the firm keep the upside while cutting off part of the downside.

  • The option to expand: start small and grow if demand proves strong. Launching Cold Brew with a few natural grocers first, before a wider roll-out, is in effect a pilot that buys this option.
  • The option to abandon: stop and recover what you can (sell the fridges, release the working capital) if demand proves weak.
  • The option to wait: delay, at the cost of a year’s cash flows, to learn more first. Valuable when the investment can’t be reversed and the uncertainty will resolve.

Such options are not free money. They have value only if the firm will really be able to act (a lease or contract may prevent abandonment), and an option to expand is worth something only if competitors can’t take the opportunity first. Exaggerated option values have been used to justify poor investments; the discipline is to model the decision, not to assert that “strategic value” makes up the shortfall.

Why not just raise the rate?

A common shortcut is to add a few points to the discount rate for a risky project: 16% instead of 11% for Cold Brew, which cuts its NPV from $25,737 to $15,595. The trouble is that the extra five points compound. A year-1 cash flow loses 4.3% of its value (1.11÷1.16=0.9571.11 \div 1.16 = 0.9571.11÷1.16=0.957), but a year-5 cash flow loses 19.8% ((1.11÷1.16)5=0.802(1.11 \div 1.16)^5 = 0.802(1.11÷1.16)5=0.802). That is right only if the risk grows steadily with time. Cold Brew’s main risk, that grocers won’t sell enough cans, is resolved in the first year or two, and it is largely specific to Lantern, not systematic. It belongs in the expected cash flows (scenarios weighted by probability), with the abandonment option modelled, and the rate left to price the risk investors can’t diversify.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the base case as the expected case

    The base case is usually built from the most likely value of each input. When the downside is larger than the upside, as with Cold Brew, the probability-weighted NPV is lower than the base-case NPV. Decisions should rest on expected cash flows.

  • Reading a tornado as a ranking of risks

    A tornado shows how far the NPV moves over the ranges you chose. An input given a narrow range looks unimportant whether or not it is. Use ranges that are equally plausible (say, a 1-in-10 chance of being beyond each end) for every input.

  • Using the accounting break-even for an investment decision

    Zero accounting profit means the investment earns no return at all. The break-even that matters for value is the financial (NPV) break-even, which is always higher for a project with an up-front investment and a positive required return (with straight-line depreciation and no salvage).

  • Trusting a simulation’s decimals

    A simulation’s probability of loss is only as good as the distributions and correlations fed into it. Independent draws for inputs that move together understate the tails. Report ranges, and say which assumptions drive them.

  • Adding a premium to the rate for project-specific worries

    Raising the rate penalises later cash flows more and more, whatever the shape of the risk. Put risks that diversification removes into the expected cash flows, and keep the rate for systematic risk.

  • Claiming an option the firm can’t exercise

    An option to abandon is worthless if a contract locks the firm in; an option to expand is worth little if a competitor will take the market first. Model who decides what, when, with what information, before adding an option value.

In practice

In practice, risk analysis is part of how a proposal is written, not an afterthought. A good capital request shows the base NPV, a tornado of the five or so drivers that matter, two or three scenarios with the assumptions behind each, the break-even volume or price, and the plan for what management would do if the early evidence disappointed. Reviewers then argue about the inputs that move the answer rather than about every line of the model.

For a small firm like Lantern, the pessimistic case has a second use: it shows whether the business could survive the loss. A large, diversified company can take a project with a 30% chance of losing money if the expected NPV is positive, because one project’s failure is absorbed by the others. A two-café business with one big launch can’t diversify in the same way, which is why Ana should design Cold Brew as a series of smaller commitments: list with a few grocers, measure sell-through, and keep the right to stop. That is the option to abandon and the option to expand, put to work.

Large firms use simulation for projects whose risks interact: a mine whose output, metal price and costs are all uncertain, or a new plant whose demand depends on the economy. Its most useful output is often not the probability of loss but the conversation it forces about which inputs move together and why.

Summary

  • An NPV is a forecast. Risk analysis finds which assumptions it rests on, how bad things could get and what management could do about it.
  • Sensitivity analysis moves one input at a time; a tornado chart ranks the inputs by the swing in NPV. For Cold Brew, volume dominates and the rate matters least.
  • Scenario analysis moves inputs together into consistent stories; weighting them by probability gives the expected NPV, which can be below the base case when the downside is lopsided.
  • Break-even volumes: accounting (EBIT = 0), cash (operating cash flow before tax = 0) and financial (NPV = 0). The financial break-even is the one that matters for value; for uneven projects, find it on the full model.
  • Operating leverage (high fixed costs) magnifies the effect of volume on profit: DOL = contribution ÷ EBIT.
  • Monte Carlo simulation gives a distribution of NPVs, but its precision is borrowed from assumed distributions; ignoring correlation understates the tails.
  • Real options (to expand, abandon or wait) add value when the firm can act on new information; value them by modelling the decision.
  • Systematic risk belongs in the discount rate; everything else belongs in the expected cash flows. Raising the rate for every worry double-counts.

Terms introduced

Sensitivity analysis
Measuring how much a model’s output changes when an input changes, usually one input at a time, to find the assumptions that matter most.
Forecasting risk
The risk that a decision is wrong because the cash-flow forecasts behind it are wrong, through error, bias or optimism.
Tornado chart
A bar chart of a sensitivity analysis: one horizontal bar per input, showing the output (such as NPV) at the input’s low and high values, sorted with the widest swing at the top.
Scenario analysis
Measuring an outcome such as NPV under a few consistent sets of assumptions (for example pessimistic, base and optimistic), often weighted by probabilities to give an expected value.
Accounting break-even
The sales volume at which a project’s accounting profit (EBIT) is zero: (fixed costs + depreciation) ÷ contribution per unit.
Cash break-even
The sales volume at which revenue just covers variable and fixed cash costs, so operating cash flow before tax is zero: fixed cash costs ÷ contribution per unit.
Financial break-even
The sales volume (or other input) at which a project’s NPV is zero, so that it just earns its required return.
Operating leverage
The extent to which a business’s or project’s costs are fixed rather than variable; high operating leverage makes profit more sensitive to volume.
Degree of operating leverage
The percentage change in EBIT for a 1% change in volume: contribution ÷ EBIT at the current volume.
Monte Carlo simulation
Repeatedly drawing random values of a model’s uncertain inputs from probability distributions and recording the output each time, to estimate the distribution of the output.
Real option
A right, but not an obligation, to change a real investment later (expand, abandon, delay or switch) after learning more; it has value because it lets the firm avoid bad outcomes while keeping good ones.
