The weighted average cost of capital

Combine the costs of debt and equity into one rate with market-value or target weights, estimate Lantern’s WACC, and learn when that rate is the wrong one: projects riskier or safer than the firm, flotation costs, and the usual mistakes.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate the weighted average cost of capital from the costs of debt, preferred stock and equity and their market-value weights.
  • Explain why market-value or target weights are used rather than book values, and choose weights for a private firm.
  • Calculate Lantern’s WACC at its 20% target debt share and compare it with Ana’s 11% working rate.
  • Explain why the WACC suits only projects as risky as the firm, and estimate a project’s rate with the pure-play approach.
  • Identify common WACC mistakes, including double-counting interest and mixing nominal and real rates, and allow for flotation costs.

Review first

  • The cost of debt and equity — The after-tax cost of debt, the cost of equity from the CAPM and the unlevered and relevered betas.
  • Project cash flows — Project free cash flows leave interest out; this lesson shows where financing enters instead.
  • Solvency and leverage — Lantern’s debt and equity at book value at the end of 2027.
  • Inflation and real returns — Nominal and real rates, and why they must match the cash flows.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana now has two rates for Lantern: lenders require 7.5% before tax on new long-term debt (5.63% after tax), and she estimates that she, as the only shareholder, requires 12.75% (9.75% from the CAPM plus a 3% premium for size). A new project will be paid for with a mix of both, in the proportions Lantern aims to keep: 20% debt, 80% equity. What return must the project earn to pay both groups what they require?

The answer is the weighted average cost of capital (WACC): the costs of the firm’s sources of capital, each weighted by its share of the firm’s value. If 80% of every dollar invested comes from equity requiring 12.75% and 20% from debt costing 5.63% after tax, the dollar must earn

WACC=0.80×12.75%+0.20×5.63%=10.20%+1.13%=11.33%\text{WACC} = 0.80 \times 12.75\% + 0.20 \times 5.63\% = 10.20\% + 1.13\% = 11.33\%WACC=0.80×12.75%+0.20×5.63%=10.20%+1.13%=11.33%

A project that earns exactly 11.33% after tax pays the lenders their interest, saves the tax the interest deducts, and leaves Ana exactly the 12.75% she requires on her share. Earning more creates value for her; earning less destroys it. That is why the WACC is the discount rate for the free cash flows of module 1: those cash flows leave interest out (financing enters through the rate instead), and they are the cash available to all the investors together.

Lantern’s 11.33% is close to the 11% working rate Ana has used since module 1, so nothing she has concluded so far changes. Cold Brew’s NPV falls from $25,737 at 11% to $24,992 at 11.33%, still comfortably positive, and its IRR of 27.1% is far above either rate. The closeness is partly luck: the estimate rests on several judgements, the largest being the 3% premium. Without it, the WACC would be 8.93%.

Key idea: The WACC is the right rate only for an average project

The WACC is the return investors require on the firm as a whole: on its existing mix of activities, financed in its target mix. It is the right discount rate for a project only if the project is about as risky as the firm’s existing business and is financed in the same proportions. A riskier project needs a higher rate, a safer one a lower rate, whatever the firm’s WACC.

In depth

The formula and its weights

With debt DDD, preferred stock PPP and common equity EEE, the firm’s value is V=D+P+EV = D + P + EV=D+P+E and

WACC=EV rE+PV rP+DV rD(1−T)\text{WACC} = \dfrac{E}{V}\,r_E + \dfrac{P}{V}\,r_P + \dfrac{D}{V}\,r_D(1 - T)WACC=VE​rE​+VP​rP​+VD​rD​(1−T)

Only the debt term carries (1−T)(1 - T)(1−T), because only interest is deductible. Each cost is a market rate from the previous lesson, and each weight is a share of the firm’s value. The weights must add to 100%. Short-term operating liabilities such as payables and accrued wages are left out: they carry no explicit interest, and their cost is already in the operating cash flows (a supplier who gives credit charges for it in its prices).

Market-value weights, not book values

The weights should be market values, because the required returns belong to the market values. A shareholder who could sell her shares for $222 million wants 12% on $222 million, not on the $140 million the balance sheet records. Book equity is a historical figure: the money shareholders paid in plus the profits kept, measured by accounting rules. It ignores what the firm’s future cash flows are worth, so for a profitable firm it is usually far below market value (F00 compared the two as the market-to-book ratio). For debt, the two are often close, because loans are recorded at what is owed and market rates have not usually moved far; for bonds issued years ago they can differ, and the market value is the price.

Wren Valley Storage, a fictional listed battery-storage installer, has 12 million shares trading at $18.50, so its equity is worth $222.0 million; its book equity is $140 million. Its bonds have a face value of $90 million and trade at 104% of face, $93.6 million. At market values debt is 29.7% of the firm; at book values it would be 39.1%. With a 12% cost of equity and a 6.4% yield (4.8% after tax), the WACC is 9.86% at market weights and 9.18% at book weights. Book weights put too much weight on the cheaper source and understate the cost.

Lantern shows the problem in a stronger form. At 31 December 2027 its interest-bearing debt was $65,250 and its book equity $62,818 (F03), so at book values debt would be 50.9% of its capital. But $62,818 records only the $20,000 Ana paid in and the profits Lantern has kept; it says nothing about what a business earning $22,431 after tax in its second year is worth. Lantern has no share price, so the market value of its equity cannot be read off a screen, and its book weights are a poor guide to the risks its investors bear.

Target weights

Even for a listed firm, today’s market weights move with the share price every day, and the firm may be in the middle of borrowing or repaying. A project lasting years is financed over years, so most analysts use target weights: the mix of debt and equity, at market values, that the firm aims to keep over the long run. A firm’s own statements of policy, its history, and its peers’ structures are the evidence for the target.

For a private firm, the target is the only practical choice. Lantern’s listed peers carry debt of 23.1%, 13.0% and 35.5% of their market value (D/E of 0.30, 0.15 and 0.55), an average of 23.9%. Ana aims for 20%: a little below the peers, reflecting her caution as an owner with most of her wealth in the business. Using the target keeps the WACC consistent with the beta, which was relevered at the same D/E of 0.25.

Lantern’s line of credit is a judgement call. It is meant to finance seasonal working capital rather than long-lived projects (it stood at $24,000 at the end of 2027, after two draws, and that $24,000 is part of the $65,250 above), so this course leaves it out. An analyst who saw it drawn permanently would treat it as long-term debt and include it at its 9%.

How the WACC changes with the debt share

Move Lantern’s target and both costs move. More debt raises the relevered beta, and so the cost of equity; at a fixed 7.5% cost of debt, the WACC falls slowly, from 11.88% with no debt to 11.33% at 20% and 11.05% at 30%, because more of the firm is financed by debt whose interest saves tax. (The widget below assumes Coastline would charge more above 25% debt, which gives about 11.28% at 30% instead.) That fall does not continue for ever: lenders charge more as debt grows, and the risk and costs of financial distress rise. The capital-structure lesson in module 4 asks whether, and how far, borrowing more adds value.

One rate for the firm, or a rate for each project?

The WACC reflects the average risk of everything the firm already does. A project is worth taking if it earns more than investors would require for that project’s risk, which may differ from the firm’s average. Investors who buy a coffee company’s shares don’t require the same return on a new canned-drinks line as on its cafés, just because the same company owns both.

A firm that applies its WACC to every project accepts too many risky projects (like A) and rejects too many safe ones (like B). Over time it drifts towards riskier activities, and its WACC rises with them. The remedy is to give each project a rate that matches its own systematic risk. Three ways are used in practice:

  • The pure-play approach. Find listed firms that do only what the project does, unlever their betas, and relever at the firm’s target D/E: the method of the previous lesson, applied to the project instead of the firm.
  • A divisional cost of capital. A firm with distinct divisions estimates a rate for each from its own peers, and every project in a division uses the division’s rate.
  • Risk classes. Some firms set the WACC for average projects and adjust it by fixed amounts for classes of project: lower for cost-saving replacements, higher for new products or new markets. The adjustments are judgements, but cruder errors are avoided.

Only systematic risk belongs in the rate. A project whose sales are uncertain but unrelated to the economy needs careful cash-flow forecasts and sensitivity analysis, not a higher rate; raising the rate for every worry counts the same risk twice (the project-risk lesson in module 2 explains why).

Is Cold Brew as risky as the cafés? It is a packaged drink sold through grocers to people who may never visit a Lantern café, with heavy promotion in a crowded category. Its natural peers are not coffee shops but drinks makers. Fizzwell Drinks, a fictional listed maker of canned drinks, has an equity beta of 1.40 at a D/E of 0.20. Unlevered, its business beta is 1.217, well above the coffee peers’ 0.850, and the pure-play method gives Cold Brew a rate of 13.25% (worked below). At that rate Cold Brew’s NPV is $20,875, smaller than at Lantern’s WACC but still positive; its IRR of 27.1% clears both. The decision survives the higher rate, which is what Ana needed to know.

Flotation costs

Raising new money costs money: underwriters’ fees and the underwriting spread for public issues, lenders’ arrangement fees, legal and accounting costs. These are flotation costs: for example, a few percent of the amount raised for new shares, and less for a bank loan. There are two ways to allow for them. One raises the cost of capital, for example by using the net proceeds per share, P0(1−f)P_0(1 - f)P0​(1−f), in the dividend growth model. The other, often recommended, leaves the WACC alone and adds the flotation costs to the project’s initial outlay, because they are a cash cost of this project, paid once, not a cost that recurs every year.

The Lantern examples in these lessons leave flotation costs out.

Nominal and real

The costs in the WACC are market rates, and market rates include expected inflation: Lantern’s 11.33% is a nominal rate. It must discount nominal cash flows, forecast in the dollars of each future year with prices and wages rising. If cash flows are forecast in today’s dollars (real terms), the rate must be real too: with 2.5% expected inflation (an assumption), (1.1133÷1.025)−1=8.61%(1.1133 \div 1.025) - 1 = 8.61\%(1.1133÷1.025)−1=8.61%. Mixing a nominal rate with real cash flows undervalues a typical project.

Common mistakes

  • Using the firm’s WACC for every project

    The WACC is the rate for a project as risky as the firm’s existing business. Using it for a riskier project accepts it too easily; for a safer one, rejects it too easily. Estimate a rate from the project’s own peers (pure play), a divisional rate, or at least a risk class.

  • Weighting with book values

    Book equity records past contributions and retained profits, not what the equity is worth. For a profitable firm it understates equity’s weight and so understates the WACC. Use market values or, for a private firm, a target mix consistent with the peers’ market-value structures.

  • Counting the cost of debt twice

    Interest is a financing flow. If a project’s cash flows already deduct interest and are then discounted at the WACC, the cost of debt is charged twice: once in the cash flows and again in the rate. Leave interest out of free cash flow; the debt term in the WACC carries it.

  • Getting the tax on debt wrong

    The debt cost in the WACC is after tax, rD(1−T)r_D(1 - T)rD​(1−T), because free cash flows compute tax on EBIT as if there were no interest. Using the pre-tax rate ignores the tax saving; taking the tax saving in the cash flows as well as in the rate counts it twice.

  • Mixing nominal and real

    A WACC built from market rates is nominal. Discounting cash flows forecast in today’s prices at a nominal rate undervalues them; inflating cash flows and discounting at a real rate overvalues them. Match the two.

  • Weights that don’t match the beta

    The relevered beta was computed at a D/E; the WACC must use the same mix. Relevering at 0.25 and then weighting debt at 40% mixes two capital structures and gives a rate no investor would require.

In practice

Ana’s rates for 2028. Ana adopts 11.33% as Lantern’s WACC for projects like its existing business: a café refit, kiosks that speed up café orders, equipment that replaces something it already uses. For Cold Brew she uses the pure-play 13.25%; for the solar panels, whose savings depend on electricity prices rather than coffee sales, she notes that the WACC is probably too high and that the decision there rests more on the cash flows than on the rate. She records each judgement, so that her accountant, and anyone she later asks for money, can see where each rate came from.

A capital budgeting manual. In a larger firm the treasury publishes the rates once a year: a group WACC, a rate for each division from its own peers, and rules for exceptional projects (a new country, a new technology). Project teams cannot choose their own rate; they must justify a different one to the treasury. The point is consistency: two managers with the same project should reach the same answer, and a manager should not be able to make a project pass by choosing a lower rate.

Checking a valuation. When an analyst reviews a business valuation built on discounted free cash flows, the WACC is one of the first things she tests. Are the weights market or target values, not book? Is the cost of debt a current yield, after tax? Does the beta’s D/E match the weights? Is any extra premium stated and defended? Are the rate and the cash flows both nominal? Each of these errors is common, and each can move a valuation by more than the forecasts do.

Summary

  • The WACC weights each source’s cost by its share of the firm’s value: (E/V) r_E + (P/V) r_P + (D/V) r_D(1 − T). It discounts free cash flows, which exclude interest.
  • Use market-value weights, or target weights for the mix kept over the project’s life; book values understate equity for a profitable firm. Lantern’s book weights (50.9% debt) would mislead.
  • Lantern at its 20% target: 0.80 × 12.75% + 0.20 × 5.63% = 11.33%, close to Ana’s 11% working rate; 8.93% without the size premium.
  • The WACC suits only projects as risky as the firm. Use a pure-play peer, a divisional rate or a risk class for others: Cold Brew’s pure-play rate is about 13.25%, and its NPV stays positive.
  • Treat flotation costs as part of a project’s outlay: gross up the amount needed by 1 − f_A.
  • Avoid the common mistakes: one rate for every project, book weights, interest in the cash flows as well as the rate, the wrong tax treatment, nominal against real, and weights that don’t match the beta.

Terms introduced

Weighted average cost of capital
The average of the costs of a firm’s sources of capital (equity, preferred stock and after-tax debt), each weighted by its share of the firm’s value: the return the firm must earn on an average-risk project to pay all its investors what they require. Abbreviated WACC.
Market-value weights
Shares of a firm’s capital measured at what each claim is worth in the market now, rather than at the amounts recorded in the balance sheet.
Target capital structure
The mix of debt and equity, at market values, that a firm aims to keep over the long run, used to weight its costs of capital.
Pure-play approach
Estimating a project’s cost of capital from listed firms that do only what the project does: their betas are unlevered, then relevered at the investing firm’s target mix.
Divisional cost of capital
A cost of capital estimated for one division of a firm from that division’s own business risk, used for the division’s projects instead of the firm’s overall WACC.
Flotation costs
The costs of issuing new securities or arranging new borrowing: underwriting fees and spreads, arrangement fees, legal and accounting costs.
