Payout policy

How firms return cash to shareholders, by dividends and share buybacks, why the choice does not change shareholders’ wealth in a perfect market, and why taxes, signals, investor clienteles, agency costs and flexibility make it matter in practice.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Describe cash dividends, special dividends, share repurchases, stock dividends and splits, and the dates in a dividend’s timetable.
  • Calculate the payout and retention ratios, the ex-dividend share price, and the share count, price and EPS after a buyback.
  • Explain why a dividend and a buyback of the same cash leave shareholders equally wealthy in a perfect market, and explain why the EPS increase from a buyback is not value.
  • Explain how taxes, signalling, clienteles, agency costs and flexibility affect the choice of payout, and summarise survey evidence on how managers decide.
  • Calculate a smoothed dividend with a partial-adjustment rule, and describe the legal and contractual limits on payouts.

Review first

  • The balance sheet and equity — How dividends are declared and recorded, treasury stock, and the declaration, record and payment dates.
  • Capital structure — The Modigliani–Miller argument, asymmetric information and agency costs carry over from financing to payout.
  • Governance and the agency problem — Free cash flow in managers’ hands is an agency problem; covenants limit dividends.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern has paid three dividends, all to Ana, its only shareholder: $300 in March 2026 and $4,700 in December 2026, out of 2026 net income of $27,387, and $2,000 in June 2027, out of $22,431. Each time the money left a company that could have used it, for coffee stock or a deposit on equipment, and went to a shareholder who could have used it too. A listed company faces the same question with thousands of shareholders who want different things, and with a second tool, buying back its own shares.

Payout policy is a firm’s choice of how much cash to return to its shareholders, in what form, and when. It is the other side of the investment decision: cash the firm pays out is cash it does not invest, and cash it keeps must earn at least what shareholders could earn elsewhere at the same risk. A firm with more positive-NPV projects than cash should pay out little; a firm with more cash than good projects should pay out the rest. The interesting questions are about the rest: in what form, and does the form matter?

Ways to pay out

  • Regular cash dividends. A payment per share, usually quarterly in the US and half-yearly in the UK. Boards treat the regular dividend as a commitment.
  • Special dividends. One-off payments, usually after a windfall, labelled so that shareholders don’t expect them again.
  • Share repurchases (buybacks). The company buys its own shares, usually on the stock market over months, sometimes by a tender offer at a fixed price. The shares bought are cancelled or held as treasury stock. Only shareholders who sell receive cash; the others own a larger share of a smaller company.
  • Stock dividends and splits. New shares handed to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings. No cash leaves the company, so these are not payouts at all, a point the In depth section returns to.

Two ratios describe how much is paid. The payout ratio is dividends divided by net income; the retention ratio, or plowback ratio, is the share kept, 1 minus the payout ratio. Lantern paid out 5,000÷27,387=18.3%5,000 \div 27,387 = 18.3\%5,000÷27,387=18.3% of its 2026 profit and 2,000÷22,431=8.9%2,000 \div 22,431 = 8.9\%2,000÷22,431=8.9% of 2027’s, retaining 91.1% to finance its growth. Because buybacks have become a large part of what listed companies return, analysts also use a total payout ratio: dividends plus buybacks, divided by net income.

The dividend timetable

A dividend passes through four dates. On the declaration date the board announces the amount and the dates, and the company records a liability. The date of record decides who is paid: shareholders on the register at the end of that day. Because a share bought on the stock market takes time to settle onto the register, there is an ex-dividend date: anyone who buys on or after it does not receive the dividend. Most US trades have settled one business day after the trade (“T+1”) since 28 May 2024, and Investor.gov explains that the ex-dividend date is now usually set as the record date itself, or the business day before if the record date is not a business day. Finally, on the payment date the cash is paid.

In a market without taxes or frictions, the share price falls by the dividend when the shares go ex-dividend: a share with the dividend attached is worth the share without it plus the cash. The shareholder is no richer; part of her investment has turned into cash.

In depth

Dividends and buybacks in a perfect market

Marisco Tea Company, a fictional listed tea importer, has 1,000,000 shares at $40, a market value of $40,000,000. Of that, $4,000,000 is surplus cash it has no use for. It earns $6,000,000 a year, of which $120,000 is interest on the cash (4% before 25% tax). The board can pay out the $4,000,000 as a dividend of $4 a share, or buy back shares at the market price of $40.

The buyback retires 4,000,000 ÷ 40 = 100,000 shares. Both payouts remove the cash and the interest it earned.

Shareholders end with $40 of wealth per share either way. With the dividend, each has a $36 share and $4 of cash. With the buyback, those who sold have $40 of cash and those who kept their shares hold shares still worth $40, because the company paid a fair price: the shares it bought cost exactly what they were worth. Neither form creates or destroys value, because the cash was already the shareholders’; the payout only moves it out of the company.

Shareholders can also undo either choice themselves, as investors could undo leverage under the Modigliani–Miller propositions. An investor with 1,000 shares who wants $4,000 of cash after a buyback can sell 100 shares at $40: a homemade dividend. One who would rather not receive a dividend can reinvest the $4,000 in shares at $36, buying 111.1 and owning shares worth 1111.1×36=40,0001111.1 \times 36 = 40{,}0001111.1×36=40,000. Miller and Modigliani showed in 1961 that in a perfect and frictionless market a firm’s value does not depend on its payout policy, given its investment decisions, as Brav and colleagues (2003) summarise it.

Key idea: A buyback raises EPS without raising value

After the buyback, Marisco’s EPS rises from $6.00 to $6.53, 8.9% higher, yet the share price stays at $40. The price-earnings ratio falls from 6.67 to 6.12, because the company has given away its safest asset, cash, and the remaining shares are a claim on riskier tea importing alone. The dividend shows the same fall in the ratio. Higher EPS from a buyback is arithmetic, not value.

Why payout policy matters in practice

Real markets have taxes, information gaps, transaction costs and managers whose interests differ from shareholders’. Each gives a reason why the form and level of payout can matter.

Taxes. A dividend is taxed as income for every shareholder in the year it is paid. A buyback taxes only the shareholders who sell, and only on their gain over what they paid; the others defer tax until they sell. In the US, qualified dividends are taxed at the lower capital-gains rates (IRS Topic 404), which narrows the difference, but the deferral and the taxation of gains rather than proceeds remain. Suppose a holder of 1,000 Marisco shares, bought at $25, pays 15% on both dividends and gains (an assumed rate). The $4,000 dividend costs her 4,000×0.15=6004{,}000 \times 0.15 = 6004,000×0.15=600 in tax. Raising the same $4,000 by selling 100 shares realises a gain of 100×(40−25)=1,500100 \times (40 - 25) = 1,500100×(40−25)=1,500 and costs 225225225; if she does not sell, nothing now. Tax-exempt investors such as pension funds are indifferent.

Signals. Managers know more about future earnings than investors do. Because boards are reluctant to cut a regular dividend, an increase suggests they expect earnings to stay high enough to sustain it, and a cut suggests the opposite; dividend changes therefore carry information. A buyback can suggest managers think the shares are cheap, the mirror image of the pecking order’s equity issue.

Clienteles. Investors differ in their taxes and in whether they want regular cash. A firm may attract a clientele whose tastes match its policy, and if each clientele is already served, changing the policy gains nothing and may upset the existing holders.

Agency costs. Cash left in a mature firm with few good projects tempts managers to spend it on growth for its own sake, the free-cash-flow problem associated with Michael Jensen (1986), as Brav and colleagues summarise it. Free cash flow in Jensen’s sense is the cash left after funding every positive-NPV project, narrower than the accounting measure F03 calls free cash flow. Paying it out, and especially committing to a regular dividend, takes it out of their hands and makes them raise money from investors, who scrutinise it, when they need more.

Flexibility. A regular dividend is hard to cut; a buyback programme can be slowed or stopped quietly. Firms with volatile or temporary cash flows therefore prefer buybacks or special dividends for the variable part of their payout.

What managers say

Brav, Graham, Harvey and Michaely surveyed 384 financial executives and interviewed 23 more about payout policy (NBER working paper, 2003). Among dividend payers, 94% agreed or strongly agreed that they try to avoid cutting dividends and 90% that they smooth dividends from year to year; cuts were a last resort. Repurchases were seen as more flexible: only 22.5% thought reducing repurchases had negative consequences. The most popular reason for repurchasing, agreed by 86.6%, was buying when the shares were a good value relative to their true value, and three-quarters said increasing EPS was an important factor. Taxes were second-order: 21.4% of dividend payers called investors’ personal taxes important or very important. Only 4.2% agreed that they used dividends to show they could bear costly payments, the classic signalling model, yet about four-fifths believed dividend decisions convey information to investors. Among firms that paid nothing, 70% said they never planned to start paying dividends.

The authors also revisit John Lintner’s 1956 study of 28 industrial firms, which found dividends sticky, smoothed and tied to long-run sustainable earnings. They found the conservatism persists but the tie to earnings has weakened: managers no longer treat a target payout ratio as the main decision.

Stock dividends and splits

In a two-for-one stock split each share becomes two. Marisco would have 2,000,000 shares and, with nothing else changed, a price of $20.00. A 10% stock dividend gives one new share for every ten held: 1,100,000 shares at 40÷1.1=$36.3640 \div 1.1 = \$36.3640÷1.1=$36.36. In both cases the company is cut into more, smaller slices and every shareholder keeps the same fraction of it. The accounting differs (a stock dividend moves an amount from retained earnings to contributed capital; a split changes the number of shares and any par value), but no cash moves and no value is created. The reason usually given is to keep the share price in a range that is convenient to trade.

Limits on payouts

Payouts are limited by law and by contract. Corporate law protects creditors by limiting distributions that would leave a company unable to pay what it owes; the details vary by jurisdiction. Delaware, for example, allows a corporation to pay dividends out of its surplus or, if it has none, out of its net profits for the year in which the dividend is declared and/or the preceding year (8 Del. C. § 170). Loan agreements often add covenants that cap dividends and buybacks or forbid them while a ratio is below a threshold, which is how lenders stop shareholders paying themselves before the lenders are paid (see F00’s governance lesson). Directors can face personal liability for distributions the law does not allow, so boards check these limits before each declaration.

Common mistakes

  • “A buyback that raises EPS creates value.”

    Marisco’s buyback raised EPS by 8.9% and left the price at $40. Fewer shares share the same earnings, but each share is now a riskier claim, so the P/E falls. A buyback adds value only if the shares were bought below their true value (which transfers value from sellers to those who stay) or if it stops managers wasting the cash.

  • “A dividend gives shareholders money they would not otherwise have.”

    The cash was already theirs, inside the company. When the share goes ex-dividend its price falls by about the dividend: the shareholder swaps part of her share’s value for cash, and pays tax on it.

  • “Shareholders who don’t sell in a buyback get nothing.”

    They own a larger fraction of a company that has fewer shares, and in a fair-price buyback their shares are worth as much as before. They also defer tax until they choose to sell.

  • “To get the dividend, buy the shares by the record date.”

    Settlement takes time, so the cut-off for buyers is the ex-dividend date. With one-day settlement it is now usually the record date itself, so you must buy before it, by the previous trading day.

  • “A stock split or stock dividend makes shareholders richer.”

    Each shareholder keeps the same fraction of the same company; the price per share falls in proportion. No cash moves.

In practice

A board sets a payout policy. The board of a fictional mature food company with steady cash flows sets a regular dividend at about 40% of earnings it believes are sustainable, raises it only when it is confident it will not have to cut it, and returns the rest of its surplus by buybacks that can be scaled down in a bad year. Before each buyback it checks the price against its own estimate of value, because buying shares above their value transfers wealth from the shareholders who stay to those who sell. It reports buybacks as returning cash, not as boosting EPS.

A growing company pays nothing. A fictional software firm with more positive-NPV projects than cash pays no dividend and buys back no shares: every dollar retained is invested at more than its cost of capital. Its investors expect returns from a rising share price. Paying a dividend and then raising money by selling shares would cost issue fees and, by the pecking order, a lower price.

An owner-managed company. In a business like Lantern, with one shareholder who also runs it, signalling and clienteles barely arise: Ana knows exactly what Lantern earns. The questions are whether the cash earns more inside the business than outside, whether any loan agreements and the law allow a payment, and how a dividend, a salary and retaining the profit are each taxed, a tax-planning question beyond this course. Lantern’s payout ratios of 18.3% in 2026 and 8.9% in 2027 reflect a growing business keeping most of its profit.

Summary

  • Payout policy decides how much cash to return, in what form and when. Cash that cannot be invested at its cost of capital should be returned.
  • Forms: regular and special dividends, share repurchases; stock dividends and splits move no cash. Payout ratio = dividends ÷ net income; retention ratio = 1 − payout ratio.
  • The timetable: declaration, ex-dividend (now usually the record date in the US), record and payment. The price falls by about the dividend on the ex-dividend date.
  • In a perfect market a dividend and a fair-price buyback of the same cash leave shareholders equally wealthy (Marisco: $40 a share either way). A buyback raises EPS but the P/E falls: no value is created.
  • In practice: dividends are taxed in full when paid, buybacks only on sellers’ gains; dividend changes signal; clienteles form; payouts limit managers’ free cash; buybacks are more flexible.
  • Managers smooth dividends and avoid cuts (94% in the Brav survey); Dt=Dt−1+a(pEt−Dt−1)D_t = D_{t-1} + a(pE_t - D_{t-1})Dt​=Dt−1​+a(pEt​−Dt−1​) captures it. Buybacks absorb the variable part.
  • Payouts are limited by corporate law (Delaware: surplus or recent net profits) and by loan covenants.

Terms introduced

Payout policy
A firm’s choice of how much cash to return to its shareholders, in what form (dividends or share repurchases) and when.
Payout ratio
Dividends divided by net income, or dividends per share divided by earnings per share. The total payout ratio adds share repurchases to dividends.
Retention ratio
The share of net income a firm keeps rather than pays as dividends: 1 minus the payout ratio. Also called the plowback ratio.
Share repurchase
A company’s purchase of its own shares from shareholders, on the market or by a tender offer; the shares are cancelled or held as treasury stock. Also called a buyback.
Ex-dividend date
The first day on which a share trades without the right to a declared dividend: buyers on or after it do not receive the dividend. In the US it is now usually the record date.
Homemade dividend
Cash an investor raises by selling some of her shares, which replaces a dividend the company did not pay.
Clientele
A group of investors drawn to a firm by its payout policy, for example because of their tax position or their need for regular income.
Stock split
A division of each existing share into several, such as two-for-one, which lowers the price per share in proportion and moves no cash.
Stock dividend
A distribution of new shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their holdings, such as one new share for every ten held; no cash leaves the company.
