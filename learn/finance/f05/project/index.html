F05 · Applied project

Funding Lantern’s second kitchen

Fictional case

February 2028. Lantern’s catering and wholesale bakery orders have outgrown the kitchen it has rented since 2026, and Ana has found a second one to lease from 1 April 2028. Fitting it out costs $96,000, more than Lantern can find on its own. Coastline Bank will lend $60,000 over five years at 7.5% (drawn on 31 March, with monthly payments from the end of April), on condition that Lantern’s current ratio, as the loan agreement defines it, stays at least 1.2 at each quarter end. Hollis Avery, a private investor, offers $75,000 for new shares that would give her 30% of Lantern; she says she wants at least 20% a year on her money. Both deals would close on 31 March, the day before the kitchen opens. Ana, Hollis, the kitchen, the bank and every figure are fictional.

Ana asks you four things: what the kitchen’s free cash flows are, what rate to discount them at now that Lantern will have outside owners and a bank loan, whether the kitchen is worth it and how badly it could go, and whether the financing works: the covenant, the price Hollis is paying, and how much room is left for a dividend.

What you will produce

  • The kitchen’s free cash flows for years 0–5, with depreciation, working capital and after-tax salvage shown.
  • Lantern’s weighted average cost of capital after the financing, with the beta, the cost of equity and the weights stated.
  • The kitchen’s NPV, IRR and payback at that rate, its break-even sales and a pessimistic scenario.
  • The covenant test on 31 March 2028 before and after the financing, and the largest dividend it allows.
  • What Hollis’s shares are worth at her price, and a memo to Ana.
