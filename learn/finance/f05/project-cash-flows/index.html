Project cash flows

An NPV is only as good as the cash flows behind it. Learn to build a project’s free cash flows year by year: the operating cash flow and its depreciation tax shield, working capital tied up and released, capital spending and its after-tax resale, and the costs that do and do not belong, from sunk studies and allocated overheads to lost sales elsewhere in the business.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Identify a project’s incremental cash flows, excluding sunk costs, allocated overheads and financing costs, and including opportunity costs and side effects on the rest of the firm.
  • Calculate a project’s free cash flow each year from its revenue, costs, depreciation, tax, capital spending and working capital.
  • Calculate depreciation by MACRS and straight line, the depreciation tax shield, and the after-tax value of an asset sold at a gain or a loss.
  • Build the free cash flows of a cost-saving project and decide whether it is worth doing.
  • Explain why interest is left out of project cash flows and why nominal cash flows must be discounted at a nominal rate.

Review first

  • Net present value — The cash flows built here are what the NPV discounts at the opportunity cost of capital.
  • Long-lived assets — Depreciation methods, book value and gains or losses on disposal.
  • Efficiency and working capital — Inventory and receivables are the working capital a growing product ties up.
  • Inflation and real returns — Nominal and real amounts and the Fisher relation, which must be kept consistent in a forecast.
  • Six principles behind financial decisions — Cash flow rather than profit, and why only incremental cash flows count.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana’s biggest proposal is Cold Brew: canned cold brew sold through natural and specialty grocers from 1 April 2028, made by a co-packer (M02 set the product, its price and its channel). She has forecasts for five April–March years: cans sold, the price, the cost of each can, promotion, the grocers’ listing fee, a part-time brand manager, display fridges for the two cafés, a launch, and the stock and unpaid invoices a grocery business carries. She also knows that some café customers who now buy iced coffee will buy a can instead. Turning all this into the six numbers an NPV needs is the job of this lesson. Every figure is fictional.

Incremental cash flows, after tax

A project’s cash flows are its incremental cash flows: the firm’s cash flows with the project minus its cash flows without it, after tax. Three words in that sentence do the work. Cash, because owners can spend cash, not profit. Incremental, because only what changes with the decision belongs. After tax, because the tax authority takes its share of any extra profit, and the opportunity cost of capital is an after-tax return.

For each year the result is the project’s free cash flow: the cash it frees up for the firm’s investors after paying for everything it needs. (F03 defined free cash flow for a whole company as operating cash flow less capital expenditure; a project’s version adds working capital and the after-tax resale of its assets.)

Cold Brew, line by line: the operating cash flow

Each can sells for $3.49 on the shelf; distributors and grocers keep 38%, so Lantern receives $3.49 × 0.62 = $2.1638 a can. Each can costs $0.74 from the co-packer, delivered. Promotion falls from $30,000 in the launch year to $15,000 a year; the listing fee is $2,000 a year and the brand manager $24,000. The display fridges cost $9,000 and are depreciated for tax over five years by MACRS.

Discounted at Ana’s working rate of 11%, these free cash flows give Cold Brew an NPV of +$25,737, the figure the previous lesson took as given.

In depth

Depreciation, MACRS and the tax shield

Depreciation is not a cash flow, but it reduces taxable profit and so reduces tax, which is. The tax saved each year is the depreciation tax shield, T×DtT \times D_tT×Dt​. Rearranging the operating cash flow shows it directly: OCF=(Revenue−Cash costs)(1−T)+T×D\text{OCF} = (\text{Revenue} - \text{Cash costs})(1 - T) + T \times DOCF=(Revenue−Cash costs)(1−T)+T×D. The first term is what the operations earn after tax; the second is the shield. For Cold Brew’s fridges the shields are $450, $720, $432, $259 and $259, worth about $1,630 today at 11%.

How fast an asset is depreciated for tax is set by tax law, not by how fast it wears out. In the United States most business equipment is depreciated under MACRS, the Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System, which assigns each kind of asset a recovery class and publishes the percentage of its cost to deduct each year (IRS Publication 946, Table A-1). Under the half-year convention an asset is treated as placed in service in the middle of its first tax year, so a 5-year asset is deducted over six tax years: 20%, 32%, 19.2%, 11.52%, 11.52% and 5.76%. The case treats the fridges as 5-year property and the roaster as 7-year property.

MACRS front-loads the deductions compared with straight-line depreciation, so the tax savings come sooner and are worth more in present value. The total deducted is the same; only the timing differs. For Cold Brew the difference is small: straight-line depreciation over five years would lower the NPV by about $44. For the $48,000 roaster it is about $356. Tax rules also allow larger first-year deductions in many cases: IRS Publication 946 for 2025 returns describes a 100% special depreciation allowance for qualified property acquired and placed in service after 19 January 2025, and section 179 expensing up to a limit. These rules change often; the case uses MACRS without them, and a real appraisal should check the rules in force for the year the asset is bought.

Selling the asset: after-tax salvage

When an asset is sold, the tax on the sale depends on its book value for tax: its cost less the depreciation already deducted. If it sells for more than book value, the gain is taxed; if for less, the loss saves tax. Either way the cash the project receives is the after-tax salvage value:

After-tax salvage=S−T (S−B)\text{After-tax salvage} = S - T\,(S - B)After-tax salvage=S−T(S−B)

where SSS is the sale price and BBB the tax book value at the sale. Five years of MACRS deductions leave the fridges with a book value of $518 (the 5.76% never claimed). Selling them for $1,500 gives a taxable gain of $982 and tax of $245, so the project receives 1,500−0.25×(1,500−518)≈1,2551{,}500 - 0.25 \times (1{,}500 - 518) \approx 1{,}2551,500−0.25×(1,500−518)≈1,255 dollars. Strictly, the half-year convention also applies in the year of disposal, so only half of year 5’s MACRS deduction is allowed. That lowers year 5’s depreciation and raises the book value by the same $518, and the two effects on year 5’s cash flow cancel exactly: the case, like most textbooks, simply ignores the refinement.

Working capital: tied up, then released

A product sold on credit through stockists needs money tied up in stock and receivables before the cash from sales arrives. The amount needed is a level; the cash flow is the change in that level. Cold Brew’s working capital grows with sales, from $9,737 to $19,474, and each increase is an outflow in the year it happens. At the end of the project the level returns to zero and the whole amount flows back. Working capital therefore costs the project time, not money: it is lent to the project and repaid later without interest. At 11% that delay costs Cold Brew about $6,387 in present value. Leaving working capital out would overstate the NPV by that amount.

What belongs in the cash flows, and what does not

Sunk costs: out
Lantern has already spent $9,600 on M05’s needs study, which first suggested Cold Brew. That money is gone whether or not Lantern launches, so it is a sunk cost and is left out. Charging it to the project would cut the NPV to about $16,137 for no reason.
Opportunity costs: in
If the project uses something the firm already owns that has another use, the value of that use is a cost of the project. The roaster would occupy a corner of the kitchen that could be sublet for $300 a month: $3,600 a year of rent forgone is part of the roaster’s cost even though no cash is paid.
Side effects: in
Effects on the rest of the firm count, good or bad: the café margin that Cold Brew takes from iced coffee ($6,000 a year after tax), or extra sales a new product brings to existing ones.
Allocated overheads: out, unless they change
Ana’s accountant suggests charging Cold Brew $5,000 a year as its share of the website and bookkeeping (a fictional proposal). If those costs are the same with or without Cold Brew, the charge is not incremental and is left out; with it, the NPV would fall by about $13,860 for no real cost. Only overheads that the project actually adds, such as an extra bookkeeping hour each month, belong.
Financing costs: out
Interest and loan repayments are not project cash flows. The opportunity cost of capital already charges the project for the money it uses. Subtracting interest as well would charge for the same money twice. How a project is financed is a separate decision (module 4), and module 3 shows how the cost of borrowing enters the discount rate.

Nominal and real: be consistent

A forecast is in nominal dollars if it includes the price rises expected over the years, and in real dollars if it is in today’s purchasing power. Either is fine, as long as nominal cash flows are discounted at a nominal rate and real cash flows at a real rate, linked by the Fisher relation from F04: (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation). Ana’s 11% is a nominal rate, so her forecasts must be nominal. Her Cold Brew price and costs are flat for five years, which in nominal terms is an assumption that prices will not rise; if she expects shelf prices and wages to rise with inflation she should build that in. In practice analysts usually work in nominal terms, because some cash flows are fixed in nominal dollars: the depreciation tax shields are set by the original cost and cannot rise with inflation, and contracts often fix prices.

A cost-saving project: the roaster

Not every project sells something new. The roaster saves money: Lantern would roast its own coffee instead of buying roasted, packed beans from Kilnworth at $36 a kilogram ($9.00 a 250 g bag). For a cost-saving project the “revenue” line is the saving, and the incremental cash flows are built the same way. Worked example 2 does it in full.

Common mistakes

  • Charging sunk costs to the project

    Money already spent, like the $9,600 needs study, is the same whatever Ana decides. Including it understates the NPV and can kill good projects (“we’ve spent too much already”) or, by the opposite logic, keep bad ones alive.

  • Deducting interest

    Interest is a financing cost, and the discount rate already charges for the money. Deducting it from the cash flows as well counts that cost twice. Leave financing out of the cash flows; module 3 builds the cost of debt into the rate.

  • Forgetting working capital, or forgetting to get it back

    Stock and receivables tie up real cash. Leaving them out overstates Cold Brew’s NPV by about $6,387; including the build-up but not the recovery at the end understates it heavily.

  • Treating the sale price as the salvage cash flow

    The tax book value decides the tax on a sale. The fridges sell for $1,500 but bring in about $1,255 after tax on the gain over their $518 book value. An asset sold below book value brings in more than its price, because the loss saves tax.

  • Ignoring effects on the rest of the firm

    A new product that takes sales from existing ones is worth only its net effect. Cold Brew without its cannibalisation would look about $22,175 better than it is. Free things are not free either: space or equipment the project uses has an opportunity cost if it has another use.

  • Mixing nominal and real

    Discounting real (today’s-dollar) forecasts at a nominal rate understates the NPV; discounting nominal forecasts at a real rate overstates it. Match the rate to the cash flows.

In practice

Building project cash flows is where most of an analyst’s time goes, and where most errors live. In a typical firm the marketing team forecasts volumes and prices, operations forecasts costs and capital spending, and finance assembles them into a free-cash-flow model, adds tax and working capital, and checks that every line is incremental. The model is usually a spreadsheet with one column per year, like Lantern’s, and its weakest inputs are flagged for the sensitivity analysis that follows.

A fictional regional bakery chain launching a frozen range in supermarkets learned the working-capital lesson the hard way. Its first appraisal treated the supermarkets’ 60-day payment terms and the freezer stock as accounting details and left them out. The range was profitable on paper but needed far more cash than planned in its first two years, and the reworked model, with working capital built up and recovered, showed a much thinner NPV.

A fictional solar-panel installer deciding whether to install its own warehouse racking or keep renting space compares the two options on incremental after-tax cash flows: the racking’s cost and its depreciation tax shield against the rent saved, with the resale value of the racking after tax. It leaves out its head-office costs, which do not change, and the interest on the loan that would pay for the racking, which its discount rate already reflects.

Fictional case

Ana keeps the Cold Brew cash flows as built and records why each disputed item is in or out, so that her accountant and, later, any lender can follow the logic. Both of her big proposals have positive NPVs on her working rate: Cold Brew about +$25,737 and the roaster about +$18,386. Together with the kiosks and the cargo bike, they need far more than the $40,000 she can spend in April without new financing, and the roaster comes in two versions with different lives. Choosing among projects under those limits, and testing how far the forecasts can be trusted, is the work of module 2.

Summary

  • Project cash flows are incremental, after-tax cash flows: the firm with the project minus the firm without it.
  • FCF = EBIT(1 − T) + depreciation − capital spending − increase in working capital + after-tax salvage, plus after-tax side effects.
  • Depreciation saves tax (T × D); MACRS front-loads the deductions, which makes them worth a little more than straight line. Tax rules on bonus depreciation change: check them.
  • After-tax salvage = S − T(S − B), where B is the tax book value: a gain is taxed, a loss saves tax.
  • Working capital is an outflow as it builds up and an inflow when it is released; its cost is the time value of the money tied up.
  • Leave out sunk costs, unchanged allocated overheads and interest; include opportunity costs and side effects such as cannibalisation.
  • Discount nominal cash flows at a nominal rate and real cash flows at a real rate.
  • Cold Brew: NPV ≈ +$25,737 at 11%; the roaster, a cost-saving project, ≈ +$18,386.

Terms introduced

Depreciation tax shield
The tax saved because depreciation reduces taxable profit: the tax rate times the depreciation deducted.
MACRS
The Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System, the main US tax depreciation system, which sets a recovery period and yearly percentages of cost for each class of business property (IRS Publication 946).
Half-year convention
A tax depreciation rule that treats property placed in service or disposed of during a tax year as placed in service or disposed of at the midpoint of that year.
After-tax salvage value
The cash a firm keeps from selling a project’s asset: the sale price minus the tax on any gain over the asset’s tax book value, or plus the tax saved on a loss.
Cannibalisation
Sales a new offer takes from the same company’s existing offers, rather than from rivals or from people who would otherwise have bought nothing.
Allocated overhead
A share of a firm’s existing shared costs, such as rent or administration, charged to a project by an accounting rule. It belongs in project cash flows only to the extent that the project changes those costs.
