Net present value

A firm creates value by investing in projects worth more than they cost. Learn what the opportunity cost of capital is, why accepting projects with a positive net present value makes the owners richer whatever their tastes, how to set out a project’s timing, and how to calculate NPV in Excel for annual and dated cash flows.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain capital budgeting as a decision about value, and the opportunity cost of capital as the expected return investors give up elsewhere at the same risk.
  • Calculate the net present value of a project from its cash flows and apply the NPV rule to independent projects.
  • Explain why accepting positive-NPV projects makes every owner better off, whether they want to spend now or later, and what that argument assumes.
  • Use value additivity to judge projects separately, and recognise how the timing convention can change a close decision.
  • Calculate NPV in Excel with NPV (adding the year-0 cash flow outside the function) and with XNPV for cash flows on actual dates.

Review first

  • Discounting cash flows — It introduced NPV, the NPV profile and Excel’s NPV function; this lesson builds the decision rule on them.
  • Annuities — Level yearly savings are annuities, valued in one step with the annuity factor.
  • Six principles behind financial decisions — Cash flow rather than profit, and incremental thinking, decide which numbers go into an NPV.
  • What finance is: money, time and risk — The risk premium explains why riskier cash flows are discounted at higher rates.

The idea

Fictional case

It is February 2028. Lantern Coffee Co. has finished its second year: an online shop and catering kitchen since 2026 and two cafés since March 2027. Ana Lim owns all its shares, and she is choosing what Lantern should invest in from 1 April 2028. Five proposals are on her desk: canned Cold Brew sold through grocers, an in-house coffee roaster, ordering kiosks for the cafés, an electric cargo bike for deliveries and solar panels on the kitchen roof. Without raising new money she can spend about $40,000 at the start of April. Every figure in the case is fictional.

Capital budgeting is a decision about value

Every proposal on Ana’s desk has the same shape: money goes out now, and more money is expected to come back over several years. Capital budgeting is the process of deciding which of these long-term investments to make. It is central to corporate finance, because a firm’s value comes from its assets and the cash they will produce. Long-term investments are also hard to reverse: a poor one can tie up money for years.

The question to ask of each proposal is not “will it make a profit?” or “will it pay for itself?” but “is it worth more than it costs?”. A project is worth the present value of the cash it will bring in. If that is more than the cash it needs today, the owners are richer by the difference the day they commit to it. That difference is the project’s net present value.

The opportunity cost of capital

To find a present value you need a discount rate. The right rate for a project is its opportunity cost of capital: the expected return that the owners could earn by investing the same money elsewhere, in an investment of the same risk. If Lantern spends $7,500 on a cargo bike, Ana gives up whatever that $7,500 would have earned in the next-best use of the same risk. The project has to beat that return to be worth doing.

Three things follow. First, the rate depends on the risk of the project’s cash flows, not on who pays for it or how: a project financed with a cheap loan is no safer than the same project paid for in cash. Second, the rate is a market rate, set by what investors in general can earn, not by what Ana would like to earn. Third, riskier projects need higher rates, because investors demand a risk premium for bearing risk. A bank deposit is the wrong comparison for a café investment; the shares of small food and coffee businesses are a better one.

Estimating that rate properly takes the whole of module 3. Until then Ana uses 11% a year as her working figure for Lantern’s projects of ordinary risk: a judgement, not a measurement. (M06 Customer Analytics valued Lantern’s customers at a round 10% a year; F05 estimates the rate with more care, and module 3 will test whether 11% is about right.)

where rrr is the opportunity cost of capital. The NPV rule says: accept a project if its NPV is positive and reject it if its NPV is negative. When projects are independent projects, so that taking one does not rule out another, each is judged on its own NPV. When they are mutually exclusive projects, such as two different roasters for the same corner of the kitchen, take the one with the highest NPV, provided it is positive (module 2 deals with the complications).

An NPV of zero is not a failure. It means the project earns exactly the opportunity cost of capital: it returns the outlay plus 11% a year, no more and no less. The owners are no better and no worse off than if they had invested the money elsewhere at the same risk. A positive NPV is the value created on top of that.

Fictional case

The cargo bike costs $7,500 and Ana expects it to save $2,300 a year, after tax, in courier fees and van fuel for 5 years. The savings form a five-year annuity, so their present value at 11% is 2,300 × 3.695897 ≈ $8,501. The NPV is 8,501 − 7,500 ≈ +$1,001. On Ana’s estimates the bike makes her about $1,001 richer, in today’s money, the day Lantern buys it.

Why the NPV rule makes the owners richer

Lantern has one owner, but most firms have many, and they differ: some want cash now, others are saving for later. It may seem that a manager would need to know their tastes to choose investments. She does not, and the reason is the most important idea in this lesson.

Suppose owners can borrow and lend in the financial markets at the opportunity cost of capital. Then a positive-NPV project lets every owner spend more, now or later or both. Take the bike. Ana could borrow $8,501 today at 11% and repay the loan with exactly the bike’s savings: $2,300 a year for five years pays off a loan of $8,501 at 11%. She would spend $7,500 of the loan on the bike and have $1,001 left to spend today, with nothing left to repay. If she prefers to spend later, she can instead invest that $1,001 at 11%, which grows to about $1,686 by the end of year 5. Either way she is better off than without the bike.

Key idea: Investment and taste can be separated

When owners can borrow and lend at the market rate, the investment decision (which projects add value) does not depend on the owners’ preferences about when to spend (which they arrange themselves by borrowing or lending). Every owner, patient or impatient, agrees that the firm should take every positive-NPV project. The firm’s job is to maximise the present value of what it owns; the owners’ job is to decide when to consume it.

The argument rests on assumptions, and it is worth knowing where they bite. Owners must be able to borrow and lend at roughly the same rate, for that level of risk. For a large listed company whose shareholders can sell shares or buy more, that is close enough. For a small private firm it may not be: Ana can lend her savings at a bank’s deposit rate but would pay much more to borrow against an uncertain stream of savings. Then her own need for cash can reasonably affect which projects Lantern takes, and quick paybacks matter more to her than the NPV rule says they should. The next lesson returns to this when it asks why so many firms still use payback.

In depth

What the NPV rule assumes

An NPV is a calculation on forecasts, and five assumptions sit inside it.

  1. The cash flows are unbiased forecasts. Each CtC_tCt​ should be the expected cash flow, the probability-weighted average of what might happen, not the hoped-for case. Optimistic forecasts make every project look good; the lesson on project risk shows how to test them.
  2. The cash flows are incremental. Only cash that changes because of the decision belongs in the NPV: incremental cash flows, after tax, including effects on the rest of the firm. The lesson on project cash flows builds them line by line.
  3. Risk is in the rate. The discount rate carries the project’s risk; the cash flows are not trimmed for risk as well. One rate for every year means the risk adjustment compounds with time: a cash flow ten years away is penalised much more than one next year. That suits most projects, whose uncertainty grows with distance, but not all.
  4. The rate fits this project. A firm-wide rate is right only for projects as risky as the firm’s existing business. A new product line may be riskier than Lantern’s cafés; module 3 shows how to adjust.
  5. Now or never, and money is available. The plain rule compares investing now with never investing, and assumes the firm can raise money for every positive-NPV project. Options to wait or expand (project-risk) and limits on capital (choosing-between-projects, both in module 2) relax these.

NPVs add up

Present values are measured in the same unit, today’s dollars, so they can be added. The NPV of two projects taken together is the sum of their NPVs: NPV(A+B)=NPV(A)+NPV(B)\text{NPV}(A + B) = \text{NPV}(A) + \text{NPV}(B)NPV(A+B)=NPV(A)+NPV(B). This property, value additivity, has two uses. It lets a firm value a programme of projects by valuing each part, and it exposes a common trick. Present the kiosks and the solar panels as one “café upgrade” and the package has an NPV of about +$1,389, the kiosks’ NPV plus the panels’, so it looks acceptable. But the package is worth less than the kiosks alone. A project that cannot stand on its own should not ride on a good one; judge each separable part separately.

Additivity also means that NPV, unlike a rate of return or a ratio, measures value in dollars. Two projects with the same percentage return can add very different amounts of value if one is ten times the size of the other.

Timing conventions

The formula is only as good as the timeline behind it, so set the timeline out before typing a number. Lantern’s conventions, used throughout F05, are these.

  • Year 0 is the decision date, 1 April 2028 for Lantern’s 2028 projects. Outlays made then are not discounted.
  • Year t runs from April to March (year 1 is April 2028 to March 2029), and its cash flow is treated as arriving at the end of the year, on 31 March. Cash that is paid at the start of a year, such as rent in advance, belongs at the end of the year before (year t − 1).
  • The rate is per year, matching the spacing of the cash flows. Monthly cash flows would need a monthly rate.

Treating a year’s cash as arriving on its last day is a simplification. Savings from the bike accrue week by week, so on average they arrive about half a year earlier than the model says. A common refinement, the mid-year convention, discounts each year’s cash flow by (1+r)t−0.5(1 + r)^{t - 0.5}(1+r)t−0.5 instead of (1+r)t(1 + r)^t(1+r)t, which multiplies the present value of the yearly flows by (1+r)0.5(1 + r)^{0.5}(1+r)0.5. For the solar panels that turns −$743 into +$235. A close call can turn on a convention, which is a reason to state the convention and to treat an NPV near zero as “about zero”, not as a verdict.

NPV in Excel: NPV and XNPV

Excel’s NPV(rate, value1, …) assumes that its first value arrives one period from now, so it is really a present-value function for years 1 to n. Microsoft’s documentation says a cash flow at the start of the first period must be added to the result rather than included in the values. With the outlay in B4 and the savings in B5:B9, the NPV is =B4+NPV(B1,B5:B9), where B1 holds the rate. Putting B4 inside the range divides the whole answer by 1 + r: for the bike, $901 instead of $1,001.

Real cash flows rarely fall on neat anniversaries. XNPV(rate, values, dates) takes a date for each cash flow and discounts each one by (1+r)(di−d0)/365(1 + r)^{(d_i - d_0)/365}(1+r)(di​−d0​)/365, where d0d_0d0​ is the first date: the first value is not discounted, and the rate is an effective annual rate. The dates must not fall before the first one. Worked example 2 uses XNPV for a project with cash flows on six different dates.

NPV against other goals

Firms sometimes say they aim to maximise profit, sales growth or the rate of return on their investments. Each can point the wrong way. Profit in a single year ignores what happens later and what the investment cost. Growth can be bought with projects that earn less than investors could get elsewhere. A high rate of return can come from a tiny project that adds almost nothing. NPV takes account of the timing of every cash flow, of risk through the rate, and of scale, and its answer is in the unit owners care about: dollars of wealth today. The next lesson compares it with the internal rate of return, payback and the profitability index, which firms also use.

Common mistakes

  • Discounting at the loan rate

    The rate that matters is the return given up at the project’s risk, not the interest rate on the money borrowed to pay for it. Discounted at the 9% on Lantern’s line of credit, the solar panels show about +$895; at the 11% their risk calls for, −$743. A loan’s rate prices the lender’s fairly safe claim; the project’s cash flows are riskier.

  • Reading an NPV of zero as “breaking even”

    A project with an NPV of zero repays its cost and also earns the full 11% a year on it. It is a fair deal, not a loss. A project that merely gets its money back, with no return, has a negative NPV.

  • Adding undiscounted savings

    The panels’ savings add up to $31,000 against a $19,000 cost, which looks like a $12,000 gain. Spread over ten years and discounted, they are worth $18,257. Undiscounted totals ignore the opportunity cost of capital.

  • Bundling a weak project with a strong one

    A package’s NPV is the sum of its parts’ NPVs. If one part has a negative NPV and can be dropped, the package is worth more without it, even when the package as a whole is positive.

  • Putting the year-0 cash flow inside Excel’s NPV

    Excel’s NPV() discounts its first value by one period. Add the outlay outside the function: =B4+NPV(B1,B5:B9). Inside the range, every cash flow is discounted once too often and the answer is the true NPV ÷ (1 + r).

  • Treating the NPV as a fact

    An NPV is a forecast discounted at an estimated rate. A result of a few hundred dollars on a $19,000 project is “about zero”: test the forecasts and the rate before relying on its sign.

In practice

In most firms capital budgeting is a process as much as a calculation. Proposals come from the people who see the opportunities: a café manager asking for kiosks, a delivery driver suggesting a bike. Finance staff turn each proposal into a set of cash-flow forecasts, choose the rate for its risk, and calculate the NPV. Small projects are approved by a manager within a spending limit; large ones go to the board. A year or two later, a post-audit compares what happened with what was forecast, which shows whether the people who make forecasts tend to be optimistic, and by how much.

A fictional chain of climbing gyms considers adding auto-belay devices to its walls. The finance lead prices them at the chain’s 10% rate for routine equipment, sets out the cash flows on the dates the supplier will be paid and the dates the extra memberships should begin, and uses XNPV because the first savings arrive within months. She values each gym’s installation separately rather than the chain-wide “upgrade”, because one small gym has too few members to cover the cost, and value additivity shows that the programme is worth more without it.

A fictional water utility must choose between projects whose risks differ: replacing old pipes, whose savings are steady, and a new treatment plant whose revenue depends on a regulator’s decision. Its analysts discount the two at different rates and report each NPV with the assumptions behind it, so the board can see which inputs the decision rests on.

Summary

  • Capital budgeting decides which long-term investments a firm makes; the test of each is whether it is worth more than it costs.
  • The opportunity cost of capital is the expected return investors give up elsewhere at the same risk. It depends on the project’s risk, not on how the project is financed. Ana uses 11% as a working figure until module 3 estimates it.

Terms introduced

Opportunity cost of capital
The expected return that investors give up by putting money into a project instead of into the best alternative investment of the same risk. It is the discount rate for the project’s cash flows, and it depends on the project’s risk, not on how the project is financed.
Independent projects
Projects whose acceptance or rejection does not affect each other, so each is accepted if its own NPV is positive.
Mutually exclusive projects
Projects of which at most one can be chosen, such as two machines for the same job; the one with the highest positive NPV is preferred.
Value additivity
The property that the NPV of several projects taken together equals the sum of their separate NPVs, because all are measured in today’s dollars.
Mid-year convention
A timing assumption that treats each year’s cash flow as arriving in the middle of the year, discounting it by (1 + r)^(t − 0.5) instead of (1 + r)^t.
