Financial planning

Turn a sales forecast into the money a business will need: percent-of-sales forecasts and additional funds needed, the internal and sustainable growth rates, a monthly cash budget with a line of credit, and scenarios that show what could go wrong.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Build a percent-of-sales forecast of the income statement and balance sheet and calculate the additional funds needed (AFN).
  • Adjust the forecast for spare capacity and for the interest on new borrowing, and explain the method’s assumptions.
  • Calculate and interpret the internal and sustainable growth rates, and identify what limits a firm’s growth.
  • Prepare a monthly cash budget with a minimum cash balance and a line of credit, and find the peak borrowing.
  • Use scenarios and covenant tests to judge whether a financing plan is robust.

Review first

  • Return on assets and equity — ROA, ROE and Lantern’s 2027 figures, which the growth rates build on.
  • Liquidity — The current ratio and the line of credit; a cash budget is liquidity looked at forwards.
  • Payout policy — The payout and retention ratios decide how much profit stays in the business to finance growth.
  • Capital structure — How the money a plan needs should be raised, and the covenants that come with debt.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern’s revenue in 2027 was $577,860, its first full year and the year the two cafés opened. The growth was paid for partly by profit kept in the business and mostly by other people’s money: interest-bearing debt rose from $30,000 to $65,250, and the cafés’ leases added $173,379 of lease liabilities. Would the money have been there if sales had come in later than planned? Financial planning asks that question before the money is spent.

A financial plan turns a forecast of sales and investment into forecasts of the statements, and so into the amount and timing of the money the business will need. Planning does not predict the future; it shows the financial consequences of the future the managers expect, and of the ones they fear, while there is still time to act: to arrange a loan, delay an investment, slow growth or keep more profit. Lenders ask for plans for the same reason: a borrower who has worked out when it will need cash, and how it will repay, is a better risk.

Plans work at two horizons. A long-term plan, usually annual for three to five years, asks how much outside money growth will need and what kind. A short-term plan, usually monthly for the coming year, asks whether there will be enough cash each month, given that receipts and payments do not arrive together. The first is built with pro forma statements and the second with a cash budget.

The percent-of-sales method

The simplest long-term forecast assumes that many items move in proportion to sales. Isbister Outdoor Gear, a fictional retailer of tents and hiking gear, had sales of $2,000,000 last year, a net profit margin of 6% and a payout ratio of 40%. Its assets were $1,200,000: $500,000 of current assets (inventory, receivables, cash) and $700,000 of fixed assets, all fully used. It owed $200,000 of payables and accruals, $400,000 to its bank, and had equity of $600,000. It expects sales to grow by 25% next year.

The forecast sorts each balance sheet item by what drives it. Assets needed to support sales (inventory, receivables, and fixed assets when the business is at full capacity) rise with sales. Spontaneous liabilities, mainly payables and accrued wages and taxes, rise with sales automatically: buy more stock on credit and the payables follow. Bank loans, bonds and share capital change only when someone decides to borrow or raise money, so the first draft holds them fixed. Retained earnings grow by next year’s profit less dividends. Whatever is left, assets needed minus everything that finances them, is the additional funds needed.

Key idea: Growth uses cash

A profitable business can run out of money by growing. Every extra dollar of sales ties up more inventory, receivables and equipment before it brings in cash; profit kept in the business pays for only part of that. The faster the growth, the more of it must be financed from outside.

A negative AFN is a surplus: the business generates more than it needs, and can repay debt, raise the payout or hold the cash. The AFN is a first draft of a financing need. How to raise it, by bank debt, by new equity, by cutting the dividend, is the capital-structure and payout question of this module’s other lessons.

The assumptions, and how to relax them

Percent of sales is a starting point, and every assumption in it can fail.

  • Constant ratios. Some assets grow more slowly than sales because of economies of scale: a warehouse that serves $2 million of sales may serve $3 million. Some grow faster: a new market may need more inventory per dollar of sales. Fixed costs do not move with sales at all, so the profit margin may rise as the business grows.
  • Full capacity. If fixed assets have spare capacity, sales can grow without new ones until the capacity is used.
  • Smooth assets. Real fixed assets come in lumps: half a delivery van cannot be bought. A plan may need a large investment in one year and none in the next.
  • Financing has no feedback. New borrowing brings interest, which lowers profit and so retained earnings, which raises the need a little. The forecast must be iterated, or the interest estimated in advance.
  • Payout and margin fixed. Managers can change the dividend or cut costs, and a plan should show what each would do.

Spare capacity. Suppose Isbister’s fixed assets were running at 80% of capacity. How fast can a business grow without outside money?

Two benchmark growth rates come from setting the AFN to zero. The internal growth rate is the fastest a firm can grow with no outside money of any kind: no new borrowing, no new shares and, in the strict version, no increase in spontaneous liabilities either, so retained earnings must pay for every new asset. The sustainable growth rate is the fastest it can grow with no new shares while keeping its debt-to-equity ratio constant: retained earnings add to equity, and debt grows in proportion. With bbb the retention ratio and ROA and ROE measured on end-of-year totals:

IGR=ROA×b1−ROA×b,SGR=ROE×b1−ROE×b\text{IGR} = \frac{\text{ROA} \times b}{1 - \text{ROA} \times b}, \qquad \text{SGR} = \frac{\text{ROE} \times b}{1 - \text{ROE} \times b}IGR=1−ROA×bROA×b​,SGR=1−ROE×bROE×b​

For Isbister, ROA =120,000÷1,200,000=10%= 120{,}000 \div 1{,}200{,}000 = 10\%=120,000÷1,200,000=10%, ROE =120,000÷600,000=20%= 120{,}000 \div 600{,}000 = 20\%=120,000÷600,000=20% and b=0.6b = 0.6b=0.6. The internal growth rate is 0.06÷0.94=6.38%0.06 \div 0.94 = 6.38\%0.06÷0.94=6.38% and the sustainable growth rate 0.12÷0.88=13.64%0.12 \div 0.88 = 13.64\%0.12÷0.88=13.64%. At 6.38% growth, with no new liabilities, the retained profit exactly pays for the new assets. At 13.64%, the retained profit raises equity by 13.64%, and $54,545 of new bank debt (the bank loan growing by the same 13.64%) keeps the debt-to-equity ratio where it was. Isbister’s planned 25% is well above both: it must raise new equity, borrow more than its current leverage allows, retain more, or grow more slowly.

The sustainable growth rate can be split into the levers that set it. By the DuPont identity of F03, ROE is the profit margin times asset turnover times the equity multiplier, so a firm can grow faster without new shares only by earning a higher margin, using its assets more intensively, borrowing more against its equity, or retaining more of its profit. If its sales grow faster than its SGR, one of those must change or it must issue shares. That makes the SGR a consistency check on a plan: a plan that assumes 25% growth, a constant payout and constant leverage, with an SGR of 13.6%, cannot be financed as written.

Note: A convention to watch

These formulas use ROA and ROE on end-of-year totals. F03 measures ROA and ROE on average balances, which gives higher figures for a growing firm. The SGR also equals retained earnings divided by equity at the start of the year: (NI−Dividends)÷Estart(\text{NI} - \text{Dividends}) \div E_{\text{start}}(NI−Dividends)÷Estart​, which avoids the conversion.

Lantern’s 2027 figures give a historical calculation. Net income was $22,431, the dividend $2,000 (so b=91.1%b = 91.1\%b=91.1%), total assets at the year end $327,883 and equity $62,818. ROA on the year-end total is 6.84% and ROE 35.71%, giving an internal growth rate of 6.65% and a sustainable growth rate of 48.2%. The SGR is exactly how fast equity grew from retained profit: 20,431÷42,387=48.2%20,431 \div 42,387 = 48.2\%20,431÷42,387=48.2%, from $42,387 to $62,818. It is so high because Lantern’s equity is thin: each dollar of it supported $5.22 of assets at the year end, much of it leased. Keeping that leverage constant as Lantern grows would mean borrowing and leasing on the same scale again, so the SGR describes 2027’s financing rather than a growth rate Lantern could rely on.

The cash budget

Annual forecasts hide the months. A business with a seasonal trade can be profitable over the year and still run out of cash in the months when it buys stock before it sells it. A cash budget lists, month by month, the cash expected in (collections from customers, which lag sales on credit), the cash expected out (suppliers, wages, rent, interest, tax, equipment, dividends) and the resulting balance. Depreciation is not in it, because it is not a payment. The business sets a minimum cash balance as a buffer for surprises, and plans to draw on a line of credit when cash would fall below it and to repay when it rises above.

Sallowfield Garden Centre, a fictional business, buys plants and stock in winter for its spring trade. It starts January with $25,000 of cash, wants to keep at least $15,000, and has an $80,000 line of credit at 1% a month. Interest each month is charged on the balance drawn at the start of the month; draws and repayments happen at the end of the month.

Cash falls below the minimum in each of the first three months, and the line peaks at $60,571 at the end of March, inside the $80,000 limit. The budget tells the owner how large a line to arrange, when the bank will be repaid, and that the plan relies on spring sales arriving on time.

Scenarios and covenants

A plan built on one forecast hides its risk. Scenario planning reruns it under other assumptions: a bad spring, a late payment from a large customer, a supplier who wants paying sooner. Suppose Sallowfield’s receipts in March, April and May come in 20% below the budget. The line then peaks at $72,571 at the end of March, uncomfortably close to its $80,000 limit, and $22,390 is still drawn at the end of June. The owner can see now that a wet spring would need a larger line, cheaper stock or later payments to suppliers.

Lenders’ conditions belong in the plan too. Loans and credit lines often carry covenants: a minimum current ratio or interest coverage, a maximum debt to EBITDA, and for many credit lines a requirement to repay the line in full for a period each year, to show it is financing seasonal stock rather than permanent needs. A plan should forecast each covenant ratio alongside the statements, in every scenario, because a breach lets the lender demand repayment just when the business is short of cash. The capital-structure lesson showed the same test on Winterbourne Ferries.

Common mistakes

  • “A profitable business doesn’t need to borrow to grow.”

    Growth needs assets before it brings in cash. Isbister earns 6% on sales and retains 60% of it, yet needs $160,000 of outside money to grow by 25%: its internal growth rate is only 6.38%.

  • Growing every balance sheet item with sales

    Bank loans, bonds and share capital change only by decision, so the forecast holds them fixed and the AFN shows how much new financing is needed. Growing them with sales assumes the financing has already been arranged and hides the gap.

  • Ignoring spare capacity

    If equipment is under-used, sales can grow without new fixed assets. Isbister’s need turns from $160,000 to a $15,000 surplus at 80% utilisation. Ask how hard the assets are working before scaling them.

  • Putting depreciation or accrued revenue in a cash budget

    A cash budget records cash when it moves: collections when customers pay, suppliers when they are paid. Depreciation is not a payment, and sales on credit are not receipts until collected.

  • “The sustainable growth rate is the growth the business can sustain.”

    It is the growth possible with no new shares, constant leverage, margin, turnover and payout. Lantern’s 48.2% for 2027 reflects thin equity and heavy leasing; a different mix of financing gives a different rate. Use it as a consistency check on a plan, not as a forecast.

In practice

A finance director prepares for the bank. The finance director of a fictional furniture maker growing at 20% a year builds a three-year percent-of-sales forecast, then refines it: separate rules for inventory and receivables based on days outstanding (F03’s working-capital ratios), a new factory line in year two rather than spread smoothly, and interest on the new borrowing iterated into the profit forecast. The AFN in each year sets the size of the loan she asks for; a monthly cash budget for the first year sets the line of credit; and the forecast covenant ratios, in a base case and a downturn, show the bank the loan is repayable.

A founder chooses a growth rate. The founder of a fictional skincare brand finds that her sustainable growth rate is 15% while her plan assumes 40%. Rather than assume finance will appear, she lists the levers: raise the margin by cutting promotion, speed up collections to lift asset turnover, stop paying herself a dividend, borrow more, or sell shares to an investor. Each changes the AFN, and each has a cost, the capital-structure and payout trade-offs of this module.

A seasonal business arranges its line. A fictional ski-hire business runs its monthly cash budget each summer for the winter ahead under three scenarios: a normal season, a late snowfall and a poor season. It arranges a line of credit large enough for the poor-season peak, agrees the repayment date with the bank before the season, and watches actual cash against the budget each month, so that a shortfall shows up in December rather than in March.

Summary

  • Financial planning turns forecasts of sales and investment into forecasts of the statements, so that financing needs are known before money is spent.
  • Percent of sales: assets and spontaneous liabilities move with sales; debt and equity change only by decision; retained earnings grow by profit less dividends. AFN = (A*/S₀)ΔS − (L*/S₀)ΔS − mS₁(1 − d). Isbister: $160,000.
  • Relax the assumptions: spare capacity can remove the need (Isbister: a $15,000 surplus at 80% utilisation); interest on new debt raises it (to $165,975).
  • Internal growth rate = ROA·b ÷ (1 − ROA·b): growth with no outside money. Sustainable growth rate = ROE·b ÷ (1 − ROE·b): no new shares, constant leverage. Isbister: 6.38% and 13.64%.
  • Growth faster than the SGR needs a higher margin, turnover, leverage or retention, or new shares. Lantern’s 2027 SGR of 48.2% reflects thin equity.
  • A cash budget tracks receipts and payments month by month, with a minimum balance and a line of credit. Sallowfield’s line peaks at $60,571 in March and is repaid by May.
  • Scenarios and covenant tests show whether the plan survives bad outcomes; a 20% shortfall in spring leaves Sallowfield owing $22,390 at the end of June.

Terms introduced

Additional funds needed
The outside financing a firm needs to support forecast sales: the increase in assets required, less the increase in spontaneous liabilities and the earnings retained. Abbreviated AFN.
Spontaneous liabilities
Liabilities, such as accounts payable and accrued wages and taxes, that rise automatically with sales as part of normal operations, without a financing decision.
Internal growth rate
The highest growth rate a firm can achieve with no outside financing of any kind, financing new assets only from retained earnings: ROA × b ÷ (1 − ROA × b) on end-of-year assets.
Sustainable growth rate
The highest growth rate a firm can achieve without issuing new shares while keeping its debt-to-equity ratio constant: ROE × b ÷ (1 − ROE × b) on end-of-year equity.
Cash budget
A forecast, usually month by month, of a business’s cash receipts, cash payments and cash balance, showing when it will need to borrow and when it can repay.
