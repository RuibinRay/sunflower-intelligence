The cost of debt and equity

Estimate what lenders and shareholders require: the cost of debt from yields, after tax; the cost of equity from the CAPM, the dividend growth model and a bond-yield check; and, for a private firm like Lantern, a beta borrowed from listed peers.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain why a firm’s cost of capital is the return its investors require now on investments of similar risk, not the rate it happened to pay in the past.
  • Calculate the cost of debt as a yield to maturity and after tax, and choose the right rate for a private firm.
  • Calculate the cost of equity with the capital asset pricing model and the dividend growth model, and check it with the bond-yield-plus-premium rule.
  • Estimate a private firm’s beta from listed peers by unlevering, averaging and relevering their betas.
  • Evaluate the judgements in each input, including the contested size and illiquidity premium, and calculate the cost of preferred stock.

Review first

  • Capital and liquidity — The cost of capital was introduced there in words: what lenders and owners expect in return.
  • Six principles behind financial decisions — Systematic and unsystematic risk, and why diversification removes only one of them.
  • Discounting cash flows — A bond’s yield to maturity is its internal rate of return.
  • Perpetuities and growth — The Gordon growth model and the value of a preferred share, which this lesson turns round to find a required return.
  • Net present value — The opportunity cost of capital and Ana’s working rate of 11%.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Since module 1 Ana has discounted Lantern’s projects at 11% a year, a figure she chose as a reasonable working rate. The roaster, Cold Brew and the kiosks all passed or failed against it. Before she commits money in April 2028 she wants to know where such a rate comes from: what Lantern’s lenders and Lantern’s owner actually require, estimated rather than assumed. This lesson estimates the two pieces; the next combines them into one rate.

A business has two broad sources of long-term money. Lenders provide debt under a contract that fixes interest and repayment; shareholders provide equity and keep whatever is left after everyone else has been paid. Each group could put its money elsewhere. The cost of capital is the return each group requires to leave its money in this business rather than in the best alternative of the same risk. It is an opportunity cost: set in the capital market by what investors can earn elsewhere, not by the business’s own accounts.

Two consequences follow, and they run through the whole lesson. First, the cost of capital is forward-looking: it is what investors require today for new money, not what the firm agreed to pay years ago. Second, it depends on risk: lenders, who are paid first and have a contract, require less than shareholders, who are paid last and have none. For the same reason a riskier firm pays more for both.

The cost of debt is the easier of the two to see, because lenders state a rate. Even so, the right number is the yield lenders would require on new borrowing now, and because interest is deductible for tax, the firm’s cost is lower than the rate the lender receives. The cost of equity has no contract behind it at all. It must be estimated from what shareholders could expect elsewhere for bearing similar risk, using a model of how risk is priced. That estimate is the hardest number in corporate finance, and this lesson is honest about how much judgement it needs.

Key idea: The cost of capital belongs to the use of the money

Investors require a return that matches the risk of what their money is used for. A firm cannot lower the return its shareholders require by paying them less, or lower its cost of debt by remembering a cheap old loan. What it can do is estimate the market’s requirement as carefully as possible and say which judgements the estimate rests on.

In depth

The cost of debt: a yield, not a coupon

A bond promises fixed interest payments, its coupons, and the face value at maturity. The coupon rate is set when the bond is issued and never changes; the bond’s price moves instead, so that a buyer today earns the return the market now requires. That return is the yield to maturity (YTM): the discount rate at which the present value of the remaining payments equals today’s price. F04 showed it is the bond’s internal rate of return to a buyer who holds it to maturity.

Brightcup Coffee, one of Lantern’s fictional listed peers, has a bond with six years left, a 5% coupon paid once a year and a face value of $100. It trades at $96.20. Its coupon is 5%, but nobody buying it today earns 5%: they pay $96.20, collect $5 a year and receive $100 at the end. The yield that makes those payments worth $96.20 is 5.77%, and that, not the coupon, is what lenders currently require from Brightcup. When a bond trades below face value its yield is above its coupon; above face value, below.

The yield to maturity is the promised return: it assumes every payment arrives. For a safe borrower the promised and expected returns are almost the same. For a risky one the expected return is lower, because some payments may not arrive, so a high yield overstates what debt really costs a firm close to default. For the borrowers in this course the difference is small and the promised yield is used.

Interest is a deductible expense, so each dollar of interest saves the firm 25 cents of tax at Lantern’s rate. The cost that matters to the firm is the after-tax cost of debt, rD(1−T)r_D(1 - T)rD​(1−T). Brightcup’s is 5.77%×(1−0.25)=4.33%5.77\% \times (1 - 0.25) = 4.33\%5.77%×(1−0.25)=4.33%. The saving exists only if the firm has taxable profits to deduct the interest from; a firm making losses (or a lender’s own tax position) does not enter. Lantern made a profit of $22,431 in 2027, so the deduction is worth having.

The cost of debt for a private firm

Lantern has no bonds trading in a market, so it has no yield to read. The equivalent question is: what rate would a lender charge Lantern today for long-term money? Coastline Bank has answered it: 7.5% a year for a new five-year term loan. That is the cost of debt, before tax. Lantern’s older rates are history. The first Coastline note at 6% was agreed in 2026 and is repaid on 1 March 2028; the 7.2% Coastline quoted for an equipment loan in March 2027 was the market’s price a year earlier. Neither tells Ana what new debt costs now.

The line of credit at 9% is a different kind of debt: short-term, drawn and repaid as cash needs rise and fall, and priced at a floating rate. It finances working capital rather than long-lived projects, and the next lesson leaves it out of the weights. Lantern’s after-tax cost of long-term debt is therefore 7.5%×(1−0.25)=5.625%7.5\% \times (1 - 0.25) = 5.625\%7.5%×(1−0.25)=5.625%, or 5.63% to two decimal places. A listed firm without traded bonds does something similar: it looks up the yield on bonds of other firms with the same credit rating, or adds a default spread for its rating to the risk-free rate.

The cost of equity: the capital asset pricing model

Shareholders are promised nothing, so their required return must be inferred. The standard tool is the capital asset pricing model (CAPM). It starts from F00’s point that diversified investors can remove unsystematic risk cheaply, so the market pays a premium only for systematic risk, the part of a share’s ups and downs that moves with the market as a whole. The CAPM says a share’s required return is the risk-free rate plus its beta times the market risk premium:

rE=rf+βE×MRPr_E = r_f + \beta_E \times \text{MRP}rE​=rf​+βE​×MRP

Beta measures how strongly a share’s returns move with the market’s. A beta of 1 means the share tends to rise and fall with the market one for one; 1.5 means it tends to move 50% more; 0.5, half as much. It is estimated by regressing the share’s past returns on the market’s, usually over two to five years of weekly or monthly data, and the estimate is noisy: OpenStax’s example quotes IBM’s beta as 0.97 from one data service and 1.25 from another. F06 Investment Analysis derives the CAPM and treats beta in depth; here it is a tool.

Each input needs a judgement. The risk-free rate is usually the yield on a government bond in the same currency as the cash flows, with a maturity close to the investments being valued, so a long-term bond yield for long-lived projects rather than a three-month bill. The market risk premium (MRP) is the extra return investors expect from the market as a whole over that bond. It cannot be observed; it is estimated either from history (the average past excess return, which depends heavily on the period chosen) or implied from today’s share prices and expected cash payouts. Aswath Damodaran publishes an implied premium for US shares every year: his table gives values between 4.23% and 5.94% for the years 2020 to 2025, and 4.23% for 2025, when his Treasury bond rate was 4.18%. Ana’s assumed 5.5% sits within that range, towards the top; another analyst could defend 4.5%.

A beta for a firm with no share price

Lantern’s shares are not traded, so it has no beta to estimate. The usual answer is to borrow one from listed firms in the same business, the comparable firms or peers. But a peer’s equity beta reflects two kinds of risk: the risk of its business, and the extra risk its shareholders carry because of its debt. Debt has a fixed claim, so the more of it there is, the more the shareholders’ residual return swings. A peer with more debt than Lantern would overstate Lantern’s risk, and one with less would understate it.

The fix is to strip each peer’s leverage out, average the business risk, and put Lantern’s own leverage back in. A peer’s equity beta is its levered beta; removing the effect of its debt gives its unlevered beta (or asset beta), the beta its shares would have with no debt. Using Hamada’s formula, which assumes the firm’s debt is risk-free (a debt beta of zero) and that its debt is kept in proportion to its value:

βU=βL1+(1−T) D/EβL=βU[1+(1−T) D/E]\beta_U = \dfrac{\beta_L}{1 + (1 - T)\,D/E} \qquad \beta_L = \beta_U \left[1 + (1 - T)\,D/E\right]βU​=1+(1−T)D/EβL​​βL​=βU​[1+(1−T)D/E]

To relever, Ana needs Lantern’s debt-to-equity ratio. The relevant one is the mix she intends to keep, a target of 20% debt and 80% equity by value (the next lesson explains why a target, and why market values), which is a D/E of 0.20÷0.80=0.250.20 \div 0.80 = 0.250.20÷0.80=0.25. The size and illiquidity premium: a contested addition

Ana adds 3 percentage points to that 9.75%, making Lantern’s cost of equity 12.75%. Her reasons are common in the valuation of small private firms: Lantern is far smaller than its listed peers, its shares cannot be sold on an exchange, and its owner has most of her wealth in it. Investors, the argument runs, demand more for owning such a firm than the CAPM allows.

The case against is strong, and you should know it. Damodaran, reviewing the evidence in 2015, found that the small-firm premium in US share returns was large before 1980 but seemed to have faded since, was concentrated in the very smallest firms and in January, and was barely statistically significant; he wrote that he has never used one and recommended putting the risks of small firms into the cash flows (for example, a probability of failure) rather than into the discount rate. There is a theoretical point too: the CAPM prices only systematic risk, and the risk of a small firm’s failure is largely specific to it. Ana, being undiversified, does bear that risk; a diversified buyer would not pay her for it.

Caution: Say what you added, and why

This course follows Ana’s choice and adds the 3% to the CAPM, so Lantern’s cost of equity is 12.75%, but it shows the figure without it (9.75%) beside it. When you see a cost of equity for a private firm, ask whether it includes such a premium and how large: a premium of a few points changes a WACC by nearly as much, and can decide a close project.

Other estimates of the cost of equity

The dividend growth model turns F04’s Gordon growth model round. If a share’s price is the present value of dividends growing for ever at ggg, P0=D1÷(rE−g)P_0 = D_1 \div (r_E - g)P0​=D1​÷(rE​−g), then the return the price implies is rE=D1÷P0+gr_E = D_1 \div P_0 + grE​=D1​÷P0​+g: the dividend yield plus growth. Glenmere Mills, a fictional listed flour miller, trades at $42.00 and has just paid $1.60 a year in dividends, which analysts expect to grow at 4.5% a year. Next year’s dividend is 1.60×1.045=1.6721.60 \times 1.045 = 1.6721.60×1.045=1.672 dollars, and the implied cost of equity is 1.672÷42.00+4.5%=8.48%1.672 \div 42.00 + 4.5\% = 8.48\%1.672÷42.00+4.5%=8.48%. The model suits mature firms with steady dividends; it fails for firms that pay no dividend or an irregular one, and the answer moves one for one with the growth rate, the least certain input. It cannot be used for Lantern, whose dividends ($300 and $4,700 in 2026, $2,000 in 2027) follow no growth path and whose shares have no market price.

The bond-yield-plus-premium rule of thumb adds a judgemental premium to the firm’s own cost of debt, on the logic that shareholders bear more risk than the firm’s own lenders: rE≈rD+premiumr_E \approx r_D + \text{premium}rE​≈rD​+premium. It has no theory behind the size of the premium, but it is a useful check because it starts from the firm’s own credit risk. With a premium of 4 points, Lantern’s gives 7.5%+4%=11.5%7.5\% + 4\% = 11.5\%7.5%+4%=11.5%; Glenmere’s, from its 5.2% bond yield, gives 9.2%.

The cost of preferred stock

A preferred stock pays a fixed dividend for ever (unless the issuer redeems it), so F04 valued it as a perpetuity, P=DP÷rPP = D_P \div r_PP=DP​÷rP​. Turned round, its cost is rP=DP÷PPr_P = D_P \div P_PrP​=DP​÷PP​. Preferred dividends are paid out of after-tax profit, so unlike interest they earn no tax deduction and need no (1−T)(1 - T)(1−T). Lantern has none.

Common mistakes

  • Using the coupon, or an old loan’s rate, as the cost of debt

    The coupon and the rate on an existing loan were set when the money was borrowed. The cost of debt is what lenders require now: the yield to maturity for traded debt, or a lender’s current quote (Coastline’s 7.5%) for a private firm. Lantern’s 6% note says nothing about what new borrowing costs.

  • Forgetting the tax deduction, or applying it to equity

    Interest is deductible, so debt costs rD(1−T)r_D(1 - T)rD​(1−T). Dividends are not, so the cost of equity (and of preferred stock) needs no tax adjustment. Taking tax off both, or off neither, misstates every rate built on them.

  • Using a peer’s equity beta as it stands

    A peer’s equity beta includes the effect of its own debt. Harbour Street’s 1.25 reflects a D/E of 0.55, far above Lantern’s target. Unlever each peer with its own D/E, average, then relever at the target firm’s D/E.

  • Treating the cost of equity as a precise number

    Beta, the market risk premium and any extra premium are estimates with wide ranges. Quoting 12.754% suggests a precision the inputs do not have. Report the estimate to a sensible precision, show the range from reasonable inputs, and check it with a second method.

  • Mismatching the risk-free rate and the premium

    A market risk premium is measured over a particular rate, usually a long-term government bond. Combining a premium measured over long-term bonds with a short-term bill rate (or the other way round) mixes two bases. Use the rate that matches the premium and the life of the investments.

  • Forcing the dividend growth model on a firm without steady dividends

    The model needs a market price and a dividend that grows steadily for ever, at a rate below the cost of equity. For a private firm, a firm that pays no dividend or one whose growth is temporarily high, it gives nonsense; use the CAPM with peers instead.

In practice

Ana’s assumptions note. Before using a new rate, Ana writes one page for her accountant and for anyone who may later invest. It lists each input, its source and a reasonable range: the risk-free rate (4.2%, a long-term government bond yield, as an assumption for February 2028), the market risk premium (5.5%, inside the range of published implied estimates), the three peers and why each is comparable, the target debt share, Coastline’s 7.5% quote, and the 3% premium with the arguments against it. She shows the cost of equity three ways: 9.75% (CAPM), 12.75% (with the premium) and 11.5% (Coastline’s rate plus 4 points). A reader who disagrees with one judgement can change it and see what follows.

A listed firm’s annual review. A treasurer like Glenmere’s updates the inputs once a year, or when markets move sharply: the bond yield from current prices, beta from two data services and its own regression, the dividend model with analysts’ growth forecasts. She reports a range and a point estimate to the board, and the finance team uses the point estimate until the next review. The discipline is in using the same method every year, so that a change in the rate reflects a change in the market, not in the analyst.

A lender’s view. A credit officer pricing a five-year loan for a café group does the first half of this lesson in reverse: she starts from the bank’s funding cost, adds a margin for the borrower’s risk and arrives at the rate she quotes. Coastline’s 7.5% to Lantern is such a price, and is higher than its 7.2% quote of March 2027. The borrower’s cost of debt is the lender’s required return, seen from the other side.

Summary

  • The cost of capital is the return investors require now on investments of similar risk: an opportunity cost set in the market, not a figure from the accounts.
  • The cost of debt is the yield to maturity on the firm’s debt (or a lender’s current quote), not the coupon; after tax it is r_D(1 − T). Lantern: 7.5% before tax, 5.63% after.

  • Cross-check with the dividend growth model (D₁ ÷ P₀ + g, for steady dividend payers) and the bond-yield-plus-premium rule.

Terms introduced

Coupon rate
The interest a bond pays each year as a percentage of its face value, fixed when the bond is issued.
Yield to maturity
The discount rate at which the present value of a bond’s remaining payments equals its price today: the return a buyer earns by holding it to maturity if every payment is made. Abbreviated YTM.
After-tax cost of debt
The cost of borrowing to a firm once the tax saving on deductible interest is allowed for: the pre-tax cost of debt × (1 − tax rate).
Cost of equity
The return a firm’s shareholders require for bearing the risk of its shares: the return they could expect elsewhere on investments of similar risk.
Capital asset pricing model
A model in which a security’s required return is the risk-free rate plus its beta times the market risk premium, because only systematic risk is rewarded. Abbreviated CAPM.
Beta
A measure of how strongly a security’s returns move with the market’s: 1 for a security that moves with the market, above 1 for one that moves more, below 1 for one that moves less.
Market risk premium
The return investors expect from the market as a whole above the risk-free rate. It cannot be observed and is estimated from history or from current prices. Also called the equity risk premium.
Levered beta
A firm’s equity beta as it is, including the extra risk that its debt places on its shareholders.
Unlevered beta
The beta a firm’s equity would have if it had no debt: a measure of the risk of its business alone. Also called the asset beta.
Size premium
An extra return some analysts add to the cost of equity of small or privately held firms for their size and illiquidity, beyond what the capital asset pricing model gives. Its evidence is contested.
