Bond prices and yields

Price a bond as the present value of its payments, read its yields, measure how far its price moves when interest rates change (duration and convexity), and see what credit risk adds to a yield.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate a bond’s price from its coupon, maturity and yield to maturity, and its yield to maturity from its price.
  • Distinguish the coupon rate, the current yield and the yield to maturity, and explain premium and discount bonds and the pull to par.
  • Calculate Macaulay and modified duration, and use them, with convexity, to estimate the price change for a change in yields.
  • Explain credit risk, ratings and credit spreads, and calculate a spread in basis points.
  • Interpret a bond fund’s loss in a year of rising rates using its duration.

Review first

  • Annuities — A bond’s coupons are an annuity; its price is the present value of that annuity plus the face value.
  • Discounting cash flows — Discounting a stream of cash flows, and the yield as an internal rate of return.
  • The cost of debt and equity — The yield to maturity as the cost of debt, with Brightcup Coffee’s bond and the RATE function.
  • Shares and markets — Where bonds sit in the queue of claims, and how securities trade.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana’s retirement account could also hold the Harbourline Aggregate Bond Index Fund, which holds investment-grade government and corporate bonds, average duration about 6 years. In the course’s simulated history it was the calm part of a portfolio: it turned $1 at the start of 2013 into $1.88 by the end of 2027, and lost money in only one calendar year. That year, 2022, it fell 13%, while the market fund also fell. Ana wants to know how a fund of high-quality bonds, whose issuers paid every coupon, could lose 13% in a year, and whether it could happen again. The answer is in how bond prices respond to interest rates. (The market history in this course is simulated for teaching and the funds are fictional; real history should be checked against sources such as Aswath Damodaran’s annually updated historical returns.)

A bond is a loan that can be traded. The issuer (a government or a company) borrows by selling bonds, and promises each holder fixed interest payments, the coupons, and repayment of the face value on the maturity date. The coupon rate is fixed when the bond is issued. Once it trades, though, a bond’s price moves every day, and the return a buyer earns depends on the price she pays.

The link is the one F04 taught for any stream of cash flows: a bond’s price is the present value of its remaining payments, discounted at the return investors currently require for that kind of bond. That return is its yield to maturity. If investors come to require more, because interest rates in the economy have risen or the issuer looks riskier, the same fixed payments are discounted at a higher rate and the price falls. Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions, always.

How far the price moves depends on how long the investor must wait for the money. A payment due in six months is barely affected by a change in the discount rate; a payment due in twenty years is affected a great deal. The measure of that waiting time, and so of a bond’s sensitivity to interest rates, is its duration. The Harbourline bond fund’s average duration is about 6 years, which is why 2022 hurt.

Key idea: Price and yield are two ways of stating the same thing

Given a bond’s payments, every price corresponds to exactly one yield to maturity and every yield to one price. Saying “the yield rose from 4.20% to 5.20%” and “the price fell from $99.11 to $94.78” describes the same event for the five-year government note.

In depth

The terms of a bond

Every bond states its issuer, its face value (usually quoted per $100, or a multiple of $1,000 for individual bonds), its coupon rate, how often the coupon is paid and its maturity date. OpenStax notes that bond yields are expressed in annual percentage terms unless otherwise indicated. With coupons paid twice a year, a 4% coupon on $100 means $2.00 every six months, and a yield quoted as 4.20% a year means 2.10% a half-year. Prices are quoted per $100 of face value, so a price of $99.11 means a bond with $1,000 of face value costs $991.06.

Government bonds issued in a country’s own currency are treated as free of default risk. Corporate bonds are not, and the two corporate bonds above are issued by F05’s listed peers. A bond with no coupons at all, a zero-coupon bond, pays only its face value at maturity and is sold at a deep discount instead.

Pricing a bond

The price is the present value of the coupons, an annuity, plus the present value of the face value. With mmm payments a year, an annual yield yyy and nnn remaining payments:

P=Cm×1−(1+y/m)−ny/m+F(1+y/m)nP = \dfrac{C}{m} \times \dfrac{1 - (1 + y/m)^{-n}}{y/m} + \dfrac{F}{(1 + y/m)^{n}}P=mC​×y/m1−(1+y/m)−n​+(1+y/m)nF​

The five-year government note pays $2.00 every half-year for ten half-years and $100 at the end, and yields 4.20%, or 2.10% a half-year. The annuity factor is (1−1.021−10)÷0.021=8.936(1 - 1.021^{-10}) \div 0.021 = 8.936(1−1.021−10)÷0.021=8.936, so the coupons are worth 2.00×8.936=17.872.00 \times 8.936 = 17.872.00×8.936=17.87 dollars. The face value is worth 100÷1.02110=81.23100 \div 1.021^{10} = 81.23100÷1.02110=81.23 dollars. The price is 17.87+81.23=99.1117.87 + 81.23 = 99.1117.87+81.23=99.11 dollars. A spreadsheet gives the same answer with =PV(4.2%/2,10,-2,-100); Excel’s PRICE and YIELD functions need settlement and maturity dates, so this course builds prices with PV and yields with RATE.

The note trades below its $100 face value because its 4% coupon is below the 4.20% investors now require. OpenStax puts the rule in one line: when the coupon rate is less than the yield to maturity, the bond sells at a discount, and when the coupon rate exceeds the yield to maturity, it sells at a premium. A discount bond pays its buyer part of her return as a gain from the price up to face value; a premium bond pays a coupon above the market’s rate and loses the excess back as its price falls to face value.

Three yields

F05 used the yield to maturity as a firm’s cost of debt and found it from a price with the RATE function. Two other yields are quoted alongside it, and the three answer different questions.

  • The coupon rate is the annual coupon divided by face value: 4.00% for the note. It never changes.
  • The current yield is the annual coupon divided by the price: 4.00÷99.11=4.04%4.00 \div 99.11 = 4.04\%4.00÷99.11=4.04%. It measures the income a buyer receives each year on the money she pays, but ignores the gain or loss as the price moves to $100.
  • The yield to maturity includes both the coupons and that gain or loss: 4.20%. It is the internal rate of return a buyer earns if she holds the bond to maturity, every payment arrives and the coupons can be reinvested at the same rate.

For a discount bond the three are always in the order coupon rate < current yield < yield to maturity; for a premium bond the order reverses; at par all three are equal. The Harbour Street 2035 bond shows the order: coupon 5.50%, current yield 5.50÷96.62=5.69%5.50 \div 96.62 = 5.69\%5.50÷96.62=5.69%, yield to maturity 6.10%.

The pull to par

A bond’s price must reach its face value on the maturity date, whatever happens before. If the yield stays where it is, a discount bond’s price rises towards $100 and a premium bond’s falls towards $100, a path called the pull to par. The table shows the five-year note at an unchanged 4.20% yield.

An investor who buys at $99.11 and sells six months later, with the yield unchanged, earns the $2.00 coupon plus a small price gain to $99.19: (99.19−99.11+2.00)÷99.11=2.10%(99.19 - 99.11 + 2.00) \div 99.11 = 2.10\%(99.19−99.11+2.00)÷99.11=2.10% for the half-year, exactly the yield per period. When yields do not change, a bond earns its yield to maturity in every period. When they do change, the return in that period differs from the yield, sometimes by a great deal, which is the subject of the rest of this lesson.

Interest-rate risk and the price–yield curve

Interest-rate risk is the risk that a bond’s price falls because yields rise. OpenStax states the core fact: when interest rates rise, bond values fall. The figure plots the 20-year government bond’s price against its yield. The curve slopes down, and it is bowed: as the yield rises, each further point of yield lowers the price by less.

Compare the two government bonds when every yield rises by one percentage point. The five-year note falls from $99.11 to $94.78, a loss of 4.37%; the 20-year bond falls from $98.06 to $86.32, a loss of 11.97%. Longer bonds carry more interest-rate risk, because more of their value lies in payments far in the future. Lower coupons add to it for the same reason: with smaller coupons, more of the value is in the face value at the end.

The final payment, $102, is 83.6% of the price, so the note’s duration, 4.58 years, is close to its five-year maturity. A zero-coupon bond’s duration equals its maturity exactly, because it has only one payment. Coupon bonds always have a duration shorter than their maturity.

Modified duration turns the waiting time into a price sensitivity. It is Macaulay duration divided by one plus the yield per period, and it gives the approximate percentage change in price for a change in yield:

Dmod=DMac1+y/m,ΔPP≈−Dmod×ΔyD_{\text{mod}} = \dfrac{D_{\text{Mac}}}{1 + y/m}, \qquad \dfrac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}} \times \Delta yDmod​=1+y/mDMac​​,PΔP​≈−Dmod​×Δy

The five-year note’s modified duration is 4.58÷1.021=4.484.58 \div 1.021 = 4.484.58÷1.021=4.48, so a one-point rise in yields lowers its price by about 4.48%; the exact fall is 4.37%. FINRA gives investors the same rule of thumb: if a bond has a duration of 10 and interest rates increase by 1 percentage point, its price would be expected to decline by approximately 10 percent. Durations are measured in years, and for a portfolio or a fund they are the value-weighted average of the holdings’ durations.

Convexity

Duration draws a straight line through a curve, so it is accurate only for small changes in yield. For the 20-year bond, modified duration of 13.02 predicts a fall of 13.02% for a one-point rise; the true fall is 11.97%. For a one-point fall it predicts a rise of 13.02%; the true rise is 14.21%. The bond loses less than duration predicts when yields rise and gains more when they fall. That asymmetry is convexity, the curvature in the figure, and adding a second term improves the estimate:

ΔPP≈−Dmod Δy+12 Cx (Δy)2\dfrac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}}\,\Delta y + \tfrac{1}{2}\,C_x\,(\Delta y)^2PΔP​≈−Dmod​Δy+21​Cx​(Δy)2

The 20-year bond’s convexity is 223.1 (in years squared). For a one-point rise the estimate becomes −13.02×0.01+0.5×223.1×0.012=−13.02%+1.12%=−11.90%-13.02 \times 0.01 + 0.5 \times 223.1 \times 0.01^2 = -13.02\% + 1.12\% = -11.90\%−13.02×0.01+0.5×223.1×0.012=−13.02%+1.12%=−11.90%, close to the true −11.97%. Convexity matters most for long bonds and large moves; for the five-year note and a one-point move it adds only 0.12 points. Other things equal, investors value convexity, because it cushions losses and adds to gains.

Credit risk, ratings and spreads

A corporate bond carries credit risk: the risk that the issuer pays late, pays less or does not pay at all. Investors demand a higher yield to bear it. Credit rating agencies grade issuers’ ability to pay; OpenStax describes a bond rating as a grade that indicates credit quality, and states that any bond rated BBB (S&P, Fitch) or Baa (Moody’s) or higher is referred to as investment grade. Lower-rated bonds are often called non-investment grade or high yield.

The extra yield over a government bond of the same maturity is the credit spread, usually quoted in basis points (hundredths of a percentage point). There is no seven-year government bond in the table, so estimate its yield by interpolating between the five-year (4.20%) and the 20-year (4.65%): 4.20%+(2÷15)×0.45%=4.26%4.20\% + (2 \div 15) \times 0.45\% = 4.26\%4.20%+(2÷15)×0.45%=4.26%. Harbour Street’s 2035 bond yields 6.10%, a spread of 6.10%−4.26%=1.846.10\% - 4.26\% = 1.846.10%−4.26%=1.84 points, or 184 basis points. Morrow & Pike’s 2031 bond has three and a half years left, so interpolate between the two-year note (4.10%) and the five-year note (4.20%): 4.10%+(1.5÷3)×0.10%=4.15%4.10\% + (1.5 \div 3) \times 0.10\% = 4.15\%4.10%+(1.5÷3)×0.10%=4.15%. The bond yields 5.45%, a spread of 1.30 points, or 130 basis points.

Both companies are rated BBB, yet Harbour Street’s spread is wider. Part of the gap is maturity (lenders ask more to lend for longer to a risky borrower) and part is leverage: Harbour Street’s debt is 0.55 times its market capitalisation, against 0.15 for Morrow & Pike. A spread that widens after bad news is a price fall for the bondholders: if Harbour Street’s spread widened by one point, its bond would fall from $96.62 to $91.29, 5.52%. The yield to maturity is the promised return; F05 noted that the expected return on a risky bond is lower, because some payments may not arrive.

2022 in the bond fund

The Harbourline Aggregate Bond Index Fund holds investment-grade government and corporate bonds with an average duration of about 6 years. In the simulated history it returned −13.00% in 2022, after nine years without a loss. From its peak at the end of December 2021 it fell 13.4% to the end of October 2022, then rose 9.7% in 2023 and regained its peak by the end of March 2025.

Duration explains a fall of that size. With a duration of about 6, every one-point rise in yields costs the fund about 6% of its value, so a 13% loss is what a rise of roughly 13÷6≈2.213 \div 6 \approx 2.213÷6≈2.2 percentage points would produce, and a little more once the year’s coupon income is counted. The generator that built the data planted the 2022 loss to show exactly this; it does not model yields, so read the 2.2 points as what duration implies, not as a figure in the data. No issuer needs to default for a bond fund to lose money: rising rates alone are enough.

Note: Higher yields are bad news now and good news later

A rise in yields lowers today’s prices but raises the income on every coupon reinvested and every new bond bought. For an investor who holds an individual bond to maturity the price fall is temporary: the pull to par brings it back to $100. For a fund, which keeps replacing maturing bonds, the higher yields flow into future returns. Roughly, an investor whose horizon is longer than her bonds’ duration ends up better off after a rise in yields, and one whose horizon is shorter ends up worse off. Ana’s horizon of about 30 years is far longer than the fund’s duration of about 6 years; the measuring-risk lesson in module 2 looks at the fund’s ups and downs alongside the market’s.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a bond’s price rises when interest rates rise

    The payments are fixed, so a higher required return means a lower present value. Price and yield always move in opposite directions. A bond fund described as “paying more when rates rise” pays more on new bonds, after its existing bonds have fallen in price.

  • Using the coupon rate or the current yield as the return

    The coupon rate is fixed at issue, and the current yield ignores the move to face value. Only the yield to maturity measures what a buyer earns by holding to maturity: 4.20% for the note, not 4.00% or 4.04%.

  • Forgetting to halve the yield and double the periods

    With coupons twice a year, discount at the yield per half-year (4.20% ÷ 2 = 2.10%) for twice as many periods (10, not 5), and use half the annual coupon ($2.00). Discounting $4.00 a year for five years at 4.20% gives a slightly different, wrong, price for a semi-annual bond.

  • Treating duration as the time to maturity

    Macaulay duration is the average time of all the payments, weighted by present value, so a coupon bond’s duration is shorter than its maturity: 4.58 years for the five-year note, 13.32 years for the 20-year bond. Only a zero-coupon bond’s duration equals its maturity.

  • Using duration for large moves without convexity

    Duration is a straight-line estimate. For a one-point move in a 20-year bond it is wrong by about a point; for a three-point move by much more. Add the convexity term, or reprice the bond at the new yield.

  • Assuming a high-quality bond fund cannot lose money

    Credit quality protects against default, not against rising rates. The simulated bond fund, holding only investment-grade bonds, fell 13% in 2022, a loss the data were built to match a rise in yields of about 2 points at a duration of 6.

In practice

Ana reading a bond fund’s fact sheet. FINRA advises investors to look up a bond fund’s duration on its fact sheet. A pension fund matching its promises. A fictional pension fund owes its members payments with a duration of about 14 years. If it held bonds with a duration of four years, a fall in interest rates would raise the present value of its promises far more than the value of its bonds. Summary

  • A bond’s price is the present value of its coupons (an annuity) and its face value, discounted at its yield to maturity; price and yield move in opposite directions.
  • Coupon rate, current yield and yield to maturity differ: for a discount bond coupon < current yield < YTM; for a premium bond the order reverses.
  • With yields unchanged a bond’s price is pulled to par as it ages, and it earns its yield each period.
  • Macaulay duration is the present-value-weighted average time of the payments; modified duration = Macaulay ÷ (1 + y/m) gives ΔP/P ≈ −D_mod × Δy.
  • Convexity makes bonds fall less and rise more than duration predicts; add ½ × C_x × (Δy)² for large moves or long bonds.
  • Credit risk adds a spread over government yields: Harbour Street’s 2035 bond is 184 basis points over an interpolated 4.26%.
  • A bond fund with a duration of about 6 years loses about 6% for each one-point rise in yields: the simulated 13% fall in 2022 needed no defaults.

A bond that trades below its face value because its coupon rate is below its yield to maturity.
Premium bond
A bond that trades above its face value because its coupon rate is above its yield to maturity.
Pull to par
The movement of a bond’s price towards its face value as it approaches maturity, which happens whether the bond trades at a premium or a discount.
Zero-coupon bond
A bond that pays no coupons, only its face value at maturity, and so is sold at a discount to face value.
Interest-rate risk
The risk that the price of a bond or other fixed-income investment falls because market interest rates rise.
Duration
A measure of a bond’s sensitivity to interest rates and of the average time until its payments are received, in years. See Macaulay duration and modified duration.
Macaulay duration
The average time until a bond’s payments are received, each payment’s time weighted by its share of the bond’s present value, in years.
Modified duration
Macaulay duration divided by one plus the yield per period: the approximate percentage fall in a bond’s price for a one-percentage-point rise in its yield.
Convexity
The curvature of the relationship between a bond’s price and its yield, which makes the price fall less and rise more than duration alone predicts.
Credit risk
The risk that a borrower pays late, pays less than promised or does not pay at all.
Credit rating
A grade given by a rating agency to a borrower or a bond to indicate its credit quality, its ability and willingness to make the promised payments.
Investment grade
A credit rating of BBB (S&P, Fitch) or Baa (Moody’s) or higher; bonds rated below it are called non-investment grade or high yield.
Credit spread
The difference between the yield on a bond with credit risk and the yield on a government bond of the same maturity, usually quoted in basis points.
Basis point
One hundredth of a percentage point (0.01%), used to quote yields, spreads and fees precisely.
