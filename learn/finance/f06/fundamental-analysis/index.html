Fundamental analysis

Read the business behind a share price: top-down and bottom-up analysis, the ratios that matter for value, quality of earnings, returns on capital against the cost of capital, and a margin of safety. Then weigh whether any of it can beat the market, what the efficient markets hypothesis and its anomalies say, and which of your own biases to watch.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Distinguish top-down from bottom-up analysis and describe the steps of each.
  • Analyse a company’s profitability, leverage, cash conversion and return on invested capital, and explain why return on capital against the cost of capital decides whether growth adds value.
  • Combine DCF, multiples and a no-growth value into a range, and read what a market price implies.
  • Apply a margin of safety to a value estimate.
  • Explain the weak, semi-strong and strong forms of the efficient markets hypothesis, the main anomalies, and what they imply for an individual investor.
  • Recognise familiarity bias and overconfidence in investment decisions.

Review first

  • Return on assets and equity — Return on equity and return on invested capital are defined there.
  • Cash flow and earnings quality — Quality of earnings, accruals and free cash flow.
  • Discounted cash flow valuation — The DCF, PVGO and the consistency of growth and reinvestment.
  • Relative valuation — Multiples, justified multiples and Harbour Street’s comparison with Morrow & Pike.
  • Funds and indexing — The evidence on active funds and the Northgate fund against its index.

The idea

Fictional case

Harbour Street Foods trades at 12.7 times its earnings, Morrow & Pike at 18.0. An article Ana reads calls Harbour Street “the value pick in coffee”. The relative valuation lesson suggested that a low multiple may simply be a fair price for a riskier, less profitable company. This lesson reads Harbour Street the way an analyst would, from its accounts to a view on its price, and then asks a harder question: whether analysis like this can expect to beat the market at all. Harbour Street and every figure in its accounts are fictional.

Fundamental analysis estimates what a share is worth from the business behind it: its markets, competitive position, management, accounts and prospects. Its output is an estimate of intrinsic value to compare with the price. It is the opposite of technical analysis, which looks for patterns in past prices and trading volume without reference to the business.

An analyst can work in either direction. Top-down analysis starts with the economy (growth, interest rates, inflation), moves to the industries that should do well or badly in it, and ends with the best companies in those industries. Bottom-up analysis starts with the company, often one the analyst knows well or found by a screen, and works outwards to check that the industry and economy do not undo the case. Most professionals use both: a top-down view sets the context, and bottom-up work decides what to buy.

The work has three parts. First, understand how the business makes money and how durable that is. Second, measure it: profitability, returns on capital, debt and the quality of its earnings. Third, turn that into a value, by DCF and by multiples, and compare it with the price, leaving a margin of safety for being wrong. Harbour Street takes us through all three.

Key idea: Growth is worth paying for only if it earns more than it costs

A company creates value when it earns a return on the capital it invests above the return its investors require. If its return on capital is below its cost of capital, every dollar it reinvests to grow is worth less than a dollar: growth destroys value. The single most useful number in fundamental analysis is the gap between the two.

In depth

Reading the company: Harbour Street against Morrow & Pike

Harbour Street makes packaged food (coffee, snacks and frozen meals) and sells it through grocers. Its scale is large, its brands are established, and its markets grow slowly. Harbour Street’s margins are thinner, as a maker of packaged food selling to powerful grocers might expect, and it carries far more debt: net debt of 2.75 years of EBITDA and interest covered 4.5 times, against Morrow & Pike’s 1.17 and 10.2. That debt makes its equity riskier, which is why its beta is 1.25.

Quality of earnings

Profit is worth what it turns into. F03’s tests of earnings quality ask whether reported profit arrives as cash, whether it will recur and how much it depends on estimates. Two checks are available here. Harbour Street’s free cash flow to the firm is 93% of its EBIT after tax: its operating profit turns into cash. And its capital spending is about equal to its depreciation (1.04 times), so depreciation is a fair charge for keeping the assets working and profit is not flattered by under-investment. Morrow & Pike converts less (83%) because it spends more than its depreciation to grow, which is investment, not a warning sign. A full review would also read the cash flow statement for rising receivables or inventories, one-off gains and changes in accounting estimates (F03’s accruals ratio).

The methods disagree by more than a factor of two, and the disagreement is informative. The two high values both assume that Harbour Street grows profitably without paying for it. The DCF grows free cash flow 5% a year while reinvesting only 6.9% of its operating profit; at a 7.8% return, 5% growth would need 64% of it. Morrow & Pike’s multiple belongs to a business earning 10.5% on capital. The low values take today’s returns as permanent.

The price, $18.00, sits between them, and it can be read backwards. Holding 3% growth and the 8.77% WACC, Harbour Street’s $3,288 million enterprise value is what the value-driver formula gives if new investment earns 19.6%, two and a half times its current return. So the market is not calling Harbour Street a bargain or a failure: it is betting on a turnaround in returns. An analyst’s view should say whether that is likely: a new management plan, a profitable brand, or costs that will fall, and evidence for each.

A margin of safety

Every value estimate is uncertain, so a disciplined investor buys only when the price is well below the estimate: the difference is the margin of safety, a cushion for being wrong. With a 25% margin on the $23.46 DCF value she would buy only below 0.75×23.46=$17.600.75 \times 23.46 = \$17.600.75×23.46=$17.60, just under the price, and that DCF is the most generous estimate in the table. On the other methods there is no margin at all. The analysis does not say Harbour Street is a bad company; it says the price already asks for improvement.

Can analysis beat the market? The efficient markets hypothesis

If thousands of analysts do this work on every listed company, prices should already reflect what they find. The efficient markets hypothesis (EMH) states how much prices reflect, in three forms. As Jones and Netter summarise them: in a weak-form efficient market “future returns cannot be predicted from past returns or any other market-based indicator”; in a semi-strong efficient market “prices reflect all publicly available information about economic fundamentals”; and in the strong form, which they describe as mainly a limiting case, “prices reflect all public and private information”.

The forms say which analysis can pay. Weak-form efficiency rules out profits from technical analysis; semi-strong efficiency rules out profits from fundamental analysis of public information too. That does not make fundamental analysis pointless: someone has to do it for prices to be efficient, and an investor still needs it to know what she owns, to avoid overpaying in a mania and to judge a fund. It means an individual should not expect to earn more than the market from it, after costs, without an edge others lack.

The simulated data in this course were built with no month-to-month memory: each month’s market return was drawn independently, apart from the planted 2020 and 2022 episodes. As a check, the correlation between one month’s return and the next over the 180 months is 0.09, with a t-statistic of 1.22 on 177 degrees of freedom, not significantly different from zero. On real data, tests of this kind are the weak-form evidence; do not read anything about real markets into this simulated one, and check any claim about real history against published series such as Damodaran’s annually updated historical returns on US stocks, bonds and bills.

The evidence on real markets is mixed. Researchers have found market anomalies: patterns that a simple risk model does not explain. Jones and Netter describe “the tendency of small-capitalization stocks to earn excessive returns, especially in January”, which many economists now treat as a premium for illiquidity, and momentum: shares with high or low returns over three to twelve months tended to continue in the same direction for the following three to twelve months (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993, as they report). They also note Fama’s observation that anomalies often disappear with a different period or method. Whether an anomaly is a mispricing or a reward for a risk the model misses is usually disputed. The funds lesson shows the practical test: most professionally managed funds do not beat their index after fees, which is what a nearly efficient market predicts.

Your own biases

Behavioural finance studies the systematic ways real investors depart from the rational investor of the theory. Two matter most for someone in Ana’s position. The SEC’s Office of Investor Education and Advocacy lists familiarity bias among the patterns that can hurt investors: the tendency to “favor investments from the investor’s own country, region, state or company”. People who work in an industry feel they understand its shares, which makes them overweight it. But their income already depends on that industry, so its shares add to a risk they already carry, and knowing a business as a customer or competitor is not the same as knowing whether its price is right.

The second is overconfidence: believing one’s information and judgement are better than they are. Barber and Odean studied 66,465 US households’ brokerage accounts from 1991 to 1996 and found that “those that trade most earn an annual return of 11.4 percent, while the market returns 17.9 percent”; they conclude that “Overconfidence can explain high trading levels and the resulting poor performance of individual investors.” The defence is procedural: write down why you are buying, what value you estimate and what would prove you wrong before you act, and compare the result with an index fund afterwards.

Common mistakes

  • Calling a low multiple a bargain

    Harbour Street’s P/E is lower than Morrow & Pike’s because it earns less on its capital, carries more debt and grows more slowly. A low multiple is a bargain only if the business is better than the multiple implies; find out why it is low first.

  • Valuing growth without asking what it earns

    Growth adds value only when new investment earns more than the cost of capital. Harbour Street growing 3% at its current 7.8% return is worth less than Harbour Street not growing ($10.56 against $11.27).

  • Averaging methods that disagree

    When a DCF says $23 and a no-growth value says $11, the average ($17) answers no question. Find the assumption that separates them (here, the return on new investment) and decide what you believe about it.

  • Reading efficiency as “prices are always right”

    The efficient markets hypothesis says it is hard to profit from information others have, not that prices equal intrinsic value at every moment. Prices can be wrong; the hypothesis says you are unlikely to know in which direction more reliably than everyone else.

  • Mistaking familiarity for knowledge

    Knowing a company’s shops, products or people says little about whether its share price is right: the market knows those things too. And owning shares in the industry you work in adds to a risk your income already carries.

In practice

A pension fund’s analyst. An equity analyst covering consumer companies writes an initiation note on each new company: the business and its industry, five years of ratios against peers, quality-of-earnings checks, a DCF with sensitivities, a multiples comparison and a recommendation that states the key assumption and what would change it. Her fund’s portfolio managers care less about her target price than about that assumption.

A company’s own board. Boards use the same tools from the inside. A food company whose return on capital is below its WACC has a reason to stop expanding into new categories until returns improve, and to explain to shareholders how it will get there. Analysts outside the company will run the value-driver arithmetic whether management does or not.

Ana’s own rules. Ana does not plan to pick shares for her retirement account; the funds lesson and the evidence on active management point her towards index funds. What fundamental analysis gives her is a way to test a story, including one she tells herself about a company she knows: how it earns money, whether its returns beat its cost of capital, what its price assumes, and how much room for error she has. She keeps a written note for any share she considers, with the value she estimates, the margin she requires and what would prove her wrong.

Summary

  • Fundamental analysis estimates intrinsic value from the business; technical analysis looks only at prices and volume.
  • Top-down: economy, industry, company. Bottom-up: company first, then check the context. Professionals use both.
  • Read profitability, leverage, cash conversion and earnings quality, and above all ROIC against the WACC: Harbour Street 7.8% against 8.77%; Morrow & Pike 10.5% against 8.49%.
  • Value-driver: EV = NOPAT₁ (1 − g ÷ RONIC) ÷ (WACC − g). Growth adds value only when RONIC exceeds the WACC.
  • Combine methods by finding the assumption that separates them. Harbour Street’s $18.00 price implies new investment earning about 19.6%.
  • A margin of safety (buy only well below estimated value) protects against error in the estimate.
  • EMH: weak (past prices), semi-strong (all public information), strong (all information). Anomalies exist but are disputed and often fade.
  • Watch familiarity bias and overconfidence: write down the case and what would prove it wrong before buying.

Terms introduced

Fundamental analysis
Estimating a security’s intrinsic value from the business behind it: its industry, competitive position, management, financial statements and prospects.
Technical analysis
Trying to predict price movements from patterns in past prices and trading volume, without reference to the business.
Top-down analysis
Investment analysis that starts with the economy, moves to the industries likely to do well in it and ends with companies within them.
Bottom-up analysis
Investment analysis that starts with individual companies and then checks that their industry and the economy support the case.
Margin of safety
The gap between an asset’s estimated intrinsic value and the price paid for it, required as a cushion against errors in the estimate.
Efficient markets hypothesis
The proposition that market prices fully reflect available information, in weak (past market data), semi-strong (all public information) or strong (all public and private information) form. Abbreviated EMH.
Market anomaly
A pattern in security returns that a standard model of risk and return does not explain, such as the small-firm or momentum effects.
Familiarity bias
The tendency of investors to favour investments they know, such as those of their own country, region, employer or industry, at the expense of diversification.
Overconfidence
Overestimating the accuracy of one’s own information and judgement, which in investing leads to excessive trading and under-diversification.
