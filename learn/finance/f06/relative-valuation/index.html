Relative valuation

Value a share by the price the market puts on similar companies: P/E, EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT, price-to-book, PEG and dividend yield, why equity and enterprise multiples must never be mixed, how to choose peers, and why two companies in the same trade deserve different multiples.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate P/E, EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT, price-to-book, PEG and dividend yield, and say which are equity multiples and which enterprise multiples.
  • Estimate a company’s value by applying peer multiples, bridging an enterprise value to equity, without mixing the two kinds.
  • Choose a peer group and explain the judgements it involves.
  • Explain why multiples differ between companies, using the Gordon-implied P/E and price-to-book with growth, risk and payout.
  • Evaluate a relative valuation for common pitfalls: negative or one-off earnings, mismatched peers and a market that is itself mispriced.

Review first

  • Value and valuation words — Market capitalisation, enterprise value, EBITDA and the main multiples were defined there.
  • Shares and markets — Morrow & Pike’s and Harbour Street’s market capitalisation and enterprise value.
  • Discounted cash flow valuation — The Gordon model, growth from retention and ROE, and the equity bridge, which explain what a multiple assumes.
  • Benchmarking and annual reports — Choosing a peer group and comparing like with like.

The idea

Fictional case

The DCF in the previous lesson took a five-year forecast, a WACC and a guess about growth for ever to value Morrow & Pike Roasters at $30.14 a share. Ana’s accountant, who has bought and sold a few small businesses, says practitioners rarely start there. “What are similar companies selling for? Start with that.” This lesson does what he suggests, with the two fictional listed companies the course uses, Morrow & Pike and Harbour Street Foods, and then uses them to price a private café chain.

A valuation multiple divides a measure of value by a measure of what the company earns or owns: price by earnings per share, enterprise value by EBITDA, market capitalisation by book equity. If similar companies trade at a similar multiple, the multiple of one can price another. Estate agents do the same with price per square metre; nobody would value a flat by forecasting its rents for ever when three similar flats sold last month.

This is relative valuation: it says what a company is worth compared with others, not what it is worth absolutely. If the whole sector is overpriced, a company priced in line with its peers is overpriced too. A DCF asks “what is the cash worth?”; a multiple asks “what is the market paying for similar cash?”. Each checks the other, and the next lesson combines them.

Key idea: A multiple is a compressed DCF

A multiple hides the same assumptions a DCF states: growth, risk and how much the company must reinvest. Paying 18 times earnings is a bet on growth and risk just as surely as a terminal growth rate is. The advantage of a multiple is that the market has already made the bet for similar companies; the danger is forgetting that it is a bet.

In depth

Equity multiples and enterprise multiples

Every multiple has a numerator (a value) and a denominator (a flow or a stock). The rule that prevents most errors is that both must belong to the same claimants. An equity multiple puts the shareholders’ value (the share price, or market capitalisation) over something that belongs to shareholders: earnings after interest, book equity, dividends. An enterprise value multiple puts the value of the whole business (enterprise value) over something that belongs to lenders and shareholders together: revenue, EBITDA, EBIT.

P/E ratio
Price ÷ earnings per share. Morrow & Pike: 28.00 ÷ 1.556 = 18.0. Equity multiple; earnings are after interest, so it depends on the firm’s debt. The trailing P/E uses the last year’s earnings; the forward P/E uses next year’s forecast: Morrow & Pike’s is 28.00 ÷ (1.556 × 1.08) = 16.7.
EV/EBITDA
Enterprise value ÷ EBITDA. Morrow & Pike: 1,258 ÷ 118 = 10.7. Enterprise multiple; unaffected by how the firm is financed or by its depreciation policy, which is why acquirers like it. It ignores the capital spending a business needs, so a company that must reinvest heavily looks cheaper than it is.
EV/EBIT
Enterprise value ÷ EBIT. Morrow & Pike: 1,258 ÷ 92 = 13.7. Enterprise multiple that charges depreciation as a rough proxy for the cost of replacing assets.
Price-to-book
Market capitalisation ÷ book equity (the market-to-book ratio). Morrow & Pike: 1,120 ÷ 520 = 2.15. Equity multiple; most useful where assets are marked close to market value (banks, insurers) and least where value lies in brands and people.
PEG ratio
P/E ÷ expected earnings growth in percent. Morrow & Pike: 18.0 ÷ 8 = 2.25. A rough way to compare P/Es of companies growing at different rates; it ignores risk and how long the growth lasts.
Dividend yield
Dividend per share ÷ price. Morrow & Pike: 0.70 ÷ 28.00 = 2.50%. Equity measure, the inverse of a price-to-dividend multiple.

Which multiple to use depends on the business. EV/EBITDA suits companies with similar capital needs and different debt, which is why it dominates takeovers. EV/EBIT is better when capital intensity differs, because depreciation stands in for the cost of keeping the assets working: Morrow & Pike spends 4.5% of its revenue on equipment, Harbour Street 3.2%. P/E is the most quoted and the easiest to explain, and suits companies with similar debt and steady profits. Price-to-book suits businesses whose assets are financial. EV/revenue (Morrow & Pike 1.66, Harbour Street 0.84) is a last resort for companies with no profit yet, since it says nothing about margins: Morrow & Pike’s higher EV/revenue mostly reflects its higher EBIT margin, 12.1% against 7.4%.

Mixing the two kinds gives nonsense, and it is easy to do by accident. Apply a P/E to EBITDA and you price the lenders’ share of the cash as if it belonged to the shareholders, and before depreciation too. Apply EV/EBITDA and stop there, and you have the value of the whole business, not of the shares: subtract debt and add cash first. The lesson’s worked example shows both slips on a private café chain.

Why Morrow & Pike trades at more than Harbour Street

Morrow & Pike sells at 18.0 times earnings and Harbour Street at 12.7. Is Harbour Street cheap? Not necessarily: a multiple should be higher for a company that grows faster, is less risky or needs to reinvest less of its earnings to grow. The Gordon model makes this exact. So the gap in multiples is partly justified (Harbour Street is riskier, grows more slowly and earns less on its equity) and partly not: the market prices Harbour Street well above what its current return on equity supports. Either investors expect its returns to improve, or the shares are expensive, not cheap. A low multiple is a question, not an answer. The fundamental analysis lesson takes up Harbour Street’s case.

Note: Multiples in the real market differ by industry

Damodaran’s table of US enterprise value multiples for January 2026 puts EV/EBITDA for food processing firms with positive EBITDA at 10.01 and for restaurant and dining firms at 17.49. Industries differ in growth, risk and how much capital they need, and their multiples differ with them. That is why peers should come from the same business, and why a multiple borrowed from another industry means little.

Choosing peers

The peer group decides the answer more than the arithmetic does. Good peers share the target’s business model, growth, risk, size and accounting basis. In practice no peer matches on everything, and the analyst trades closeness against number: two close peers may be better than ten loose ones, but a single peer’s multiple carries all its own quirks. Morrow & Pike (a roaster) and Harbour Street (a packaged-food maker) are both in coffee but neither runs cafés. Brightcup Coffee, the closest listed café chain, is the course project’s subject and is left out here.

Having chosen peers, use the median rather than the mean when there are several (one outlier can move a mean a long way), look at the range as well as the middle, and adjust for known differences rather than applying the middle blindly. Use the same year for every company, trailing or forward, and the same definition of earnings: if one company’s profit includes a one-off gain, strip it out before comparing.

Pitfalls

  • Negative or tiny earnings. A P/E is meaningless when earnings are negative and enormous when they are close to zero. Use an enterprise multiple of revenue or EBITDA instead, or a forward multiple.
  • Cycles. At the top of a cycle earnings are high and P/Es look low; at the bottom the reverse. Normalised (average-cycle) earnings help.
  • Accounting differences. Different depreciation lives, one-off items and how leases or acquisitions are treated move earnings and EBITDA; compare on the same basis.
  • The whole market can be wrong. Relative valuation inherits whatever the market believes about the sector. In a sector-wide boom every company looks fairly priced against its peers.
  • Control and liquidity. Listed multiples price small, easily sold stakes. A buyer of a whole private company gets control but cannot sell easily; how far these offset each other is a judgement, and practitioners argue about it.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing equity and enterprise multiples

    A P/E applied to EBITDA, or EV/EBITDA applied to net income, prices the wrong claimants’ cash. Match the numerator and denominator: equity value with earnings after interest, enterprise value with operating figures before interest.

  • Forgetting the bridge

    An EV multiple times EBITDA is an enterprise value. Subtract debt and add cash before dividing by the shares or comparing with a market capitalisation.

  • Calling a low multiple cheap

    Harbour Street’s 12.7 times earnings is below Morrow & Pike’s 18.0, but on its risk and return on equity the Gordon model justifies only about 8.7. Ask why a multiple is low before calling it a bargain.

  • Peers chosen for convenience

    A peer group drawn from the same broad sector but different business models (a roaster and a food maker to price a café chain) can be the best available but still needs adjusting. Say why each peer is comparable and how it differs.

  • Averaging with outliers or mismatched years

    One peer on a P/E of 60 after a bad year drags a mean far from the typical company. Use the median, the same year for every company, and earnings without one-off items.

In practice

Investment bankers’ comparables. When a company floats on a stock exchange, its advisers propose a price range largely from the multiples of listed peers, adjusted for differences in growth and size. The DCF in the prospectus work is a cross-check, and investors who buy in the offering test the range against the same peers.

Buying a small business. Brokers who sell cafés, bakeries and practices usually quote a multiple of profit: of EBITDA for larger ones, of the owner’s earnings for small ones. Ana’s accountant uses one when he values a business for a client, then asks what the multiple assumes about growth and how much the owner’s own work is part of the profit, since a buyer will have to pay someone to do it.

A fund analyst’s screen. An analyst at an equity fund sorts her sector by EV/EBITDA and P/E each month, not to buy the cheapest but to choose which companies deserve a closer look: a low multiple with a good return on capital is interesting; a low multiple with a poor return is usually a fair price. The screen starts the work; the fundamental analysis does it.

Summary

  • A multiple prices one company by what the market pays for similar ones; it is relative, so a mispriced sector misprices every member.
  • Equity multiples (P/E, price-to-book, dividend yield) use equity values and shareholders’ figures; enterprise multiples (EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT) use enterprise value and operating figures. Never mix them.
  • An EV multiple gives enterprise value: subtract debt and add cash to reach the equity.
  • Morrow & Pike: P/E 18.0, EV/EBITDA 10.7, price-to-book 2.15. Harbour Street: 12.7, 8.0 and 1.31.
  • Justified P/E = payout × (1 + g) ÷ (r_E − g); justified price-to-book = ROE × justified P/E. Higher growth, lower risk and higher ROE justify higher multiples.
  • Harbour Street’s lower multiple is more than explained by its risk and its ROE below the cost of equity: low is not cheap.
  • Choose peers on business, growth, risk and size; use medians and ranges; adjust for one-offs, cycles and accounting.

Terms introduced

Equity multiple
A valuation multiple whose numerator is the value of the shares (price or market capitalisation) and whose denominator belongs to shareholders, such as net income or book equity: P/E and price-to-book are examples.
Enterprise value multiple
A valuation multiple whose numerator is enterprise value and whose denominator belongs to lenders and shareholders together, such as revenue, EBITDA or EBIT: EV/EBITDA is the most common.
Trailing P/E
Share price divided by earnings per share for the last twelve months or the last financial year.
Forward P/E
Share price divided by forecast earnings per share for the next twelve months or the next financial year.
PEG ratio
The P/E ratio divided by the expected annual growth rate of earnings per share in percent: a rough adjustment of the P/E for growth.
Justified multiple
The multiple a valuation model supports for a company’s growth, risk and payout, such as the P/E implied by the Gordon growth model, used to judge whether a market multiple is high or low.
