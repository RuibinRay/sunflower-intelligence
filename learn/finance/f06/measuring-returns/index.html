Measuring returns

Turn prices and payouts into returns you can compare: holding-period returns, arithmetic and geometric averages, annualised and real returns, expected returns from scenarios and the premium over the risk-free rate, using fifteen years of simulated fund returns and Ana’s retirement plan.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate a holding-period return from prices and income, split it into its price and income parts, and annualise it.
  • Calculate the arithmetic and geometric mean of a series of returns and explain why the geometric mean is never larger.
  • Choose the right average for a question: the expected return for one period, or the compound growth of money over many.
  • Convert nominal returns into real returns with the Fisher relation and measure a return in excess of the risk-free rate.
  • Calculate an expected return from scenarios and contrast it with an average of past returns.

Review first

  • Simple and compound interest — Compounding and the compound annual growth rate, which the geometric mean generalises.
  • Inflation and real returns — The exact Fisher relation between nominal returns, real returns and inflation.
  • What finance is: money, time and risk — Expected value, the risk-free rate and the risk premium were introduced there in words.
  • Shares and markets — What a share, a dividend and an index fund are.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

It is June 2028. On 1 July Ana opens a retirement account with $5,000, a gift from her parents, and will add $400 at the end of every month until she retires in about 30 years. Her flat-deposit savings stay where F04 put them, $914.19 a month in her Sandpiper Online savings account for April 2032, and her $6,000 emergency fund stays in cash. Before she chooses what the retirement account holds, she wants to know how to measure what investments have earned, so that she compares like with like.

A return measures what an investment earned relative to what it cost. Owning a share or a fund pays you in two ways: its price may rise or fall, and it may pay income, dividends on shares or coupons on bonds. The total return counts both. A return measured over the time you hold the investment, from the day you buy to the day you sell or value it, is its holding-period return.

One period’s return is easy. The questions start when there are many. If an investment rises 50% one year and falls 50% the next, its average yearly return is 0%, yet $100 became $150 and then $75. The plain average, the arithmetic mean, describes a typical single year; the geometric mean, the constant rate that would have produced the same ending wealth, describes what compounding actually did. Here it is −13.4% a year. Both are correct answers to different questions, and confusing them is the commonest error in the subject.

A return also needs a yardstick. Against inflation it becomes a real return: what the money gained in purchasing power. Against the risk-free rate it becomes an excess return: what investors earned for bearing risk, a historical measure of the risk premium. And for decisions about the future, the figure you want is an expected return: a probability-weighted view of what might happen, which history can inform but cannot supply.

Caution: The market history in this course is simulated

The fifteen years of monthly returns used in F06 (January 2013 to December 2027) were generated by a computer program for teaching. The funds, their index and the companies are fictional, and the sizes were chosen to look broadly like long-run US figures, so the methods you practise here are the ones you would use on real data. Never quote these numbers as facts about real markets. Real history should be checked against sources such as Aswath Damodaran’s annually updated table of historical returns on US stocks, bonds and bills.

In depth

The holding-period return

Buy at a price P0P_0P0​, receive income DDD while you hold the investment, and sell or value it at P1P_1P1​. The holding-period return is the gain divided by what you paid: HPR=(P1−P0+D)÷P0\text{HPR} = (P_1 - P_0 + D) \div P_0HPR=(P1​−P0​+D)÷P0​. It splits into a price return (or capital gain yield), (P1−P0)÷P0(P_1 - P_0) \div P_0(P1​−P0​)÷P0​, and an income return (the dividend yield for a share), D÷P0D \div P_0D÷P0​. OpenStax calls the result the realised return: “The realized return of an investment is the total return that occurs over a particular time period.”

A price index ignores income; a total return index assumes every payout is reinvested. For shares the difference compounds into a large gap over decades, so always ask which one a chart shows. Every monthly return for the funds and shares in this course is a total return, and each fund’s return is after its fees.

A holding-period return measures one sum of money over one stretch of time. Ana’s account will not be one sum: she adds $400 a month. Its return can be measured in two ways. The time-weighted return links each month’s return, as the fund tables in this lesson do; it measures the investment, regardless of when money arrived. The money-weighted return is the internal rate of return of her own deposits and the ending value (F04); it measures her experience, and is higher than the time-weighted return if she happened to add most money just before good months. Fund reports use the first, because the manager does not control the timing of deposits.

Annualising

Returns over different lengths of time can be compared only once they are put on the same basis, usually a year. A return earned over TTT years compounds to an annual rate of (1+HPR)1/T−1(1 + \text{HPR})^{1/T} - 1(1+HPR)1/T−1, the compound annual growth rate of F04. A 30% gain over two and a half years is 1.301/2.5−1=11.07%1.30^{1/2.5} - 1 = 11.07\%1.301/2.5−1=11.07% a year, not 30%÷2.5=12%30\% \div 2.5 = 12\%30%÷2.5=12%. In the same way a monthly growth rate ggg compounds to (1+g)12−1(1 + g)^{12} - 1(1+g)12−1 a year.

This course follows one convention for monthly averages: the arithmetic monthly mean is multiplied by 12, and the geometric monthly mean is compounded. Multiplying is not exact compounding, but it keeps the annual figure an arithmetic mean, which is what the risk measures of the next lesson and the portfolio lessons of module 3 are built on. Say which you have used whenever you report an annual figure.

Arithmetic and geometric means

For nnn period returns r1,…,rnr_1, \dots, r_nr1​,…,rn​, the arithmetic mean is rˉ=(r1+⋯+rn)÷n\bar r = (r_1 + \dots + r_n) \div nrˉ=(r1​+⋯+rn​)÷n and the geometric mean is g=[(1+r1)(1+r2)⋯(1+rn)]1/n−1g = [(1+r_1)(1+r_2)\cdots(1+r_n)]^{1/n} - 1g=[(1+r1​)(1+r2​)⋯(1+rn​)]1/n−1. The geometric mean is the single rate that, compounded nnn times, turns $1 into the same ending wealth as the actual returns did. Compounding works on the growth factors 1+r1 + r1+r, which is why the geometric mean multiplies them rather than adding the returns.

The geometric mean is never larger than the arithmetic mean, and the two are equal only when every return is the same. The gap grows with how much the returns vary: approximately, g≈rˉ−12σ2g \approx \bar r - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2g≈rˉ−21​σ2, where σ\sigmaσ is the standard deviation of the returns, the measure of spread that the next lesson treats in full. A loss needs a larger gain to recover from it (after −50%, +100%), so swings cost compound growth even when they average out. Investors call this cost volatility drag.

The simulated total market fund shows all of this. Its fifteen calendar-year returns, from −26.9% in 2022 to +46.5% in 2014, have an arithmetic mean of 11.19% and a geometric mean of 9.57%: $1 at the start of 2013 became $3.94 by the end of 2027, and 3.9381/15−1=9.57%3.938^{1/15} - 1 = 9.57\%3.9381/15−1=9.57%. Their standard deviation is 19.33%, so the approximation gives 11.19%−12(0.1933)2=9.32%11.19\% - \tfrac{1}{2}(0.1933)^2 = 9.32\%11.19%−21​(0.1933)2=9.32%, close to the exact 9.57%.

Monthly data tell the same story at a finer grain. The fund’s mean monthly return is 0.869% and its geometric mean monthly return 0.764%. Compounding the geometric monthly mean gives 9.57% a year again: compound growth does not depend on how finely you slice the period. The arithmetic mean does. Twelve times the monthly mean is 10.43%, not the 11.19% of the calendar years, because a calendar-year return already includes compounding within the year. This is why an arithmetic mean should always be quoted with its period and how it was annualised.

Key idea: Which mean answers which question

To estimate the return of one future period drawn from the same history, the arithmetic mean is the better guide. To describe how money actually grew, or to plan how a sum may grow over many years, use the geometric mean. Over a long horizon a typical outcome grows at about the geometric rate; compounding the arithmetic mean instead gives the average of all the possible ending values, which a few very good outcomes pull upwards.

Growth of $1

A wealth index follows $1 invested at the start through every period’s return, with all income reinvested: it is the running product of the growth factors. It shows the path as well as the destination: the total market fund’s dollar was worth $3.07 at the end of 2021, $2.25 at the end of 2022, and did not pass its 2021 value again until 2026. The bond fund’s dollar grew to $1.88 and bills’ to $1.36. The chart in Explore draws all three.

Real returns

F04’s exact Fisher relation converts a nominal return into a real one: 1+rreal=(1+rnominal)÷(1+π)1 + r_{\text{real}} = (1 + r_{\text{nominal}}) \div (1 + \pi)1+rreal​=(1+rnominal​)÷(1+π), where π\piπ is inflation over the same period. The simulated history has no inflation series, so to see what the funds’ compound growth would mean in purchasing power, apply Ana’s planning assumption of 2.8% a year. The market fund’s 9.57% becomes 1.0957÷1.028−1=6.58%1.0957 \div 1.028 - 1 = 6.58\%1.0957÷1.028−1=6.58% real; the bond fund’s 4.31% becomes 1.47%; and bills’ 2.05% becomes −0.73%: money in bills would have lost purchasing power.

The shortcut of subtracting inflation (9.57% − 2.8% = 6.77%) is close at low rates and wrong by more as rates rise. Long-horizon plans are clearest in real terms, because a real ending value is in dollars of today’s spending power.

Expected return from scenarios

History tells you what happened. A decision needs a view of what may happen, and the simplest is a set of scenarios with probabilities. The expected return is their probability-weighted average, E(r)=∑spsrsE(r) = \sum_s p_s r_sE(r)=∑s​ps​rs​: F00’s expected value applied to a return. Suppose an analyst gives the total market fund three scenarios for next year (fictional): a strong year (+30%) with probability 0.25, a normal year (+10%) with 0.50 and a downturn (−15%) with 0.25. Then E(r)=0.25×30%+0.50×10%+0.25×(−15%)=8.75%E(r) = 0.25 \times 30\% + 0.50 \times 10\% + 0.25 \times (-15\%) = 8.75\%E(r)=0.25×30%+0.50×10%+0.25×(−15%)=8.75%.

The expected return is not a return anyone is expected to earn. No scenario pays 8.75%; it is the centre of the distribution of outcomes. The probabilities and the outcomes are judgements, so an expected return is only as good as the scenarios behind it, and history is one input to them, not a substitute.

Excess returns and the risk premium

An excess return subtracts the risk-free rate for the same period: rt−rf,tr_t - r_{f,t}rt​−rf,t​. In this course the risk-free asset is the one-month bill, whose return each month is known when the month begins. The market fund’s mean excess return over the fifteen years is 0.70% a month, 8.40% a year (the simulation set it there on purpose). The bond fund’s is 2.28% a year. Against the analyst’s scenarios, with a one-year bill paying 4.0% (fictional), the market’s expected excess return next year is 8.75%−4.0%=4.75%8.75\% - 4.0\% = 4.75\%8.75%−4.0%=4.75%.

An average excess return over a past period is a historical estimate of the risk premium. F05’s market risk premium of 5.5% is a different quantity: a forward-looking assumption measured over long-term government bonds rather than bills. A premium measured over bills is larger, because bills usually yield less than long-term bonds, so always say what a premium is measured over and over which years.

What real history looks like

For comparison with the simulation, Damodaran’s table of US returns (updated January 2026) shows that $100 invested at the start of 1928 in the S&P 500 with dividends reinvested was worth $1,157,598.95 at the end of 2025; in three-month Treasury bills, $2,578.30; and in ten-year Treasury bonds, $7,752.88. Over those 98 years that is compound growth of about 10.0%, 3.4% and 4.5% a year (our arithmetic from his figures), before inflation. One country’s century, chosen because its data are good, is still a single history: it is evidence about the future, not a promise.

Common mistakes

  • Averaging returns to describe growth

    A fund that gains 50% and then loses 50% has an arithmetic mean of 0% but has lost a quarter of the money. To describe or project compound growth use the geometric mean, or follow $1 through the returns.

  • Leaving out the income

    A price return ignores dividends and coupons. Comparing a share’s price change with a fund’s total return, or reading a price index as if it were what investors earned, understates shares’ returns. Check that both figures are total returns.

  • Annualising by division

    A 30% gain over 2.5 years is 11.07% a year, not 12%: compound with (1+HPR)1/T−1(1 + \text{HPR})^{1/T} - 1(1+HPR)1/T−1. Multiplying a monthly arithmetic mean by 12 is a stated convention; multiplying a monthly growth rate by 12 is an error.

  • Subtracting inflation

    A real return is (1+r)÷(1+π)−1(1 + r) \div (1 + \pi) - 1(1+r)÷(1+π)−1. Subtracting is a shortcut that overstates the real return, by more as rates rise. Never mix a nominal return with a real one in the same comparison.

  • Treating a past average as the expected return

    Fifteen years of history, real or simulated, is one draw of what could have happened. A past average is evidence for an expected return, not the expected return itself, and it depends heavily on the start and end dates. Say which period an average covers.

  • Judging an account by a fund’s published return

    A fund’s time-weighted return measures the fund. An investor who added money before falls and withdrew before rises earned a different, money-weighted return. Compare like with like.

In practice

Ana’s planning note. Ana’s projection for her accountant states each assumption in a line: the deposits, the return as a geometric mean, the inflation rate, and the result in today’s dollars. She shows two rates, not one, because no single history pins the return down; the next lesson adds the spread of outcomes around them. She keeps the deposit savings and the emergency fund out of the projection: money she needs within four years is a different problem, and the next lesson shows with the same data why it does not belong in shares.

Reading a fund report. A fund report usually shows cumulative returns and annualised returns over one, three, five and ten years. Read the annualised figures as geometric means, check that they are total returns after fees, compare them with the fund’s benchmark over the same dates, and notice the start date: a period that begins just after a crash flatters every average. The funds-and-indexing lesson in module 3 adds what fees cost and how to judge an active fund.

A pension trustee’s assumptions. Trustees who must decide what a scheme can promise its members work in real, compound terms: they ask what the assets may earn above inflation each year over decades, and treat higher arithmetic figures as an upper bound. They also separate the return they assume from the return they hope for, and test the plan against lower figures, the subject of the next lesson.

Summary

  • A holding-period return is (P₁ − P₀ + D) ÷ P₀: a price return plus an income return. Use total returns, with income reinvested.
  • Annualise by compounding: (1 + HPR)^(1/T) − 1. This course annualises a monthly arithmetic mean by × 12 and a monthly geometric mean by compounding.
  • The geometric mean is never above the arithmetic mean; the gap is about ½σ², so more volatile investments lose more to compounding.
  • Use the arithmetic mean for one period’s expected return and the geometric mean for compound growth over many periods.
  • Real return = (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1. Long plans are clearest in real terms.
  • An expected return is a probability-weighted average of scenarios: a view of the future, not an average of the past.
  • An excess return is the return minus the risk-free rate; its past average estimates the risk premium. Say what it is measured over.
  • F06’s market history is simulated: the simulated total market fund grew 9.57% a year compounded (arithmetic mean 11.19% of calendar years).

Terms introduced

Total return
The whole return on an investment over a period: the change in its price plus the income it paid (dividends, coupons or distributions), usually as a percentage of the starting price.
Holding-period return
The total return earned over the time an investment is held: (ending price − starting price + income) ÷ starting price.
Geometric mean
The nth root of the product of n positive values, equal to the back-transformed mean of their logs; never larger than the arithmetic mean.
Wealth index
The value over time of $1 invested at the start, found by compounding each period’s total return. Also called the growth of $1.
Expected return
The probability-weighted average of the returns an investment could earn over a future period: the sum of each possible return times its probability.
Excess return
The return on an investment minus the risk-free return over the same period.
