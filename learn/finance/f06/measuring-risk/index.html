Measuring risk

Put numbers on how far returns can stray: variance and standard deviation from scenarios and history, annualised volatility, what a normal distribution would predict and where real returns break it, worst years and drawdowns, and how the time until you need the money changes what risk means.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate the variance and standard deviation of returns from scenarios and from a sample of past returns, and annualise a monthly standard deviation.
  • Use the normal distribution to estimate the chance of a return below a threshold, and explain why fat tails make that estimate too low for extreme losses.
  • Measure downside risk directly with worst periods and the maximum drawdown.
  • Explain how the spread of outcomes changes with the holding period, and judge whether money needed by a given date belongs in shares.
  • Compare the risk and return of bills, bonds, share funds and single shares, and describe the risk–return trade-off and where it fails.

Review first

  • Measuring returns — Arithmetic and geometric means, annualising and the simulated fund history.
  • Describing distributions — Variance and standard deviation, and why a sample divides by n − 1 (recommended).
  • The normal distribution — z-scores and probabilities from the normal curve (recommended).
  • What finance is: money, time and risk — Risk as outcomes that may differ from what was expected.
  • Capital and liquidity — Liquidity and the emergency fund.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana now knows the simulated total market fund grew 9.57% a year compounded over fifteen years. Her accountant’s first question is the right one: “And how bad did it get?” Ana also has a second pot to think about. She is saving $914.19 a month for a $60,000 flat deposit she needs in April 2032, less than four years away, and she has wondered whether that money, too, should go into the fund that earned so much more than her savings account.

In finance, risk is the chance that an outcome differs from what you expected. For an investment it is the spread of the returns it might earn. A fund that always returns 4% has none; one that might return +30% or −30% has a great deal, even if both expect the same on average. The most used measure of that spread is the standard deviation of returns, the square root of the variance; applied to an investment’s returns and stated for a year, it is usually called volatility.

A standard deviation summarises the spread in one number, but it treats a surprise gain like a surprise loss and says nothing directly about how bad the worst outcomes are. So investors also look at the worst outcomes themselves: the worst year, and the drawdown, the fall from a previous peak. And they ask what risk means over the time they will hold the investment, their time horizon, because a fall that is recovered over thirty years can ruin a plan that needs the money in four.

In depth

Variance and standard deviation from scenarios

When the possible returns and their probabilities are set out as scenarios, the variance is the probability-weighted average squared distance from the expected return: σ2=∑sps [rs−E(r)]2\sigma^2 = \sum_s p_s\,[r_s - E(r)]^2σ2=∑s​ps​[rs​−E(r)]2, and the standard deviation σ\sigmaσ is its square root, in the same units as the returns. The previous lesson’s fictional scenarios for the market fund next year (+30%, +10% and −15% with probabilities 0.25, 0.50 and 0.25) have an expected return of 8.75%. Their variance is 0.25(21.25)2+0.50(1.25)2+0.25(−23.75)2=254.690.25(21.25)^2 + 0.50(1.25)^2 + 0.25(-23.75)^2 = 254.690.25(21.25)2+0.50(1.25)2+0.25(−23.75)2=254.69 in squared percentage points, so σ=15.96%\sigma = 15.96\%σ=15.96%.

OpenStax treats the standard deviation of historical returns in the same way, as the measure of an investment’s volatility.

The simulated market fund’s 180 monthly returns have a mean of 0.869% and a standard deviation of 4.618% a month. A month 4.6 points above or below the mean was ordinary; a month of −17% was not.

Annualising a standard deviation

If monthly returns are independent and have the same distribution, the variance of a year’s total is twelve times the monthly variance, so the standard deviation grows with the square root of time: σyear=σmonth12\sigma_{\text{year}} = \sigma_{\text{month}}\sqrt{12}σyear​=σmonth​12​. The market fund’s 4.618%×12=16.00%4.618\% \times \sqrt{12} = 16.00\%4.618%×12​=16.00%; the bond fund’s 1.19% a month is 4.14% a year. Means scale with time but standard deviations with its square root, which is why the course multiplies a monthly mean by 12 but a monthly SD by √12.

Bills’ standard deviation, 0.56% a year, needs care. A one-month bill’s return is known on the day you buy it, so a bill holder faces no risk of loss over the month. The figure measures how much the bill rate itself changed over fifteen years, from near zero to about 5%. For an investor the risk in bills is different: the rate may fall, and after inflation the return may be negative, as the previous lesson found.

What a normal distribution would imply

If yearly returns followed a normal distribution with the market fund’s mean of 10.43% and SD of 16.00%, about two years in three would fall within one SD of the mean (−5.6% to 26.4%) and about 95% within two (−21.6% to 42.4%). The chance of a loss in a year would be Φ(−10.43÷16.00)=25.7%\Phi(-10.43 \div 16.00) = 25.7\%Φ(−10.43÷16.00)=25.7%, and of a year worse than Ana’s −25% limit, Φ((−25−10.43)÷16.00)=1.3%\Phi((-25 - 10.43) \div 16.00) = 1.3\%Φ((−25−10.43)÷16.00)=1.3%, where Φ\PhiΦ is the standard normal probability below a z-score (Excel’s =NORM.DIST(-0.25,0.1043,0.16,TRUE)). The fund lost money in 5 of its 15 calendar years and had one year below −25%: 2022, at −26.9%. Fifteen yearly returns are too few to test the tails; 180 monthly returns are better.

Fat tails

A normal distribution puts 0.27% of outcomes more than three standard deviations from the mean: about 0.5 of the market fund’s 180 months. The fund had three such months: −17.0% in March 2020 and gains of 21.6% in April 2015 and 14.9% in April 2025. March 2020 was 3.87 SD below the mean; under a normal distribution a month that bad has a probability of 0.0055%, about once in 18,000 months, or 1,500 years.

Distributions with more extreme outcomes than the normal have fat tails, and their kurtosis measures it: Excel’s =KURT(range) returns the excess kurtosis, 0 for normal data and 3.09 for these monthly returns. This is no accident of the sample: the simulation drew the market’s monthly excess returns from a t distribution with 5 degrees of freedom, whose tails are fatter than the normal’s, and added the March 2020 crash on purpose, because that is what real returns look like. Fama’s classic 1965 study of the daily price changes of the thirty Dow Jones Industrial shares found that “in every case the empirical distributions are more peaked in the center and have longer tails than the normal distribution.” The practical rule follows: a standard deviation and a normal curve describe ordinary months well and understate the chance of extreme ones, so look at the extremes directly as well.

Note: Real extremes

Damodaran’s table of US returns shows the S&P 500, with dividends, returning −43.84% in 1931 and −36.55% in 2008, and in 2022 −18.04% while ten-year Treasury bonds returned −17.83%: shares and bonds fell together. The simulated history is milder than the real one’s worst years.

Worst periods and drawdowns

A drawdown is the fall in an investment’s value from its highest previous value: at each date, DDt=Wt÷max⁡s≤tWs−1DD_t = W_t \div \max_{s \le t} W_s - 1DDt​=Wt​÷maxs≤t​Ws​−1, where WWW is the wealth index. The maximum drawdown is the largest such fall over a period. It answers the question an investor feels: “How much could I have lost from the top?”

The simulated market fund’s maximum drawdown was −39.1%, from the end of September 2021 to the end of September 2022, deeper than any calendar year because it spans two. The fund then spent 57 months below its 2021 peak before regaining it at the end of July 2026. The March 2020 crash was sharper but shorter: −24.6% from the end of 2019 in three months, regained by the end of August 2021. The bond fund’s maximum drawdown was −13.4% (December 2021 to October 2022), the planted fall of 2022 as interest rates rose, an example of interest-rate risk (the bond lesson explains why rising yields lower bond prices), and it took until March 2025 to recover.

Risk and the time horizon

How risky shares are depends on how long you hold them, but in two opposite senses. Over longer periods good and bad years partly offset, so the spread of the annual average return narrows: with independent years it shrinks with the square root of the horizon, from 16% for one year to 8% over four and 2.9% over thirty. But the spread of the ending wealth, in dollars, keeps widening, because the uncertainty compounds. Both are true; which matters depends on the decision.

In this history the worst one-year window lost 39.1%, the worst five-year window still lost 6.5% a year, and no ten-year window lost money. Do not read that last line as a law: the simulated data hold only about five years of starting months for ten-year windows, and real markets have had long stretches when shares trailed bonds. What the table does show reliably is the first column: over a few years, shares can lose a large part of their value and not recover in time.

For money needed on a known date, the risk that counts is the value on that date. Investor.gov puts it plainly: “A portfolio heavily weighted in stock or stock mutual funds, for instance, would be inappropriate for a short-term goal, such as saving for a family’s summer vacation.” The same guide notes that “investing solely in cash investments may be appropriate for short-term financial goals,” and that investors willing to ride out the volatile returns of stocks over long periods have generally been rewarded. The time horizon decides which risk measure matters.

The risk–return trade-off, and where it fails

Across bills, bonds and the share funds, investors who accepted more volatility earned more on average: the trade-off that makes investing worthwhile at all. It holds for broad classes of investment because they carry risk that no one can diversify away, systematic risk, and investors must be paid to bear it. It does not hold for every single investment. Morrow & Pike’s shares had a volatility of 25.25% and earned less than the market fund; their price fell 67.9% at worst. Much of a single share’s risk is specific to the company and can be removed by holding many shares, so the market does not pay for it: the subject of the diversification lesson in module 3.

Ana’s answer to her questionnaire, that she could live with a worst year of about −25%, can now be checked against the data. The market fund’s worst calendar year, −26.9%, was just beyond it, and its worst twelve months, −39.1%, far beyond. A portfolio entirely in shares would have tested her limit; the efficient-portfolios lesson shows how adding bonds and bills sets the risk to fit it.

Common mistakes

  • Annualising a standard deviation by 12

    Means scale with time; standard deviations with its square root. A monthly SD of 4.6% is 16% a year, not 55%. Multiply by √12, and only when months are roughly independent.

  • Treating volatility as the whole of risk

    A standard deviation counts surprise gains as risk too and says nothing about how deep or how long the worst falls were. Report the worst year and the maximum drawdown as well, and judge them against when the money is needed.

  • Trusting the normal curve in the tails

    Returns have fatter tails than the normal: the simulated data had six times the 3-SD months a normal predicts, and real returns behave the same way. Use the normal for ordinary outcomes and look at the historical extremes for the bad ones.

  • “Shares are safe in the long run”, so short-term money can go in too

    The annualised spread narrows with time, but losses over three to five years were common in this history and the ending value is what a goal needs. Money for a date within a few years belongs in cash or short, high-quality bonds.

  • Assuming more risk always earns more

    Only risk that cannot be diversified away is rewarded. Single shares carry a great deal of company-specific risk that the market does not pay for, as Morrow & Pike’s lower return with 25% volatility shows.

  • Reading a bill’s standard deviation as risk

    A one-month bill’s return is known when it is bought. Its historical SD measures how the rate changed over the years, not a risk of loss.

Summary

  • Risk is the spread of possible returns. Its main measure is the standard deviation, the square root of the variance: from scenarios Σp(r − E(r))², from history Σ(r − r̄)² ÷ (n − 1).
  • Annualise a monthly SD by √12 (means by 12). The simulated market fund: 4.618% a month, 16.00% a year.
  • A normal distribution gives quick probabilities, but real returns have fatter tails: extreme months are far more common than it predicts.
  • Look at the extremes directly: worst year, worst rolling periods and the maximum drawdown (−39.1% for the simulated market fund, 57 months to recover).
  • Over longer horizons the annualised spread narrows, but losses over a few years are common and the spread of ending wealth widens.
  • Money needed on a date within a few years belongs in cash-like savings, not shares; long-horizon money can take risk it has time to recover from.
  • Across broad asset classes more volatility has come with higher average returns; for single shares much of the risk is unrewarded and can be diversified away.

Terms introduced

Standard deviation
A measure of spread: roughly the typical distance of values from their mean, calculated as the square root of the average squared distance (dividing by n − 1 for a sample).
Variance
The average squared distance of values from their mean, dividing by n − 1 for a sample (VAR.S) or by n for a whole population (VAR.P); the square of the standard deviation.
Volatility
The standard deviation of an investment’s returns, usually stated for a year: the most common measure of how much its returns vary.
Normal distribution
The symmetric, bell-shaped continuous distribution fixed by its mean μ and standard deviation σ, written N(μ, σ). About 68%, 95% and 99.7% of its values lie within one, two and three standard deviations of the mean.
Kurtosis
A measure of how heavy a distribution’s tails are. Excel’s KURT reports excess kurtosis, which is about 0 for normally distributed data and positive for heavier tails.
Fat tails
A property of a distribution whose extreme outcomes, far from the mean, are more frequent than a normal distribution with the same mean and standard deviation predicts.
Drawdown
The fall in an investment’s value from its highest previous value, as a percentage of that peak.
Maximum drawdown
The largest drawdown over a period: the biggest percentage fall from a peak to a later low before a new peak is reached.
Time horizon
How long an investor expects to hold an investment before needing the money: the expected number of months, years or decades of investing.
Risk–return trade-off
The tendency for investments with more risk that cannot be diversified away to offer higher expected returns, as payment for bearing it.
