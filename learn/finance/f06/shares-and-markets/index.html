Shares and markets

What a share is, how shares are issued and traded, how an index is built, and what market capitalisation and enterprise value measure, using two listed coffee and food companies.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Distinguish common and preferred shares by their claim on cash flows, their votes and their place in the queue if a company fails.
  • Explain how primary and secondary markets, brokers, exchanges, market and limit orders and the bid–ask spread fit together.
  • Calculate the return on a value-weighted and a price-weighted index, and explain why the two can differ sharply.
  • Calculate market capitalisation and enterprise value from a company’s share price, shares, debt and cash.
  • Explain what a share price reflects, and what can move it, in terms of expected cash flows and required returns.

Review first

  • Financial markets — Primary and secondary markets, exchanges and over-the-counter markets were introduced there.
  • Market participants — Brokers, dealers, market makers and the bid–ask spread.
  • Value and valuation words — Book value, market capitalisation and enterprise value in words, with a first example.
  • Perpetuities and growth — The Gordon growth model, used here to read what a share price implies.
  • The cost of debt and equity — The cost of preferred stock and the capital asset pricing model as a tool.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

June 2028. Lantern Coffee Co. is in its third year, and Ana Lim is opening a retirement account. From 1 July 2028 it will hold $5,000 from her parents and $400 a month from her salary, for about 30 years, until she retires. Her other money stays where it is: the $914.19 a month she saves towards a $60,000 flat deposit by April 2032 stays in her savings account, and her $6,000 emergency fund stays in cash. Module 2 shows with data why money needed within a few years is not for shares. The retirement money is different: it can be invested for decades, and the obvious candidate is the Harbourline Total Market Index Fund, which holds every listed share in the home market, weighted by market value. Before choosing, Ana wants to understand what she would own.

A share is a unit of ownership in a company. If a company has 40 million shares and you own 400 of them, you own one hundred-thousandth of it: a hundred-thousandth of its votes and of whatever it pays its owners. Owners are not lenders. A lender has a contract that fixes interest and repayment; an owner has a residual claim, a right to what is left after everyone with a prior claim has been paid. That is why shares can lose much of their value in a bad year and gain a great deal in a good one, and why shareholders expect a higher return than lenders over time.

Shares are bought and sold in markets. A company sells new shares to raise money in the primary market; investors then trade those shares among themselves in the secondary market, where the company receives nothing. Most trading happens in the secondary market, through a broker that sends an investor’s orders to an exchange or another trading venue. The price at which shares change hands is the market’s current view of what one share is worth.

Multiply that price by the number of shares and you have the market value of the whole company’s equity, its market capitalisation. Add the company’s debt and subtract its cash and you have its enterprise value, the price of the business as a whole. An index such as the Harbourline Total Market Index adds up many companies’ market values to measure how the whole market is doing, and an index fund buys the shares in the index in proportion to those values.

Key idea: Owning a share means owning what is left

A shareholder gets nothing until lenders (and any preferred shareholders) have been paid, and then gets everything that remains. The share price is the market’s estimate of the value of that remainder, now and in the future. Everything in this course, from risk to valuation, builds on that idea.

In depth

Common shares and preferred shares

Most shares are common shares (ordinary shares in the UK). Investor.gov summarises their two rights: common stock entitles owners to vote at shareholder meetings and to receive dividends. The votes elect the board of directors, which appoints management and decides whether to pay dividends and how much. Nothing obliges the board to pay any: a dividend is a decision, not a debt. Common shareholders also have limited liability: if the company fails they can lose what they paid for their shares, but creditors cannot pursue them for more.

Preferred stock sits between debt and common equity. It pays a fixed dividend, usually stated as a percentage of its par value, and Investor.gov sums up its place: “Preferred stockholders usually don’t have voting rights but they receive dividend payments before common stockholders do, and have priority over common stockholders if the company goes bankrupt and its assets are liquidated.” Because the dividend is fixed and usually has no end date, F04 valued a preferred share as a perpetuity, and F05 turned that round into its cost, rP=DP÷PPr_P = D_P \div P_PrP​=DP​÷PP​. Preferred dividends, unlike interest, are not a contractual obligation whose non-payment is a default, so preferred shares carry more risk than the same company’s bonds.

In this course “shares” means common shares unless stated otherwise. Index funds such as the Harbourline fund hold common shares; the return a shareholder earns is the change in the price plus the dividends received, a total return, which the measuring-returns lesson in module 2 calculates.

Primary and secondary markets

F00 introduced the two markets. A company raises money only in the primary market: in an initial public offering, when it first sells shares to the public, or in a later sale of new shares. Suppose Morrow & Pike sold 2 million new shares to investors. It would receive the money (less its bankers’ fees), and its shares outstanding would rise from 40 million to 42 million. When one investor later sells 1,000 Morrow & Pike shares to another, the money passes between the two investors and Morrow & Pike’s bank balance does not change.

The secondary market still matters to the company. Investors pay more for new shares when they know they can sell them easily later, and the secondary-market price tells the company what its next issue would raise. A price that has fallen means each new share raises less money and existing owners give up a larger slice of the company for the same amount.

Brokers, exchanges and orders

An individual investor does not trade on an exchange directly. She opens an account with a broker, which acts as her agent: it takes her order and routes it to an exchange or another venue where it can be matched with an order on the other side. Ana’s retirement account will work the same way, except that she will mostly buy units of a fund, and the fund’s managers will do the trading in individual shares. The funds-and-indexing lesson in module 3 explains how funds are bought and priced.

At any moment a share has two prices. The bid is the highest price at which someone is currently willing to buy, and the ask is the lowest price at which someone is willing to sell. Suppose Morrow & Pike is quoted at a bid of $27.98 and an ask of $28.02 (fictional quotes). The bid-ask spread is 28.02−27.98=0.0428.02 - 27.98 = 0.0428.02−27.98=0.04 dollars, or 0.04÷28.00=0.14%0.04 \div 28.00 = 0.14\%0.04÷28.00=0.14% of the midpoint. Someone who bought 100 shares at the ask and sold them straight back at the bid would lose $4.00 without the price moving at all: the spread is a cost of trading.

An investor chooses how her order is filled. Investor.gov defines the two basic kinds. A market order is “an order to buy or sell a security immediately”: a market order to buy Morrow & Pike would normally be filled at about the ask, $28.02, but the page warns that the last-traded price is not necessarily the price at which a market order will be executed, because prices can move before the order arrives. A limit order is “an order to buy or sell a security at a specific price or better”: a buy limit order at $28.00 will be filled only if someone will sell at $28.00 or less. A market order gives certainty of trading but not of price; a limit order gives certainty of price but not of trading.

Market capitalisation and enterprise value

A company’s market capitalisation is its share price times its shares outstanding. For Morrow & Pike, 28.00×40=1,12028.00 \times 40 = 1{,}12028.00×40=1,120 million dollars; for Harbour Street, 18.00×120=2,16018.00 \times 120 = 2{,}16018.00×120=2,160 million. A share price alone says nothing about size: Morrow & Pike’s shares cost more than Harbour Street’s, but Harbour Street’s equity is worth almost twice as much, because it has three times as many shares.

Market capitalisation prices only the equity. A buyer of the whole business would also take on its debt and receive its cash, so the price of the business is its enterprise value: market capitalisation plus debt minus cash. Which measure to use depends on the question. For what the shareholders own, and for an index fund’s weights, use market capitalisation. For the size of the business or what it would cost to buy, use enterprise value; the relative-valuation lesson uses it to compare companies financed differently.

Stock market indexes

A stock market index measures the combined price movement of a group of shares, so that “the market rose 1% today” has a precise meaning. The group may be all the listed shares in a market, the largest companies, or one industry. S&P Dow Jones Indices describes the S&P 500 as including 500 leading US companies and covering approximately 80% of available market capitalisation. How the shares are weighted matters as much as which shares are included.

In a value-weighted index (also called capitalisation-weighted) each company counts in proportion to its market capitalisation, so the index’s return is the average of the companies’ returns weighted by their market values. The Harbourline Total Market Index in this course is value-weighted: Harbour Street, with $2,160 million of equity, moves it almost twice as much as Morrow & Pike, with $1,120 million. In a price-weighted index each company counts in proportion to its share price: the index is the sum of the prices divided by a number called the divisor. S&P Dow Jones Indices describes the Dow Jones Industrial Average as a price-weighted measure of 30 US blue-chip companies.

The two methods can tell very different stories on the same day. The challenging worked example below builds a three-company index: on a day when Ardlow, the smallest, rises 10% while Brennoch falls 2% and Calvorth rises 1%, the price-weighted index rises 6.91% and the value-weighted one only 0.65%. Price-weighting gives most weight to whichever share happens to have the highest price, which depends on how many shares the company chose to issue, not on its size. A stock split shows the problem: when a company splits each share in two, its price halves and its weight in a price-weighted index halves too, although nothing about the company has changed, so the divisor must be reset to stop the index jumping. A value-weighted index needs no such adjustment, because the market capitalisation does not change.

Note: Which indexes a fund can track without trading

A fund that holds the same fraction of every company’s shares tracks a value-weighted index automatically: each holding grows or shrinks with its own price, exactly as the company’s market capitalisation does. A fund that holds the same number of shares of each member tracks a price-weighted index just as automatically. An equally weighted index is different: when prices move, the holdings drift apart, so a fund tracking it must sell winners and buy losers to restore equal weights. For a value-weighted fund, trading is needed only when companies enter or leave the index or issue or buy back shares, and its weights measure what investors as a whole actually hold, which is why broad index funds use them.

An index can be calculated from prices alone (a price index) or with dividends reinvested (a total return index). Over long periods the difference is large, because dividends are a substantial part of what shareholders earn. The returns this course uses for the Harbourline funds are total returns, net of each fund’s fees.

What a share price reflects

Why does a Morrow & Pike share cost $28.00 rather than $10 or $100? A buyer is paying for the dividends the share will pay and the price she can sell it for later, which in turn depends on the dividends after that. The price is therefore the present value of the cash flows shareholders expect, discounted at the return they require for the risk, the same logic F04 used for annuities and perpetuities. Module 4 values shares carefully; here the aim is to see what the price says.

Morrow & Pike paid a dividend of $0.70 a share in 2027, a dividend yield of 0.70÷28.00=2.5%0.70 \div 28.00 = 2.5\%0.70÷28.00=2.5%. F05’s capital asset pricing model, with its beta of 0.90, a 4.2% risk-free rate and a 5.5% market risk premium, gives a required return of 4.2%+0.90×5.5%=9.15%4.2\% + 0.90 \times 5.5\% = 9.15\%4.2%+0.90×5.5%=9.15%. Read literally, at $28.00 investors are paying for dividends that grow about 6.5% a year for ever. That is faster than an economy can grow for ever, so the realistic reading is faster growth for some years and slower growth afterwards. The discounted-cash-flow lesson in module 4 reads the same $28.00 with a five-year forecast of 5–8% growth, and finds that the price implies long-run growth of about 2.5% (2.54%) after that. The price is a bundle of expectations, not a measure of what the company has already done.

Two things move a share price, then: news about the cash flows shareholders can expect (a lost contract, a good harvest, a new product), and changes in the return they require (higher interest rates, or more fear about risk in general). A fall in the price is not always bad news about the company: when interest rates rise, the required return rises and prices of most shares fall together. Whether market prices fully reflect the available information is the question of market efficiency, which F00 introduced and the fundamental-analysis lesson in module 4 takes further.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a company receives money when its shares trade

    Only a sale of new shares in the primary market raises money for the company. When investors trade existing shares the money moves between them. A rising share price makes the owners richer and makes a future issue cheaper, but it puts no cash in the company’s bank account.

  • Reading size from the share price

    Morrow & Pike’s shares cost $28.00 and Harbour Street’s $18.00, yet Harbour Street’s equity is worth nearly twice as much. Size is price times shares: market capitalisation, or enterprise value for the whole business. The same error is built into a price-weighted index.

  • Adding cash to enterprise value, or leaving debt out

    Enterprise value is market capitalisation plus debt minus cash. A buyer of the business takes on the debt (a cost) and acquires the cash (which offsets part of the cost). Writing 1,120 + 168 + 30 or using market capitalisation alone misstates the price of the business.

  • Treating preferred shares as safe because the dividend is fixed

    The fixed dividend is paid only if the board declares it, and it ranks behind every lender. Preferred shares carry more risk than the same company’s bonds, though less than its common shares.

  • Expecting a market order to fill at the last price, or a limit order to fill at all

    A market order buys at whatever the best available price is when it arrives, normally the ask, which may differ from the last trade. A limit order protects the price but may never be filled if the market moves away from it.

In practice

Ana’s first look at the market fund. Ana reads the Harbourline fund’s description: it holds every listed share in the home market, weighted by market value. That tells her that roughly twice as much of her money would go into Harbour Street as into Morrow & Pike (their market capitalisations are $2,160 million and $1,120 million), and that the fund need not trade when prices move. She also notes what it does not tell her: how risky the fund has been, how its returns compare with bonds’ and what its fees cost over 30 years. Those are the questions of modules 2 and 3.

An index provider’s weights. An index provider building a value-weighted index decides which companies qualify and recalculates the weights whenever a company issues or buys back shares, joins or leaves. When a large company joins a widely followed index, every fund tracking that index must buy its shares on the same day, which is why index changes are announced in advance and why the rules are published. The weights are market capitalisations, not enterprise values, because the fund buys shares, not businesses.

A treasurer planning a share issue. The finance director of a listed company that wants to raise $50 million of new equity watches the secondary-market price closely. At $28.00 a share Morrow & Pike would need to sell about 1.8 million new shares to raise $50 million (before fees); at $22.00 it would need about 2.3 million, and its existing shareholders would give up more of the company for the same money. She may delay the issue, raise debt instead, or sell the new shares at a discount to the market price so that the offering is taken up.

A buyer comparing targets. An acquirer such as the fictional Quellby compares businesses by enterprise value, because that is what it pays once the debt it inherits is counted. Comparing Morrow & Pike and Harbour Street by market capitalisation alone would understate Harbour Street’s size relative to Morrow & Pike, since so much of Harbour Street is financed with debt.

Summary

  • A share is a residual claim: votes and whatever is left after lenders and preferred shareholders are paid. Dividends are at the board’s discretion.
  • Preferred shares pay a fixed dividend, usually carry no vote, and rank ahead of common shares but behind lenders.
  • Companies raise money in the primary market; existing shares change hands in the secondary market, through brokers and exchanges.
  • A market order trades at once at the best available price; a limit order trades only at a stated price or better. The bid–ask spread is a cost of trading.
  • Market capitalisation = price × shares; enterprise value = market capitalisation + debt − cash. Morrow & Pike: $1,120 million and $1,258 million; Harbour Street: $2,160 million and $3,288 million.
  • A value-weighted index weights companies by market capitalisation; a price-weighted index by share price, which can make a small company dominate. The Harbourline index is value-weighted.
  • A share price is the present value of the cash flows shareholders expect, so it moves with news about cash flows and with the return investors require.

Terms introduced

Share
A unit of ownership in a company, giving its holder a proportional part of the company’s votes and of what it pays its owners. Also called a stock.
Residual claim
The right to whatever is left of a company’s cash flows and assets after every prior claim, such as those of lenders and preferred shareholders, has been paid. Common shareholders hold it.
Limited liability
The rule that a company’s shareholders can lose no more than they paid for their shares: the company’s creditors cannot claim their other assets.
Market order
An order to buy or sell a security immediately at the best price available when the order arrives.
Limit order
An order to buy or sell a security only at a stated price or better: no higher for a buy order, no lower for a sell order.
Stock market index
A number that tracks the combined price movement of a defined group of shares, so that the performance of a market or part of it can be measured and compared.
Value-weighted index
An index in which each company counts in proportion to its market capitalisation. Also called a capitalisation-weighted index.
Price-weighted index
An index calculated as the sum of its members’ share prices divided by a divisor, so that each company counts in proportion to its share price.
Dividend yield
A share’s dividends per share for a year divided by its price.
