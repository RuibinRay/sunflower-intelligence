Diversification

Measure how two investments move together with covariance and correlation, work out the risk of a portfolio, and see how much risk spreading money removes, how much it can’t, and when it lets you down.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate the covariance and correlation of two assets’ returns, from scenarios and from history, and interpret them.
  • Calculate the expected return and standard deviation of a two-asset portfolio and find the mix with the least risk.
  • Explain why a portfolio’s standard deviation is below the weighted average of its parts unless the correlation is 1.
  • Distinguish systematic from unsystematic risk and estimate how a portfolio’s risk falls as holdings are added.
  • Evaluate how well diversification held up in the simulated crashes of 2020 and 2022, and what correlation can and can’t promise.

Review first

  • Measuring risk — Standard deviation of returns from history and scenarios, and annualising it with √12.
  • Measuring returns — Expected return, arithmetic means and the risk premium over bills.
  • Six principles behind financial decisions — Diversification, systematic and unsystematic risk were introduced there in words.
  • Covariance and Pearson’s correlation are taught in depth in B05; this lesson explains what it needs.

The idea

Fictional case

June 2028. Ana Lim has opened a retirement account: $5,000 from her parents on 1 July and $400 a month after that, for about 30 years. Her provider offers three Harbourline index funds (total market, international and bonds) and an actively managed fund. Her first instinct is to put everything in the total-market fund, which earned the most over the last fifteen years. Her second is to ask why anyone holds a bond fund that earned less than half as much. This lesson answers the second question, and in doing so explains why the total-market fund itself is a good idea.

A portfolio is the set of investments someone holds. Each holding’s portfolio weight is its share of the total value: $6,000 in shares and $4,000 in bonds is a 60% weight in shares and 40% in bonds. The weights add up to 1. A portfolio’s return in any period is simply the weighted average of its holdings’ returns. If shares return 10% and bonds 4%, the 60/40 portfolio returns 0.6×10%+0.4×4%=7.6%0.6 \times 10\% + 0.4 \times 4\% = 7.6\%0.6×10%+0.4×4%=7.6%. Expected return works the same way.

Risk does not. The standard deviation of a portfolio is usually less than the weighted average of its holdings’ standard deviations, because when one holding has a bad month the others often don’t, and the losses are partly offset. That is diversification: spreading money across investments that don’t move in lockstep. How much it helps depends on one thing only, beyond the holdings’ own risks: how closely they move together. This lesson measures that with covariance and the correlation coefficient.

The figures in this course’s market history are simulated for teaching: monthly total returns for January 2013 to December 2027, generated to look broadly like real markets, for fictional funds and companies. They are not real history. For real long-run returns, check a source such as Aswath Damodaran’s annually updated table of US stock, bond and bill returns since 1928 (see the references).

Key idea: Return averages; risk doesn’t

A portfolio’s expected return is the weighted average of its holdings’ expected returns. Its standard deviation is below the weighted average of their standard deviations whenever their correlation is less than 1. The gap is the free lunch of diversification: less risk for the same expected return.

In depth

Covariance and correlation

Covariance measures whether two returns tend to be above their means at the same time. For each period, multiply the two assets’ deviations from their own means; the covariance is the average of those products (dividing by n−1n - 1n−1 for a sample, as Excel’s COVARIANCE.S does). If both tend to be above average together and below average together, most products are positive and so is the covariance. If one tends to be up when the other is down, it is negative.

Covariance is hard to read on its own because its units are the product of the two returns’ units (per cent squared, per month squared). Dividing it by the two standard deviations gives the correlation coefficient, ρ\rhoρ (rho), a pure number from −1 to +1: +1 when the two always move in exact proportion in the same direction, −1 when they always move in exact proportion in opposite directions, and 0 when there is no straight-line relation at all. Correlation describes co-movement; it does not say that one asset causes the other’s moves.

For the simulated history, the total-market fund’s monthly standard deviation is 4.62% and the bond fund’s 1.19%. Their monthly covariance is 0.000177, so their correlation is 0.000177÷(0.0462×0.0119)=0.320.000177 \div (0.0462 \times 0.0119) = 0.320.000177÷(0.0462×0.0119)=0.32. Annualised, the covariance is 12×0.000177=0.0021312 \times 0.000177 = 0.0021312×0.000177=0.00213; the correlation is the same in monthly and annual units, because the factor of 12 multiplies the covariance and the product of the two standard deviations alike.

With scenarios instead of history, use probabilities in place of the equal weights: the covariance is ∑sps(rA,s−E[rA])(rB,s−E[rB])\sum_s p_s (r_{A,s} - E[r_A])(r_{B,s} - E[r_B])∑s​ps​(rA,s​−E[rA​])(rB,s​−E[rB​]), with no n−1n - 1n−1. The worked examples do one.

The first two terms are each asset’s own risk, scaled by the square of its weight. Three cases mark out the possibilities. The minimum-variance mix

Setting the derivative of the variance to zero gives the weight in A with the least risk:

w∗=σB2−ρABσAσBσA2+σB2−2ρABσAσBw^{*} = \dfrac{\sigma_B^2 - \rho_{AB}\sigma_A\sigma_B}{\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2 - 2\rho_{AB}\sigma_A\sigma_B}w∗=σA2​+σB2​−2ρAB​σA​σB​σB2​−ρAB​σA​σB​​

For the total-market and bond funds it gives w∗=−1.8%w^{*} = -1.8\%w∗=−1.8%: the least risky mix would sell shares short. Without short selling, the least risky portfolio is 100% bonds. That happens whenever the correlation is above the ratio of the smaller standard deviation to the larger (here 0.0414÷0.1600=0.260.0414 \div 0.1600 = 0.260.0414÷0.1600=0.26): adding a little of a much riskier asset that moves partly with the safe one adds more risk than it removes. Had the correlation been 0, the least risky mix would have held 6.3% in shares. So a little of a risky asset can reduce risk, but only if it is not too correlated with what you already hold.

Two shares of similar risk give a more interesting answer. Morrow & Pike Roasters and Harbour Street Foods, two of the course’s fictional listed companies, have standard deviations of 25.25% and 32.03% a year in the simulated history and a correlation of 0.35. Half in each has an expected return of 11.06% and a standard deviation of 23.61%, well below the 28.64% average of the two. The least risky mix holds 67.7% in Morrow & Pike, with a standard deviation of 22.88%. Notice that every one of these two-share portfolios is still far riskier than the total-market fund (16.00%), which earned about the same.

Many assets: the risk that diversification can’t remove

Add more holdings and the pattern continues, up to a limit. For an equally weighted portfolio of nnn assets, each with the same average variance and the same average covariance with the others, the portfolio variance is

σP2=1n σ2‾+(1−1n)cov‾\sigma_P^2 = \dfrac{1}{n}\,\overline{\sigma^2} + \left(1 - \dfrac{1}{n}\right)\overline{\text{cov}}σP2​=n1​σ2+(1−n1​)cov

As nnn grows, the first term, each holding’s own risk, shrinks towards zero: that is unsystematic risk, the part specific to each company, being diversified away. The second term tends to the average covariance and stays. That is systematic risk, the part every share shares because they all respond to the same economy. No amount of spreading within shares removes it.

Suppose typical shares are like Morrow & Pike and Harbour Street: an average variance of 0.0832 a year (a standard deviation of 28.84%) and an average covariance of 0.0283. The table shows what equal weighting does. Most of the reduction comes from the first ten or so holdings, and the floor, 0.0283=16.83%\sqrt{0.0283} = 16.83\%0.0283​=16.83%, is close to the total-market fund’s own 16.00%: the market fund has already diversified away almost all the specific risk. OpenStax’s Principles of Finance (section 15.2) makes the same point with real shares: risk falls quickly at first and then slowly, and a portfolio of around a dozen shares already removes much of the firm-specific risk.

This is why the market pays no premium for unsystematic risk: anyone can get rid of it cheaply. In the simulated history Morrow & Pike’s standard deviation (25.25%) was far above the market fund’s (16.00%) but its mean return was lower (9.59% against 10.43%). The CAPM lesson turns this into a rule: expected return depends only on the systematic part of risk, measured by beta. To remove systematic risk you have to hold something outside the share market, such as bonds or bills, and accept a lower expected return.

When diversification lets you down

Correlations are not fixed. A common observation in real markets is that shares in different countries and industries fall together in a crash, just when diversification is wanted most. The simulated data show the same pattern for shares, and show why. Measured within single calendar years, the home and international funds’ correlation was 0.90 in 2020 and 0.85 in 2022, against 0.78 over the fifteen years; for Morrow & Pike and Harbour Street it was 0.78 in 2020 and 0.49 in 2022, against 0.35. In March 2020 one shock, a 17% fall in the market, hit every share at once, and a common shock that large dominates the year’s movements.

Bonds behaved differently. Their monthly correlation with shares was lower in 2020 and 2022 than over the whole period. Yet in 2022 the total-market fund lost 26.9% and the bond fund 13.0%: both had a bad year at once. Low correlation means the two didn’t move together month by month; it says nothing about whether both drift down over the same year. In 2022, rising interest rates pushed bond prices down steadily while shares fell for their own reasons. Correlation measures co-movement around each asset’s own average, not whether the averages themselves are positive.

Caution: Diversification reduces risk; it doesn’t guarantee anything

A 60/40 mix of the two funds still lost 21.3% in 2022 in the simulated history. Diversification makes bad outcomes less likely and less severe on average. It does not make shares safe for money needed soon, which is why Ana’s flat deposit, due in April 2032, stays in her savings account.

Common mistakes

  • Averaging standard deviations

    A 60/40 portfolio’s standard deviation is not 60% of one plus 40% of the other unless the correlation is exactly 1. Use the two-asset formula; with Ana’s funds it gives 10.25%, not 11.25%. Expected returns, by contrast, do average.

  • Thinking more holdings always means much less risk

    Adding holdings removes unsystematic risk only. Beyond a few dozen shares the gain is small, and the systematic risk that all shares share remains. Thirty shares in one industry diversify less than a broad index fund, because they share industry risk too.

  • Reading low correlation as protection in a bad year

    Correlation measures how two returns move around their own averages. Two assets with low correlation can both lose money over the same year, as the simulated shares and bonds did in 2022 (−26.9% and −13.0%).

  • Treating a correlation as a constant

    Correlations estimated from history change with the period and are noisy over short windows. Shares tend to become more correlated in crashes. Use a long sample, check how it varies, and don’t rely on a precise value.

  • Confusing covariance and correlation

    Covariance has units (per cent squared) and depends on how volatile the assets are, so its size says little by itself. Correlation divides by both standard deviations and lies between −1 and 1. Use covariance in the variance formula, correlation to judge how closely two assets move.

In practice

Ana’s fund choice. Ana’s first plan, everything in the total-market fund, is already diversified across the whole home share market: its 16.00% standard deviation is close to the floor for shares. What it can’t diversify is the market itself. Adding the bond fund, with its 0.32 correlation, is the cheapest way to lower risk, and the international fund adds a little more diversification across economies. How much of each she should hold depends on her horizon and on how large a fall she could live with: that is the subject of the next lesson.

A pension committee’s review. The trustees of a fictional company pension plan review their asset mix every year. Their consultant reports each asset class’s expected return and standard deviation and the correlations between them, then the portfolio’s standard deviation from the full covariance matrix (the many-asset version of the formula above). She also shows the correlations measured over the worst months, because the trustees care most about whether the mix holds up in a crash.

Employees and their employer’s shares. A fictional battery-storage installer offers staff shares at a discount. An employee who puts most of her savings into them doubles up on one company’s risk: if the firm struggles she could lose her job and her savings together, so her savings and her income are highly correlated. A financial planner would suggest keeping that holding small and spreading the rest across broad funds.

Terms introduced

Portfolio
The set of investments a person or institution holds, described by what each holding is and its weight.
Portfolio weight
A holding’s value divided by the value of the whole portfolio. The weights add up to 1; a negative weight means the asset has been sold short.
Covariance
The average product of two variables’ deviations from their means (dividing by n − 1 for a sample, by n for a population): positive when they tend to move together, negative when they move in opposite directions. Its units are the product of the two variables’ units.
Correlation coefficient
Pearson’s r: a number from −1 to +1 that measures how closely two numeric variables follow a straight line, positive when they rise together. Excel’s CORREL.
Minimum-variance portfolio
The mix of a given set of assets with the lowest possible variance (and so standard deviation) of return, with or without short selling allowed.
Equally weighted portfolio
A portfolio that puts the same amount in each of its n holdings, so that each has a weight of 1/n.
