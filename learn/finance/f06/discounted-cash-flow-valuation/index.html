Discounted cash flow valuation

Value a share as the present value of the cash it will pay: dividends discounted at the cost of equity, or free cash flow to the firm discounted at the WACC, with a terminal value and a bridge from enterprise value to a value per share. Then test how much the answer depends on the rate and on growth.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain why a share’s intrinsic value is the present value of the cash it is expected to pay, and how that differs from its price.
  • Calculate a share’s value with the constant-growth and multi-stage dividend discount models, and check that growth, payout and return on equity agree.
  • Calculate free cash flow to the firm, build a five-year DCF with a Gordon terminal value, and bridge enterprise value to a value per share.
  • Calculate the share of value that comes from the terminal value and the present value of growth opportunities.
  • Evaluate how sensitive a DCF value is to the discount rate, terminal growth and margins, and use a reverse DCF to read what a price assumes.

Review first

  • Perpetuities and growth — The Gordon growth model and the terminal value are growing perpetuities.
  • Project cash flows — Free cash flow from EBIT after tax, depreciation, capital spending and working capital is built the same way for a project.
  • The weighted average cost of capital — The WACC is the rate for free cash flow to the firm; this lesson builds Morrow & Pike’s with that method.
  • Shares and markets — Market capitalisation and enterprise value, which the bridge from a DCF to a share price runs through.
  • The CAPM and beta — Morrow & Pike’s beta of 0.90 and the CAPM cost of equity.

The idea

Fictional case

In June 2028 Ana has opened her retirement account and is reading about the listed coffee companies she meets in her trade. Morrow & Pike Roasters, a speciality roaster that sells to cafés and grocers, closed 2027 at $28.00 a share. Is that a fair price for what the business will earn? This module answers the question three ways: by discounting the cash the company will produce (this lesson), by comparing its price with similar companies’ (relative valuation) and by reading the business behind the numbers (fundamental analysis). Ana is not buying Morrow & Pike; it is the company the course uses to learn the methods. All its figures are fictional.

A share entitles its owner to a slice of whatever the company pays out, for as long as it exists. Its intrinsic value is the present value of that cash, discounted at a rate that rewards the risk of receiving it. Its price is what buyers and sellers agree on today. The two can differ, and an investor who thinks they do is making a forecast: that the company will pay more (or less) than the price implies, or that the market is discounting at the wrong rate.

There are two ways to count the cash. The first follows the shareholder: a share is worth the dividends it will pay, discounted at the cost of equity. This is the dividend discount model, and its simplest form is F04’s Gordon growth model. The second follows the business: estimate the cash the operations produce before anyone is paid, the free cash flow to the firm, discount it at the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) to get the value of the whole business, its enterprise value, and then hand the lenders their share to leave the value of the equity. The second is what most analysts mean by a DCF, and it is the main method in this lesson.

Neither route can forecast cash for ever, so both split the future in two: a few years forecast one by one (the explicit forecast period, five years here), and everything after summarised by a terminal value, usually a growing perpetuity. For a healthy company the terminal value is most of the answer, which is the method’s main weakness and the reason this lesson spends as long on testing a DCF as on building one.

Key idea: A valuation is a set of assumptions with a number attached

Every DCF value depends on a growth path, margins, reinvestment, a discount rate and a terminal growth rate. Change any of them within a reasonable range and the value moves by more than most price differences you are trying to judge. The skill is not producing one number but knowing which assumptions drive it and what the market price assumes.

Dividends: the Gordon model and its limits

What growth? A firm that keeps a share bbb of its earnings (the retention ratio) and earns a return on equity ROE on what it keeps grows its earnings, and so its dividends, at about g=b×ROEg = b \times \text{ROE}g=b×ROE (F05’s sustainable growth, in its simplest form). Morrow & Pike earned net income of $62.25 million on book equity of $520 million, an ROE of 12.0%, and paid out $0.70 of its $1.56 earnings per share, a payout ratio of 45.0%, so it retains 55.0%. Growth is 0.550×12.0%=6.59%0.550 \times 12.0\% = 6.59\%0.550×12.0%=6.59%, next year’s dividend $0.746, and the Gordon value 0.746÷(9.15%−6.59%)=$29.110.746 \div (9.15\% - 6.59\%) = \$29.110.746÷(9.15%−6.59%)=$29.11, close to the $28.00 price.

Close, but fragile. The denominator is the gap between two estimates, 2.56 points here, so small changes swing the answer: at 5% growth the value is $17.71; at 6%, $23.56. And 6.59% a year for ever is a strong claim: a firm that grows faster than the economy for ever eventually becomes the economy. The model also cannot value a firm that pays no dividend, and it assumes the payout never changes, which is the next problem.

Multi-stage dividends, and keeping growth and payout consistent

A multi-stage model lets growth change: dividends grow fast for a few years, then settle at a long-run rate, valued by Gordon at the end of the fast stage. Suppose Morrow & Pike’s dividends grow 8% a year for five years, then 3% for ever. Keeping the 45% payout throughout, the five dividends ($0.756 to $1.029) are worth $3.39 today, the terminal value at the end of year 5 is 1.029×1.03÷(9.15%−3%)=$17.231.029 \times 1.03 \div (9.15\% - 3\%) = \$17.231.029×1.03÷(9.15%−3%)=$17.23, worth $11.12 today, and the share $14.51: barely half the price.

That answer is inconsistent, not cautious. If growth falls to 3% while the firm still earns 12.0% on equity, it needs to retain only 3%÷12.0%=25%3\% \div 12.0\% = 25\%3%÷12.0%=25% of its earnings, so its payout rises to about 75%. A mature company pays out more, not the same. The challenging worked example below redoes the valuation with the higher payout and gets $21.92. The lesson is general: in every growth model, growth must be paid for by reinvestment, and the reinvestment must match the return the company earns on it.

Free cash flow to the firm

The firm route values the business, not the dividend policy. Free cash flow to the firm (FCFF) is the cash the operations generate after tax and after the investment needed to keep them growing, available to all the firm’s lenders and shareholders together:

FCFF=EBIT (1−T)+D&A−Capital spending−ΔNWC\text{FCFF} = \text{EBIT}\,(1 - T) + \text{D\&A} - \text{Capital spending} - \Delta\text{NWC}FCFF=EBIT(1−T)+D&A−Capital spending−ΔNWC

It is the same construction as a project’s cash flow in F05: operating profit taxed as if the firm had no debt (the WACC carries the tax saving on interest, so it must not be counted twice), depreciation and amortisation added back because they are not cash, capital spending and the increase in net working capital taken off because they are. Morrow & Pike’s 2027: 92×0.75+26−34−4=5792 \times 0.75 + 26 - 34 - 4 = 5792×0.75+26−34−4=57, $57 million. This is not F03’s free cash flow (cash from operations minus capital spending), which comes after interest has been paid and so belongs to shareholders, not to the firm as a whole.

Morrow & Pike’s discount rate

FCFF belongs to lenders and shareholders, so it is discounted at the WACC (F05’s method). The cost of equity is the 9.15% above. The cost of debt is the yield to maturity on Morrow & Pike’s 2031 bond: a 4.75% coupon paid twice a year, three and a half years to run, trading at $97.80 per $100, which gives a yield of 5.45% (the bond lesson shows the calculation), or 4.09% after 25% tax. Its debt is $168 million against a market capitalisation of $28.00×40 million=$1,120\$28.00 \times 40\text{ million} = \$1{,}120$28.00×40 million=$1,120 million, a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.15 (F05’s), so debt is 13.0% of value and equity 87.0%:

WACC=0.870×9.15%+0.130×4.09%=8.49%\text{WACC} = 0.870 \times 9.15\% + 0.130 \times 4.09\% = 8.49\%WACC=0.870×9.15%+0.130×4.09%=8.49%

One point on the WACC moves the value by about $5 to $6 a share at 3% growth; one point of terminal growth by $3 to $10. One point on the EBIT margin (11.1% or 13.1% instead of 12.1%) moves it to $26.97 or $33.30. Against that range, the 7.6% gap between $30.14 and $28.00 is well inside the noise. A DCF that says “7.6% undervalued” is really saying “about right, unless you have a view on growth, margins or risk that the market does not”.

An alternative terminal value multiplies the final year’s EBITDA by an exit multiple. At Morrow & Pike’s current EV/EBITDA of 10.66, 2032’s EBITDA of $163.6 million gives a terminal value of $1,744.5 million and $32.83 a share. The exit multiple hides a growth assumption: this one implies 3.49% growth for ever. Using today’s multiple at the end of the forecast also assumes the market will price the company in 2032 as it does now, so it is a check on the Gordon value rather than a replacement for it; the relative valuation lesson covers multiples.

Is the terminal value consistent?

The dividend model’s lesson applies here too. Morrow & Pike’s return on invested capital in 2027 was 69÷(520+168−30)=10.5%69 \div (520 + 168 - 30) = 10.5\%69÷(520+168−30)=10.5%. To grow 3% a year for ever at that return it must reinvest 3%÷10.5%=28.6%3\% \div 10.5\% = 28.6\%3%÷10.5%=28.6% of its after-tax operating profit each year; in 2032 the forecast reinvests only 12.0% (capital spending above depreciation, plus working capital). A terminal value that assumes 3% growth with too little reinvestment counts growth the company has not paid for. With the reinvestment matched to the return, the terminal free cash flow is 95.8×1.03×(1−0.286)95.8 \times 1.03 \times (1 - 0.286)95.8×1.03×(1−0.286) and the value falls to $25.16 a share. Which is right depends on whether new investment can earn more than the 10.5% the existing business does; the analyst has to say which she believes.

What the price is paying for: PVGO

If Morrow & Pike stopped growing and paid out all its earnings, a share would be worth EPS÷rE=1.556÷9.15%=$17.01\text{EPS} \div r_E = 1.556 \div 9.15\% = \$17.01EPS÷rE​=1.556÷9.15%=$17.01. The rest of the price, 28.00−17.01=$10.9928.00 - 17.01 = \$10.9928.00−17.01=$10.99 or 39% of it, is the present value of growth opportunities (PVGO): what buyers pay for the expectation that the company will invest at returns above its cost of capital. Growth adds value only when new investment earns more than the cost of capital; growth at a return equal to it adds nothing, and below it destroys value. The fundamental analysis lesson uses this to read a company.

Reading a price backwards: the reverse DCF

Because the inputs are uncertain, many analysts turn the question round and ask what the price assumes. Holding the forecast and the 8.49% WACC, a terminal growth rate of 2.54% makes the DCF equal to $28.00; holding 3% growth, a WACC of 8.86% does. The market price is consistent with long-run growth a little below the forecast’s 3%, or a discount rate about 0.4 points higher. The question becomes “do I believe Morrow & Pike will grow faster than 2.5% for ever?”, which is easier to argue about than a single value.

Common mistakes

  • Discounting free cash flow to the firm at the cost of equity

    FCFF belongs to lenders and shareholders, so its rate is the WACC; dividends (and free cash flow to equity) belong to shareholders, so their rate is the cost of equity. Discounting Morrow & Pike’s FCFF at 9.15% instead of 8.49% gives $26.48 a share: a different answer from a mismatched rate, not a more cautious one.

  • Stopping at enterprise value

    The DCF of FCFF values the whole business. Dividing it by the number of shares ($1,343.4m ÷ 40m = $33.59) gives every dollar of the lenders’ claim to the shareholders. Subtract debt, add cash, then divide.

  • Terminal growth that is too high or unpaid for

    A terminal growth rate close to the discount rate makes the value as large as you like. Keep it at or below long-run growth in the economy, and check that the forecast reinvests enough to earn it: growth ÷ return on capital.

  • Treating the DCF value as precise

    A value of $30.14 against a price of $28.00 is not a 7.6% opportunity when one point on the WACC moves the value by $5. Report a range from defensible inputs, and say which input decides the conclusion.

  • Freezing the payout in a multi-stage dividend model

    When growth slows, a firm needs to reinvest less and can pay out more. Keeping the high-growth payout in the long-run stage understates value (Morrow & Pike: $14.51 instead of $21.92). Set the long-run payout to 1 − g ÷ ROE.

  • Counting interest twice

    FCFF uses EBIT × (1 − T), with no interest deducted, because the WACC already uses the after-tax cost of debt. Subtracting interest from the cash flows as well undervalues the firm.

In practice

An equity analyst’s model. A sell-side analyst covering food and drink companies keeps a DCF for each. She updates the forecast after every set of results, rebuilds the WACC once a year, and publishes a target price with a sensitivity table, because clients want to know what has to be true for the shares to be worth more. Most of her time goes on the drivers (volume, prices, margins, capital spending) rather than on the discounting, which a spreadsheet does in one line.

A company buying another. When a listed food group (fictional) considers buying a roaster, its corporate development team values the target by DCF at the target’s own WACC (its risk, not the buyer’s), on the buyer’s forecast including the savings it expects. The DCF gives the most it should pay; anything above that transfers value to the seller’s shareholders. The same team always checks the DCF against what comparable companies trade at.

Ana’s own use. Ana will probably never buy shares in Morrow & Pike. What she takes from this lesson is a habit for any share someone tells her is cheap: ask what the price assumes about growth and risk, and whether that is more or less believable than the story. The course project asks her to apply it to a coffee company she knows well, which is exactly when the habit is hardest to keep.

Terms introduced

Intrinsic value
What an asset is worth on an analyst’s estimate of the cash it will produce and the return its risk requires, as distinct from its market price.
Dividend discount model
A valuation of a share as the present value of the dividends it is expected to pay, discounted at the cost of equity. The Gordon growth model is its constant-growth form. Abbreviated DDM.
Free cash flow to the firm
The cash a company’s operations generate after tax and after the investment in long-lived assets and working capital needed to sustain them, before any payment to lenders or shareholders: EBIT × (1 − tax rate) + depreciation and amortisation − capital spending − the increase in net working capital. Abbreviated FCFF.
Present value of growth opportunities
The part of a share’s price above the value of its current earnings as a no-growth perpetuity (earnings per share ÷ cost of equity): what investors pay for expected profitable growth. Abbreviated PVGO.
Reverse discounted cash flow
Solving a DCF model for the growth rate, margin or discount rate that makes its value equal the market price, to see what the price assumes. Also called a reverse DCF.
