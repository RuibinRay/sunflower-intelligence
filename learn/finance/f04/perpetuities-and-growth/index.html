Perpetuities and growth

A payment that never stops still has a finite value today. Learn to value level and growing perpetuities, including ones that start now or later, price a preferred share, size an endowment, and see why anything valued as growing forever depends so heavily on the gap between the discount rate and the growth rate.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate the present value of a level perpetuity, a perpetuity due and a deferred perpetuity.
  • Calculate the present value of a growing perpetuity, and explain why it needs r > g and next period’s payment.
  • Calculate the value of a preferred share and the gift needed to endow a payment forever, with or without growth.
  • Analyse how the value of a growing perpetuity responds to small changes in the discount rate and the growth rate.
  • Explain what a terminal value is, and estimate one for a simple forecast.

Review first

  • Annuities — A perpetuity is an annuity with no last payment; the timing rules (end of period, in advance, deferred) and the growing annuity carry over.
  • Present and future value — You need discount factors and why a present value falls as the rate or the wait increases.
  • Interest and inflation — The growing scholarship keeps its purchasing power by growing with inflation, Ana’s 2.8% assumption.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

In 2027 the Bayside Community College Foundation, which raises money for a local college, invites businesses to endow named scholarships. Ana would like to create a Lantern Barista Scholarship: $2,000 a year to a student on the college’s hospitality course, for as long as the course runs, which she hopes is for ever. The foundation invests every gift and pays awards from the income; it expects to earn 5% a year. The first award would be made a year after the gift. How much would Lantern have to give? The payments never stop, so they add up to an infinite number of dollars. Yet the answer is a modest, finite sum. (The foundation, the scholarship and its figures are fictional.)

Payments that never stop

A perpetuity is a stream of equal payments at equal intervals that continues for ever. True perpetuities are rare, but several important things come close: a scholarship or professorship paid from an endowment, a preferred share whose fixed dividend has no end date, the rent from land, and, in valuation, the cash a business is expected to generate long after any forecast stops.

The value of a perpetuity is easiest to see from the endowment’s side. Suppose the foundation holds $40,000 and earns 5% a year. Each year the fund earns 40,000×0.05=2,00040{,}000 \times 0.05 = 2{,}00040,000×0.05=2,000 dollars, which it pays out as the award, leaving the $40,000 untouched to earn the same again next year, and the year after, for ever. So $40,000 today is exactly enough to pay $2,000 a year in perpetuity: the payment divided by the rate.

PV=Cr=2,0000.05=40,000\text{PV} = \frac{C}{r} = \frac{2{,}000}{0.05} = 40{,}000PV=rC​=0.052,000​=40,000

The infinite stream has a finite value because distant payments are worth almost nothing today. The table splits the $40,000 into the value contributed by each stretch of years.

The first ten awards supply almost two-fifths of the value, and everything after year 100 adds less than one percent. That is why a perpetuity costs only a little more than a long annuity: a scholarship for 50 years would cost $36,511.85, against $40,000 for ever.

Payments that grow

A fixed $2,000 has a weakness. If prices rise by 2.8% a year, as Ana assumes, the award buys less every year; after 25 years it buys only about half what it does today. To keep the scholarship’s purchasing power, the award must grow with inflation: $2,000 in the first year, $2,056 in the second, and so on. A stream that grows at a constant rate ggg for ever is a growing perpetuity, and its value is the first payment divided by the gap between the rate and the growth rate:

PV=C1r−g=2,0000.05−0.028=2,0000.022≈90,909.09\text{PV} = \frac{C_1}{r - g} = \frac{2{,}000}{0.05 - 0.028} = \frac{2{,}000}{0.022} \approx 90{,}909.09PV=r−gC1​​=0.05−0.0282,000​=0.0222,000​≈90,909.09

The endowment view explains the formula again. A fund of $90,909.09 earns 5%, or $4,545.45, in the first year. It pays out $2,000, which is 2.2% of the fund, and keeps $2,545.45, which is exactly 2.8% of the fund. So the fund grows by 2.8%, and next year it can pay 2.8% more, for ever. Out of every year’s 5% return, the fund spends r−gr - gr−g and reinvests ggg.

Growth makes the scholarship cost more than twice as much, and it moves the value further into the future. With growth, the first ten awards supply only about 19.1% of the value, and the awards after year 100 about 12.0%, against 0.8% without growth. The further the value lies in the future, the more it depends on assumptions about the far future.

Key idea: Everything depends on r − g

The value of a growing perpetuity is the first payment divided by r − g, so the gap between the two rates matters far more than either rate on its own. When the gap halves, the value doubles. When g reaches r, the formula breaks down: payments that grow as fast as the discount rate never shrink in today’s money, and their value is unlimited. Any valuation built on a growing perpetuity is only as good as its r − g.

Fictional case

Ana now has three prices. A level $2,000 award for ever needs $40,000. An award that keeps pace with inflation needs about $90,909. A level award for ten years, an annuity, needs 2,000×(1−1.05−10)÷0.05≈15,443.472{,}000 \times (1 - 1.05^{-10}) \div 0.05 \approx 15{,}443.472,000×(1−1.05−10)÷0.05≈15,443.47 dollars. A year after Lantern opened, $90,909 is far beyond what it can give; the In practice section returns to her decision.

In depth

From annuity to perpetuity

A perpetuity is the limit of an annuity as the number of payments grows without end. In the annuity formula, PV=C[1−(1+r)−n]÷r\text{PV} = C[1 - (1 + r)^{-n}] \div rPV=C[1−(1+r)−n]÷r, the term (1+r)−n(1 + r)^{-n}(1+r)−n is the present value of $1 paid in period nnn, and it shrinks towards zero as nnn grows. What is left is C÷rC \div rC÷r. In the growing-annuity formula the corresponding term is [(1+g)÷(1+r)]n[(1 + g) \div (1 + r)]^n[(1+g)÷(1+r)]n. It shrinks to zero only if g<rg \lt rg<r; if g≥rg \ge rg≥r it never shrinks, each payment is worth at least as much today as the one before, and the sum has no limit. That is the mathematical reason for the condition r>gr \gt gr>g.

Turning the argument round gives a useful way to value annuities, used in the annuities lesson: an annuity is a perpetuity starting now minus a perpetuity that starts after the last payment.

When the payments start: perpetuity due and deferred perpetuity

Like the annuity formula, C÷rC \div rC÷r values the stream one period before the first payment. Every timing variation follows from that rule.

  • A perpetuity due pays its first amount today. It is today’s payment plus an ordinary perpetuity: C+C÷r=C(1+r)÷rC + C \div r = C(1 + r) \div rC+C÷r=C(1+r)÷r. If Bayside paid the first award on the day of the gift, Lantern would need 2,000+40,000=42,0002{,}000 + 40{,}000 = 42{,}0002,000+40,000=42,000 dollars.
  • A deferred perpetuity starts later. If the first payment is at the end of period k+1k + 1k+1, the formula gives its value at time kkk; discount that by kkk periods. If the course, and so the first award, began in four years’ time, the gift would be 40,000÷1.053≈34,553.5040{,}000 \div 1.05^3 \approx 34{,}553.5040,000÷1.053≈34,553.50 dollars.

Preferred shares

A preferred stock (preference share in the UK) is a share that pays a fixed dividend, usually stated as a percentage of its par value, and ranks ahead of common stock: preferred holders are paid their dividends before common shareholders receive any, and have priority if the company is liquidated, though they usually have no vote. Most preferred shares have no maturity date. If the dividend is paid for ever, the share is a level perpetuity and its value is the dividend divided by the return investors require on similar shares:

P=DrpP = \frac{D}{r_p}P=rp​D​

A fictional utility’s preferred share with a $100 par value and a 5.5% dividend pays $5.50 a year. If investors require 7%, it is worth 5.50÷0.07≈78.575.50 \div 0.07 \approx 78.575.50÷0.07≈78.57 dollars; if the required return rises to 8% the value falls to $68.75, and if it falls to 6% the value rises to $91.67. A fixed payment stream moves in price opposite to the rates investors demand, and a level perpetuity is very sensitive because all its payments are fixed for ever. Rearranging, a market price tells you the required return: rp=D÷Pr_p = D \div Prp​=D÷P.

Two cautions. Dividends are often paid quarterly: then use the quarterly dividend and the quarterly rate. If 7% is an APR compounded quarterly, the quarterly rate is 1.75% and 1.375÷0.01751.375 \div 0.01751.375÷0.0175 gives the same $78.57; if 7% is an effective annual rate, the quarterly rate is 1.071/4−1≈1.7059%1.07^{1/4} - 1 \approx 1.7059\%1.071/4−1≈1.7059% and the share is worth about $80.60, because quarterly dividends arrive sooner than one annual payment. And a fixed dividend does not keep pace with inflation, so its purchasing power falls year after year.

Growing perpetuities and share prices

Applied to a company’s common shares, the growing perpetuity becomes the constant-growth dividend discount model, also called the Gordon growth model: if the next dividend is D1D_1D1​ and dividends are expected to grow at ggg for ever, the share is worth P0=D1÷(r−g)P_0 = D_1 \div (r - g)P0​=D1​÷(r−g). Two practical points matter. The formula needs next period’s dividend: if a company has just paid D0D_0D0​, then D1=D0(1+g)D_1 = D_0(1 + g)D1​=D0​(1+g). And rearranging gives r=D1÷P0+gr = D_1 \div P_0 + gr=D1​÷P0​+g: the return a shareholder can expect is the dividend yield plus the growth rate, a common way to estimate the return investors require from a market price. The model suits mature companies whose dividends grow steadily; it says nothing useful about a company that pays no dividends. Raising ggg from 3% to 4% at an 8% discount rate raises the value by 25%, from $2,000 to $2,500; raising rrr from 8% to 9% with 3% growth cuts it by about 16.7%. The smaller the gap, the more violent the effect: with r−gr - gr−g at 1%, narrowing the gap by half a percentage point doubles the value, and narrowing it by a full point makes it infinite. Small, uncertain differences in assumptions become large differences in value, which is why analysts present a range rather than a single figure.

Terminal value

No one can forecast a business’s cash flows year by year for ever. A standard approach forecasts them in detail for a few years, then assumes that from the end of the forecast the cash flows grow at a steady rate for ever. The value at the end of the forecast of everything that comes after it is the terminal value. With a forecast that ends in year NNN:

TVN=CFN+1r−g,PV of the terminal value=TVN(1+r)N\text{TV}_N = \frac{\text{CF}_{N+1}}{r - g}, \qquad \text{PV of the terminal value} = \frac{\text{TV}_N}{(1 + r)^N}TVN​=r−gCFN+1​​,PV of the terminal value=(1+r)NTVN​​

The terminal value sits at year NNN, because the formula values a stream one period before its first payment, CFN+1\text{CF}_{N+1}CFN+1​. It must be discounted NNN periods, like any single sum at year NNN. Terminal values often supply most of a business’s estimated value, as the worked example shows, so the choice of ggg and rrr deserves more scrutiny than any single year of the forecast. Assumptions and limits

  • The rate and the growth rate are constant for ever. Neither is literally true; the model is a summary of the long run.
  • The growth rate must be below the discount rate. A long-run growth rate above the growth of the whole economy cannot last for ever: a business growing faster than the economy for ever would eventually be larger than the economy.
  • The payments must actually continue. A preferred share that the company may retire, a scholarship course that may close, or a business that may fail is worth less than the formula suggests.
  • Rates and payments must be consistent: nominal payments (which include inflation, like the growing scholarship) with a nominal rate, real payments with a real rate. The inflation-and-real-returns lesson in Module 4 shows the two give the same value when done consistently.
  • The payment period and the rate period must match, as for annuities.

Common mistakes

  • Using this year’s payment instead of next year’s

    The growing-perpetuity formula needs C₁, the payment one period from now. If a company has just paid a $2.00 dividend and dividends grow 3%, the next dividend is $2.06, not $2.00. Using $2.00 understates the value by the factor (1 + g).

  • Applying C₁ ÷ (r − g) when g is not below r

    With g equal to or above r the formula returns infinity or a negative number. Neither is a value: the assumption of constant growth at or above the discount rate for ever is impossible. Revisit the growth rate, which should not exceed long-run economic growth.

  • Getting the date of the value wrong

    C ÷ r values the stream one period before the first payment. A perpetuity paying today is worth C + C ÷ r; a perpetuity whose first payment is in year 5 is worth (C ÷ r) at year 4, discounted four years, not five.

  • Mixing periods

    A quarterly dividend needs a quarterly rate. Dividing an annual dividend by a quarterly rate, or a quarterly dividend by an annual rate, is off by a factor of four.

  • Treating a terminal value as precise

    A terminal value often supplies most of a valuation, and it depends on r − g. Report a range over plausible rates and growth rates, and check the result against other evidence, such as the valuation multiples of similar businesses.

  • Mixing nominal and real figures

    A payment that grows with inflation is a nominal stream: discount it at a nominal rate. Discounting it at a real rate, or a level real payment at a nominal rate, double-counts or ignores inflation. Module 4 develops this.

In practice

Perpetuity formulas are short, which makes them easy to misuse; professionals rely on them for their clarity and then test them hard.

Pricing preferred shares. The treasurer of a fictional regional bank plans to issue preferred shares to strengthen its capital. Investors require about 6.5% on similar issues, so to sell $100 shares at par the bank must offer a dividend of about $6.50 a share. If rates rise before the issue, the bank must raise the dividend or accept a lower price. An investor holding such shares watches the same arithmetic in reverse: when required returns rise, the market value of a fixed dividend for ever falls.

Running an endowment. The investment committee of a fictional university endowment expects a long-run return of 6% and inflation of about 2.5%. If it wants each year’s spending to keep its purchasing power for ever, it can spend about r−g=3.5%r - g = 3.5\%r−g=3.5% of the fund each year and must reinvest the rest. Spending more pleases today’s students at the expense of future ones; spending less does the opposite. The growing perpetuity turns that trade-off between generations into a single number the committee can debate.

Valuing a business. An analyst valuing a fictional chain of hotels forecasts five years of cash flows and adds a terminal value. Because the terminal value is most of the answer, she shows a grid of values for discount rates from 8% to 10% and growth rates from 1.5% to 3%, and compares the implied value with what similar hotel chains sell for. If her central case needs growth above the long-run growth of the economy to justify the price, she treats that as a warning, not a conclusion.

Fictional case

Ana decides that Lantern cannot yet endow a scholarship for ever. She asks the foundation whether Lantern could fund a ten-year award instead, which at 5% costs about $15,443.47, and plans to revisit a permanent, inflation-linked award, about $90,909 at today’s assumptions, once the business is established. If she did endow it, she would ask the foundation how it decides what to pay out each year, because its long-run return, not hers, sets the r in the formula.

Summary

  • A perpetuity pays the same amount every period for ever. Its value is C ÷ r, one period before the first payment: the sum that, invested at r, pays C for ever without running down.
  • A growing perpetuity pays C₁ next period, growing at g for ever. Its value is C₁ ÷ (r − g), and it exists only when r > g.
  • A perpetuity due (first payment today) is worth C + C ÷ r; a deferred perpetuity is valued one period before its first payment, then discounted to today.
  • A preferred share with a fixed dividend for ever is worth D ÷ r_p; its price falls when required returns rise. The Gordon growth model values a share as D₁ ÷ (r − g).
  • Distant payments add little to a level perpetuity, but much more to a growing one: the value of anything growing for ever depends heavily on r − g.
  • A terminal value, CF(N+1) ÷ (r − g) at year N, captures everything after a forecast and often most of a business’s value, so it should be reported as a range.
  • Lantern’s $2,000 scholarship would need $40,000 at 5% as a level award, about $90,909 growing with 2.8% inflation, and about $15,443 for ten years only.

Terms introduced

Perpetuity
A stream of equal payments at equal intervals that continues for ever. Its present value is the payment divided by the rate per period, C ÷ r.
Growing perpetuity
A stream of payments that continues for ever and grows at a constant rate g each period. Its present value is the next payment divided by (r − g), and it exists only when r > g.
Perpetuity due
A perpetuity whose first payment is made today. Worth the payment plus an ordinary perpetuity: C + C ÷ r.
Endowment
A fund whose capital is invested so that its income pays for a purpose, such as a scholarship or a professorship, indefinitely.
Preferred stock
A class of shares that pays a fixed dividend, usually stated as a percentage of par value, and ranks ahead of common stock for dividends and in a liquidation, usually without voting rights. Called preference shares in the UK.
Gordon growth model
The constant-growth dividend discount model: a share is worth next year’s dividend divided by (required return − growth rate), P₀ = D₁ ÷ (r − g).
Terminal value
The value, at the end of an explicit forecast period, of all the cash flows expected after it, often estimated as a growing perpetuity: CF(N+1) ÷ (r − g).
