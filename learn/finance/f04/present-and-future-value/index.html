Present and future value

An amount of money has a different value at every date. Learn to draw a timeline, discount single amounts with discount factors, move any amount to any date, cope with rates that change, and solve for the missing amount, rate or time, so that amounts at different dates can be compared fairly.

About 90 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Construct a cash-flow timeline for a single-amount problem and match the rate per period to the spacing of the periods.
  • Calculate the present value of a future amount with a discount factor, for any compounding frequency and for amounts due between compounding dates.
  • Compare and combine amounts due at different dates by moving them to a common comparison date, including when the rate changes from period to period.
  • Find the unknown amount, rate or time in a single-amount problem with a formula, a financial calculator or a spreadsheet, and check the answer.
  • Explain why present value falls as the rate or the time rises, and why distant amounts are far more sensitive to the rate than near ones.

Review first

  • What finance is: money, time and risk — It introduced future value, present value and the discount rate over whole years.
  • Simple and compound interest — You need compound growth, and solving for the rate with a root and for the time with logarithms.
  • Effective annual rates — You need to turn a quoted annual rate into a rate per period and an effective annual rate.

The idea

Fictional case

It is March 2027 and Lantern Coffee Co. has been trading for a year. Two amounts on Ana’s desk are both “$10,000”, and they are not worth the same. One is cash in Lantern’s bank account today. The other is the $10,000 Lantern must repay Coastline Bank on 1 March 2028, when its two-year note matures. Since Lantern borrowed in March 2026 at 6% a year, rates have risen: a similar loan now costs 8% a year compounded quarterly (a fictional rate, like everything about Coastline). Ana wants to know what next year’s repayment is worth in today’s money. The answer is less than $10,000, and working out how much less is the subject of this lesson.

One equation, four quantities

F00 introduced the two directions of time value. An amount today grows to a future value by compounding; an amount received later has a present value, found by discounting. They are one equation read in two directions:

FV=PV (1+r)n⟺PV=FV(1+r)n\text{FV} = \text{PV}\,(1 + r)^n \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \text{PV} = \frac{\text{FV}}{(1 + r)^n}FV=PV(1+r)n⟺PV=(1+r)nFV​

The equation links four quantities: the present value, the future value, the rate per period rrr and the number of periods nnn. Know any three and you can find the fourth. Module 1 used it to grow money forward and to solve for the rate and the time. This lesson turns it round, to value amounts that arrive later, and stretches it to any pair of dates.

Every symbol must use the same period. If interest is compounded quarterly, rrr is the rate per quarter and nnn counts quarters; if monthly, months. With a quoted annual rate (an APR) compounded mmm times a year, r=APR÷mr = \text{APR} \div mr=APR÷m and n=m×tn = m \times tn=m×t, where ttt is the time in years. The lesson on effective annual rates explains why. Here it is the first step of every calculation.

Draw the timeline first

A cash-flow timeline is a line marked off in periods, with time 0 at the date you are valuing from, usually today, and each amount written at the date it arrives. Drawing one takes ten seconds and prevents most time-value mistakes. It shows how many periods separate each amount from the date you care about, which way the amount has to move, and whether the rate matches the spacing. Amounts you receive are usually written above the line and amounts you pay below it, or with a minus sign.

For Lantern’s repayment, time 0 is 1 March 2027 and the periods are quarters, because the market rate is compounded quarterly. The $10,000 sits at quarter 4, on 1 March 2028. The rate per quarter is 8%÷4=2%8\% \div 4 = 2\%8%÷4=2%. Moving the amount back one quarter means dividing by 1.02; moving it back four quarters means dividing by 1.0241.02^41.024.

PV=10,0001.024=10,000×0.923845=9,238.45\text{PV} = \frac{10{,}000}{1.02^4} = 10{,}000 \times 0.923845 = 9{,}238.45PV=1.02410,000​=10,000×0.923845=9,238.45

In March 2027 the repayment is worth $9,238.45. Put the other way round, $9,238.45 invested today at 2% a quarter grows to exactly $10,000 by 1 March 2028, so a lender who can earn 8% compounded quarterly elsewhere would pay no more than that today for the right to receive the $10,000. The note also pays $150 of interest every quarter until then; the next lesson, on discounting cash flows, adds those payments and values the whole note.

The discount factor

The number 0.923845 is a discount factor: the present value of $1 received after nnn periods at a rate rrr per period, 1÷(1+r)n1 \div (1 + r)^n1÷(1+r)n. Multiply any amount by it to bring the amount back to today. A discount factor is a price: at 2% a quarter, a dollar due in four quarters costs about 92.4 cents today. With a positive rate every factor lies between 0 and 1, and it shrinks as the rate rises or the wait lengthens.

At 2% a dollar due in 30 years is worth about 55 cents today; at 12%, just over 3 cents. That is why distant amounts barely count when rates are high, and why long-lived decisions, such as pensions, buildings and power plants, depend so heavily on the rate used to discount them.

Key idea: Why present value falls as r or n rises

A present value is the amount that would grow into the future sum. A higher rate makes money grow faster, so less is needed today. A longer wait gives the money more periods to grow, so again less is needed today. The present value never reaches zero, but for long waits at high rates it comes close.

Discounting the repayment at 8% compounded once a year would give 10,000÷1.08=9,259.2610{,}000 \div 1.08 = 9{,}259.2610,000÷1.08=9,259.26, about $21 more, because 8% compounded annually is a lower effective rate than 8% compounded quarterly, which is 8.243216% a year. Either route gives the right answer as long as the rate and the periods match: discount at 2% a quarter for 4 quarters, or at the effective annual rate for 1 year, 10,000÷1.08243216=9,238.4510{,}000 \div 1.08243216 = 9{,}238.4510,000÷1.08243216=9,238.45.

In depth

Moving an amount to any date

Present value is a special case of a more general rule. An amount XXX at period jjj is worth X(1+r)k−jX(1 + r)^{k - j}X(1+r)k−j at period kkk. If kkk is later than jjj, the exponent is positive and the amount is compounded forward; if kkk is earlier, the exponent is negative and the amount is discounted back. The date you move everything to is the comparison date (some textbooks call it the focal date). It need not be today.

Why choose another date? Because that is sometimes where the question lives. A business rescheduling a debt wants the single payment, at a new date, that is worth the same as the payments it replaces; a saver wants to know what a deposit will be worth at retirement. As long as one rate applies throughout, the choice of comparison date never changes which of two options is worth more. Moving every amount from one date to another multiplies all their values by the same factor.

Three offers compared at two dates, at 6% a year compounded annually

Each value at year 4 is the value at year 0 multiplied by 1.06⁴ = 1.262477.

The $9,000 in two years is worth the most at either date, by a whisker: $9.97 more than $8,000 today and $12.58 more at year 4, the same gap grown by 1.262477. The $10,000 in four years, the largest sum, is worth the least. The values change with the date; the ranking does not.

Replacing payments: the equation of value

When a borrower and a lender agree to replace one set of payments with another, the new payments must be worth the same as the old ones at the agreed rate. Writing that condition at a comparison date gives an equation of value: the old payments, each moved to the date, add up to the same total as the new payments, each moved to the same date. Any date gives the same answer. The convenient one is usually the date of the unknown payment, because it then needs no factor.

Suppose a borrower owes $3,000 in one year and $4,000 in three years, and the lender agrees to take one payment XXX at year 2 instead, at 5% a year. At year 2 the first debt is a year overdue, so it is compounded once; the second is a year early, so it is discounted once: X=3,000×1.05+4,000÷1.05=3,150+3,809.52=6,959.52X = 3{,}000 \times 1.05 + 4{,}000 \div 1.05 = 3{,}150 + 3{,}809.52 = 6{,}959.52X=3,000×1.05+4,000÷1.05=3,150+3,809.52=6,959.52. Written at time 0 instead, the equation is X÷1.052=3,000÷1.05+4,000÷1.053X \div 1.05^2 = 3{,}000 \div 1.05 + 4{,}000 \div 1.05^3X÷1.052=3,000÷1.05+4,000÷1.053, which gives the same $6,959.52. Never add the two debts as they stand: $7,000 mixes dollars from different dates.

Amounts due between compounding dates

Nothing in the formula requires nnn to be a whole number. An amount due in 7 months, discounted at 6% compounded quarterly, is 7÷3=2137 \div 3 = 2\tfrac{1}{3}7÷3=231​ quarters away, so its discount factor is 1.015−7/3=0.9658561.015^{-7/3} = 0.9658561.015−7/3=0.965856. The same factor comes from the effective annual rate, 1.0154−1=6.1364%1.015^4 - 1 = 6.1364\%1.0154−1=6.1364%, applied for 7/12 of a year, because (1.0154)7/12=1.0157/3(1.015^4)^{7/12} = 1.015^{7/3}(1.0154)7/12=1.0157/3.

In practice lenders do not always compound over part of a period. Some charge simple interest for the fraction, and the day-count convention (actual/365, 30/360 and others) varies by market and by contract, as the lesson on simple and compound interest explains. When a contract fixes the convention, follow it to the cent. For valuing and comparing amounts, the fractional exponent is the standard approach and the one this course uses.

When the rate changes over time

The single-rate formula assumes the same rate in every period. Often the rate changes: a bank resets a variable savings rate, and markets charge different rates for different horizons. The rule generalises directly. An amount is multiplied by the growth factor of each period it passes through, whatever that period’s rate. Over periods with rates r1,r2,…,rnr_1, r_2, \ldots, r_nr1​,r2​,…,rn​, the future value is PV(1+r1)(1+r2)⋯(1+rn)\text{PV}(1 + r_1)(1 + r_2)\cdots(1 + r_n)PV(1+r1​)(1+r2​)⋯(1+rn​), and a present value divides by the same product.

The Sandpiper Online savings account Ana plans to use shows why it matters. Its variable rate was 3.2% compounded monthly in 2026 and is now 3.6% (both fictional). A fictional saver who left $10,000 in an account like it for six months at 3.2% and then six months at 3.6% would end the year with 10,000×(1+0.032/12)6×(1.003)6=10,345.3510{,}000 \times (1 + 0.032/12)^6 \times (1.003)^6 = 10{,}345.3510,000×(1+0.032/12)6×(1.003)6=10,345.35 dollars. No single quoted rate describes that year; the right summary is the constant rate that gives the same product of factors.

That constant rate is a geometric mean: [(1+r1)(1+r2)⋯(1+rn)]1/n−1\big[(1 + r_1)(1 + r_2)\cdots(1 + r_n)\big]^{1/n} - 1[(1+r1​)(1+r2​)⋯(1+rn​)]1/n−1. For a three-year deposit paying 4%, 5% and 6%, the growth factor is 1.04×1.05×1.06=1.157521.04 \times 1.05 \times 1.06 = 1.157521.04×1.05×1.06=1.15752, and the equivalent constant rate is 1.157521/3−1=4.9968%1.15752^{1/3} - 1 = 4.9968\%1.157521/3−1=4.9968%, a shade below the simple average of 5%. When interest rates move gently the gap is tiny. When returns swing, it is large: a fund that gains 20% and then loses 10% averages 5% a year arithmetically, but $1,000 becomes 1,000×1.2×0.9=1,0801{,}000 \times 1.2 \times 0.9 = 1{,}0801,000×1.2×0.9=1,080 dollars, the same as 3.923% a year for two years. Growth factors multiply; rates do not add.

Markets often quote a different rate for each horizon: one rate for money borrowed for a year, another for five years. Discounting each amount at the rate for its own horizon is the same idea, and later courses on bonds develop it.

The calculator mindset

Financial calculators and spreadsheets solve the same equation. A financial calculator has keys for the number of periods (N), the rate per period (I/Y, entered as a percentage), the present value (PV), a level payment (PMT) and the future value (FV): enter any four and compute the fifth. For a single amount the payment is zero. Calculators also have settings for payments and compounding periods a year; this course always works with the rate per period, so set both to 1 or follow your model’s manual.

Two conventions catch people out. Calculators and spreadsheets treat money you receive as positive and money you pay as negative, so the present value and the future value of one deal carry opposite signs; enter both as positive when solving for a rate or a time and you get an error. In a spreadsheet, =PV(0.02, 4, 0, 10000) returns −9,238.45: the amount you would pay today to receive $10,000 after four quarters. =FV(rate, nper, pmt, pv), =RATE(nper, pmt, pv, fv) and =NPER(rate, pmt, pv, fv) solve for the other unknowns, with pmt set to 0. Whatever the tool, the rate must be the rate per period that matches nper.

How sensitive is a present value to the rate?

The further away an amount is, the more its present value moves when the rate changes. Raise the rate from 5% to 6% a year and the present value of $1,000 due in one year falls from $952.38 to $943.40, by 0.94%. Due in ten years, it falls from $613.91 to $558.39, by 9.04%. Due in thirty years, from $231.38 to $174.11, by 24.75%. The fall grows with the wait, almost in proportion over short waits and a little less over long ones. This is why the values of long-term bonds, pension promises and distant business cash flows swing so much more with interest rates than the values of near ones.

Common mistakes

  • Mismatching the rate and the periods

    With 6% compounded monthly, the rate per period is 0.5% and a three-year wait is 36 periods. Using 6% for 36 periods, or 0.5% for 3 periods, gives nonsense. Write r and n in the same unit before you calculate.

  • Rounding the discount factor too early

    The present value of $15,000 due in three years at 9% is 15,000÷1.093=11,582.7515{,}000 \div 1.09^3 = 11{,}582.7515,000÷1.093=11,582.75. Rounding the factor 1÷1.093=0.7721831 \div 1.09^3 = 0.7721831÷1.093=0.772183 to 0.77 gives $11,550.00, and even 0.7722 gives $11,583.00. Published answers sometimes carry slips of this kind. Keep at least six significant figures in every factor and round only the final amount.

  • Adding amounts from different dates

    A debt of $3,000 in one year plus $4,000 in three years is not “$7,000”. Move every amount to one comparison date first; then add.

  • Averaging changing rates

    Rates of 4%, 5% and 6% over three years do not grow money at 5% a year: multiply the growth factors (1.04 × 1.05 × 1.06) and take the cube root, which gives 4.9968%. With returns that swing, such as +20% and −10%, the arithmetic average (5%) overstates the true rate (3.923%) badly.

  • Subtracting the interest instead of dividing

    Taking 9% × 3 = 27% off $15,000 gives $10,950, and dividing by 1 + 0.27 gives $11,811.02. Neither is a compound present value. Discounting divides by (1 + r) once for every period: 15,000 ÷ 1.09³.

  • Entering PV and FV with the same sign

    Calculators and spreadsheets treat money paid as negative and money received as positive. Solving for a rate or a time with both entered as positive returns an error; give the amount you pay a minus sign.

In practice

Present values of single amounts sit behind many business decisions, often without anyone using the words.

“Buy now, pay in a year.” A fictional furniture retailer advertises a $1,200 sofa with nothing to pay for twelve months. Its finance manager treats the offer as a price cut. Money costs the retailer about 9% a year (a fictional figure), so $1,200 received in a year is worth 1,200÷1.09=1,100.921{,}200 \div 1.09 = 1{,}100.921,200÷1.09=1,100.92 dollars today. The promotion gives away $99.08 a sofa, 8.26% of the price, before allowing for customers who pay late or not at all. A straight 5% discount would cost $60. On these numbers the “free credit” is the dearer promotion, and the manager can say by how much.

Settling a claim. A fictional engineering firm owes a client $250,000 under a court judgment payable in three years, and offers $205,000 now to settle. The client’s adviser discounts at 6%, the return the client could earn on similarly safe investments: 250,000÷1.063=209,904.82250{,}000 \div 1.06^3 = 209{,}904.82250,000÷1.063=209,904.82 dollars. The offer is about $4,905 short, so the client asks for $210,000. If there were a real chance the firm would not survive three years, the adviser would discount at a higher rate, as F00 explains for risky amounts, and $205,000 now would start to look attractive.

Rates that move. A treasurer who holds cash for a known future payment, such as a tax bill or a loan repayment, often rolls it over in short deposits. Each rollover earns whatever rate is then on offer, so the amount needed today is the payment divided by the product of the growth factors she expects, not by one rate raised to a power. When she cannot afford to come up short, she may lock in a single rate for the whole period instead, giving up any gain if rates rise in exchange for certainty.

Fictional case

Ana runs the same arithmetic on her own plan. She hopes to put down a $60,000 deposit on a flat in April 2032, five years away, and the Sandpiper Online account she will save in now pays 3.6% a year compounded monthly, 0.3% a month. Summary

  • FV = PV(1 + r)ⁿ and PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ are one equation linking four quantities; know three and you can find the fourth. The rate and the periods must match.
  • Draw a timeline: time 0 at the valuation date, each amount at its own date, periods matching the compounding.
  • A discount factor, 1 ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ, is the present value of $1. It falls as the rate or the wait increases, so present values fall too.
  • An amount X at period j is worth X(1 + r)^(k − j) at period k. Compare or add amounts only at one comparison date; with one rate, the ranking of options does not depend on which date you choose.
  • An equation of value sets old and new payments equal at a comparison date, which is how debts are rescheduled.
  • With changing rates, multiply the growth factors period by period; the equivalent constant rate is a geometric mean, below the arithmetic average.
  • Solve for the rate with a root and for the time with logarithms, or with RATE and NPER (pmt = 0), giving PV and FV opposite signs. Check every answer by substituting it back.
  • Distant amounts are far more sensitive to the rate: a rise from 5% to 6% cuts the value of $1,000 due in 30 years by about a quarter.

Key formulas

  • Future value of a single amount
  • Present value and the discount factor
  • Rate per period and number of periods from a quoted annual rate
  • Value at any date (moving an amount)
  • Changing rates and the equivalent constant rate
  • Solving for the rate or the number of periods

Terms introduced

Cash-flow timeline
A line marked off in equal periods, with time 0 at the valuation date and each cash flow written at the date it occurs, used to set up time-value problems.
Discount factor
The present value of $1 received after n periods at a rate r per period: 1 ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ. Multiplying an amount by it gives the amount’s present value.
Comparison date
The single date to which amounts due at different dates are moved, by compounding or discounting, before they are compared or added. Also called the focal date.
Equation of value
An equation stating that two sets of payments are worth the same at a given rate, written by moving every payment to one comparison date. Used to reschedule debts and to solve for an unknown payment or date.
