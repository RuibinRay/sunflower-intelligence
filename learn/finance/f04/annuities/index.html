Annuities

Loan instalments, rent, leases, pensions and savings plans are all streams of equal payments. Learn to value such a stream in one step, whether the payments come at the end or the start of each period, start now or later, stay level or grow, and to solve for the payment, the number of payments or the rate.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate the present and future value of an ordinary annuity and of an annuity due, and explain why they differ by a factor of (1 + r).
  • Calculate the value of a deferred annuity and of a growing annuity by moving the stream to a common date.
  • Calculate the payment or the number of payments of an annuity, using logarithms for the number of payments.
  • Find the interest rate of an annuity by trial and error or with a spreadsheet’s RATE function.
  • Evaluate an instalment plan sold as “0% interest” by finding its present value and its implied interest rate.

Review first

  • Present and future value — Every annuity formula is a shortcut for discounting or compounding single sums, and you need to move an amount from one date to another.
  • Discounting cash flows — An annuity is a cash-flow stream whose payments happen to be equal: discounting each payment and adding is the definition behind every formula here.
  • Effective annual rates — Payments are often monthly while rates are quoted a year at a time; you need the rate per period that matches the payment period.
  • Powers, negative powers and the natural logarithm (ln) on a calculator.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

March 2027. Lantern Coffee Co. wants a cold-brew brewing system that costs $24,000. The supplier offers its own instalment plan: 24 monthly payments of $1,050, the first one month after delivery, advertised as “0% interest”. Coastline Bank, Lantern’s bank, would instead lend the $24,000 at 7.2% a year compounded monthly. Ana adds up the instalments: 24×1,050=25,20024 \times 1{,}050 = 25{,}20024×1,050=25,200 dollars, which is $1,200 more than the cash price. So is the plan free, as the advertisement says, or does it cost $1,200? Neither answer is right, because the 24 payments are spread over two years, and a dollar paid in two years is worth less than a dollar paid today. To compare the plan with the cash price, Ana needs the value today of 24 equal payments: the value of an annuity.

Streams of equal payments

An annuity is a series of equal payments made at equal intervals for a fixed number of periods. Loan instalments, rent, equipment leases, insurance premiums, pension payments and a savings plan that puts the same amount aside every month are all annuities. So are the 24 payments of $1,050 in the supplier’s plan.

In everyday speech an annuity is also an insurance product: a contract with an insurance company that pays an income, usually in retirement. Those products are built from the same mathematics, but in this course the word means any level stream of payments, whoever pays it.

When the payments fall within each period matters, so there are two basic kinds:

Ordinary annuity
Each payment is made at the end of its period, so the first payment comes one period from now. Loan repayments and the supplier’s plan work this way. This is the default: “annuity” on its own means an ordinary annuity.
Annuity due
Each payment is made at the start of its period, so the first payment is made today. Rent, leases and insurance premiums are usually paid in advance and work this way.

Four numbers describe an annuity: the payment per period CCC, the number of payments nnn, the interest rate per period rrr, and the timing (end or start). The rate and the payments must refer to the same period. For monthly payments with a rate quoted as an APR compounded monthly, the monthly rate is the APR divided by 12; Coastline’s 7.2% a year is 0.6% a month.

Valuing an annuity one payment at a time

You already know how to value a stream of payments: discount each payment to today and add up the results. An annuity is no different. Take $2,000 at the end of each year for five years, valued at 6% a year. Each payment is multiplied by its discount factor, 1÷1.06t1 \div 1.06^t1÷1.06t, where ttt is the year in which it is paid.

The five payments add up to $10,000, but they are worth $8,424.73 today: the later the payment, the less it is worth now. Look at the total of the discount factors, 4.212364. Because every payment is the same, the present value is simply the payment times that total. The total is called the annuity factor, and there is a formula for it, so you never need the table:

Payments at the start: the annuity due

If the same five payments of $2,000 were made at the start of each year instead of the end, each would arrive one year sooner and so be worth 6% more in today’s money. The whole annuity due is therefore worth 8,424.73×1.06≈8,930.218{,}424.73 \times 1.06 \approx 8{,}930.218,424.73×1.06≈8,930.21 dollars. The rule is general: an annuity due is worth an ordinary annuity times (1+r)(1 + r)(1+r), for present values and for future values alike. The difference is one period’s interest on the whole stream, not a different formula.

Looking forward: the future value of an annuity

A savings plan asks the opposite question: if you deposit the same amount every period, how much will you have at the end? Suppose Lantern sets aside $400 at the end of every month for three years, in a business savings account paying 3% a year compounded monthly (0.25% a month), to replace its espresso machine. Each deposit grows until the end of the 36 months; the first grows for 35 months and the last not at all. Adding them up gives the future value of the annuity:

FV=C×(1+r)n−1r=400×1.002536−10.0025=400×37.620560≈15,048.22\text{FV} = C \times \frac{(1 + r)^{n} - 1}{r} = 400 \times \frac{1.0025^{36} - 1}{0.0025} = 400 \times 37.620560 \approx 15{,}048.22FV=C×r(1+r)n−1​=400×0.00251.002536−1​=400×37.620560≈15,048.22

Lantern deposits 36×400=14,40036 \times 400 = 14{,}40036×400=14,400 dollars and the account adds $648.22 of interest. (The account, the rate and the plan are fictional.) The future value is the present value moved forward nnn periods: FV=PV×(1+r)n\text{FV} = \text{PV} \times (1 + r)^nFV=PV×(1+r)n, so once you know one, you know the other.

Is the supplier’s “0% interest” plan free?

Now Ana can value the plan. If Lantern pays cash, it must find $24,000 now, and the obvious place to find it is Coastline Bank at 7.2% a year compounded monthly. So she values the 24 payments at 0.6% a month, the rate at which Lantern can move money through time:

PV=1,050×1−1.006−240.006=1,050×22.289933≈23,404.43\text{PV} = 1{,}050 \times \frac{1 - 1.006^{-24}}{0.006} = 1{,}050 \times 22.289933 \approx 23{,}404.43PV=1,050×0.0061−1.006−24​=1,050×22.289933≈23,404.43

At Lantern’s 7.2% borrowing rate, the plan costs the same as paying $23,404.43 today: about $595.57 less than the $24,000 cash price. Spreading the payments is worth more than the extra $1,200 they add up to. But the plan is not free either. A plan that really charged 0% would ask for 24,000÷24=1,00024{,}000 \div 24 = 1{,}00024,000÷24=1,000 dollars a month. The extra $50 a month is interest by another name. The rate it implies is the rate at which the 24 payments are worth exactly $24,000; the theory section finds it, about 0.394% a month, an APR of about 4.73%. That is cheaper than Coastline’s 7.2%, which is why the plan wins at that rate.

Key idea: Match the rate to the payment period

Every annuity formula works in periods, not years. Monthly payments need a monthly rate and the number of months; quarterly payments need a quarterly rate and the number of quarters. With a rate quoted as an APR compounded monthly, divide the APR by 12. If the compounding period differs from the payment period, first convert the rate to the equivalent rate per payment period, as in the effective-annual-rates lesson.

In depth

Why the formula works

The present value of an ordinary annuity is a sum of nnn discounted payments, each one (1+r)(1 + r)(1+r) times smaller than the one before: a geometric series. Multiply the sum by (1+r)(1 + r)(1+r) and every term moves one period closer; subtract the original and all but two terms cancel:

PV=C1+r+C(1+r)2+⋯+C(1+r)n⟹(1+r) PV−PV=C−C(1+r)n\text{PV} = \frac{C}{1 + r} + \frac{C}{(1 + r)^2} + \cdots + \frac{C}{(1 + r)^n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (1 + r)\,\text{PV} - \text{PV} = C - \frac{C}{(1 + r)^{n}}PV=1+rC​+(1+r)2C​+⋯+(1+r)nC​⟹(1+r)PV−PV=C−(1+r)nC​

The left side is r×PVr \times \text{PV}r×PV, so PV=C [1−(1+r)−n]÷r\text{PV} = C\,[1 - (1 + r)^{-n}] \div rPV=C[1−(1+r)−n]÷r. A second way to see it uses the next lesson, perpetuities-and-growth: a payment of CCC every period forever is worth C÷rC \div rC÷r. An annuity is a perpetuity that stops after nnn payments, so it is worth a perpetuity starting now minus a perpetuity whose payments start after period nnn, which is worth (C÷r)(C \div r)(C÷r) discounted by (1+r)n(1 + r)^n(1+r)n. For the five payments of $2,000: 33,333.33−24,908.61=8,424.7333{,}333.33 - 24{,}908.61 = 8{,}424.7333,333.33−24,908.61=8,424.73 dollars.

The future-value formula is the present value moved forward: multiply by (1+r)n(1 + r)^n(1+r)n and you get C [(1+r)n−1]÷rC\,[(1 + r)^n - 1] \div rC[(1+r)n−1]÷r. If the rate is zero both formulas break down (they divide by zero), but then there is nothing to discount: the value is just n×Cn \times Cn×C.

Ordinary annuity or annuity due?

Moving every payment one period earlier multiplies each present value, and each future value, by (1+r)(1 + r)(1+r), so the conversion is a single multiplication. You can also think of an annuity due as one payment now plus an ordinary annuity of n−1n - 1n−1 payments: C+C [1−(1+r)−(n−1)]÷rC + C\,[1 - (1 + r)^{-(n-1)}] \div rC+C[1−(1+r)−(n−1)]÷r, which gives the same number. Read the contract to decide which you have. A loan’s first payment is normally due a period after the money is lent: an ordinary annuity. A lease or a rent is normally paid in advance: an annuity due. Spreadsheets and financial calculators handle both with a timing switch (described below).

Deferred annuities

A deferred annuity starts later than one period from now. University fees that begin in ten years, or a pension that starts at 65, are examples. Value it in two moves. First apply the annuity formula: it gives the value one period before the first payment. Then discount that single amount back to today. If the first payment comes at the end of period k+1k + 1k+1, the annuity formula values the stream at time kkk, so you discount by kkk periods, not k+1k + 1k+1:

PV0=1(1+r)k×C×1−(1+r)−nr\text{PV}_0 = \frac{1}{(1 + r)^{k}} \times C \times \frac{1 - (1 + r)^{-n}}{r}PV0​=(1+r)k1​×C×r1−(1+r)−n​

A check that never fails: the deferred annuity is worth an annuity of k+nk + nk+n payments minus an annuity of the first kkk payments, which never happen. Worked example 2 uses both routes.

Growing annuities

Some streams rise at a steady rate: a supply contract with a price increase each year, or a salary that rises with inflation. A growing annuity pays C1C_1C1​ at the end of the first period, then grows at ggg per period, so the payment in period ttt is C1(1+g)t−1C_1(1 + g)^{t-1}C1​(1+g)t−1. The same geometric-series argument gives:

PV=C1r−g[1−(1+g1+r)n]\text{PV} = \frac{C_1}{r - g}\left[1 - \left(\frac{1 + g}{1 + r}\right)^{n}\right]PV=r−gC1​​[1−(1+r1+g​)n]

Note that C1C_1C1​ is the first payment, not today’s amount. The formula works whether ggg is below or above rrr; it fails only when they are equal, and then each payment is worth exactly C1÷(1+r)C_1 \div (1 + r)C1​÷(1+r) today, so the present value is n×C1÷(1+r)n \times C_1 \div (1 + r)n×C1​÷(1+r). Solving for the payment

Rearranging the present-value formula gives the payment that a present amount will support: C=PV×r÷[1−(1+r)−n]C = \text{PV} \times r \div [1 - (1 + r)^{-n}]C=PV×r÷[1−(1+r)−n]. This is the loan payment: a lender who hands over PV\text{PV}PV today and wants its money back with interest at rrr needs payments of CCC. The loan-amortisation lesson builds on it. Rearranging the future-value formula gives the deposit needed to reach a target, C=FV×r÷[(1+r)n−1]C = \text{FV} \times r \div [(1 + r)^n - 1]C=FV×r÷[(1+r)n−1]; a fund built this way is called a sinking fund. For example, to have $20,000 in four years at 3% a year compounded monthly, deposit 20,000×0.0025÷(1.002548−1)≈392.6920{,}000 \times 0.0025 \div (1.0025^{48} - 1) \approx 392.6920,000×0.0025÷(1.002548−1)≈392.69 dollars at the end of each month (a fictional target).

Solving for the number of payments

How many payments of CCC will repay an amount PV\text{PV}PV? Rearrange the formula until the power stands alone, (1+r)−n=1−PV×r÷C(1 + r)^{-n} = 1 - \text{PV} \times r \div C(1+r)−n=1−PV×r÷C, then take natural logarithms of both sides. A logarithm turns a power into a multiplication, ln⁡(xn)=nln⁡x\ln(x^n) = n \ln xln(xn)=nlnx, which is what lets you solve for nnn:

n=−ln⁡(1−PV×rC)ln⁡(1+r)n = \frac{-\ln\left(1 - \dfrac{\text{PV} \times r}{C}\right)}{\ln(1 + r)}n=ln(1+r)−ln(1−CPV×r​)​

Take a fictional $10,000 loan at 9% a year compounded monthly (0.75% a month) repaid at $500 a month: n=−ln⁡(1−10,000×0.0075÷500)÷ln⁡1.0075=−ln⁡0.85÷ln⁡1.0075≈21.75n = -\ln(1 - 10{,}000 \times 0.0075 \div 500) \div \ln 1.0075 = -\ln 0.85 \div \ln 1.0075 \approx 21.75n=−ln(1−10,000×0.0075÷500)÷ln1.0075=−ln0.85÷ln1.0075≈21.75. The answer is rarely a whole number. In practice the borrower makes 21 full payments of $500 and a smaller 22nd payment that clears what is left, about $375.52 here (the balance after 21 payments, $372.73, plus a month’s interest). The formula has a condition: the payment must be larger than the interest on the amount, C>PV×rC > \text{PV} \times rC>PV×r. At $75 a month the payments would only cover the interest and the debt would never be repaid; below $75 it would grow. For a savings target the matching formula is n=ln⁡(1+FV×r÷C)÷ln⁡(1+r)n = \ln(1 + \text{FV} \times r \div C) \div \ln(1 + r)n=ln(1+FV×r÷C)÷ln(1+r).

Solving for the rate

There is no formula that isolates rrr in the annuity equation once there are more than a couple of payments. The rate is found by trial and error: guess a rate, value the payments, and adjust the guess until the value matches the price. Higher rates give lower values, so each trial tells you which way to move. Here is the search for the rate in the supplier’s plan, where 24 payments of $1,050 must be worth $24,000:

Halving the gap between a guess that is too high and one that is too low (bisection) closes in on the answer in a few dozen steps; a computer does it instantly. The plan’s implied monthly rate is about 0.394%, an APR of about 4.73%, and an effective annual rate of about 4.83% (1.0039412−11.00394^{12} - 11.0039412−1). This is the plan’s implied interest rate: the rate the “0%” plan really charges, given that a buyer paying cash would pay $24,000.

Spreadsheets do the search for you. In Excel and most other spreadsheets, =RATE(24, -1050, 24000) returns the rate per period, about 0.394%; multiply by 12 for the APR. Microsoft’s documentation says RATE is calculated by iteration and can have zero or more solutions, and that it returns an error if it fails to settle on an answer after 20 tries, in which case you can supply a guess. The companion functions PV, FV, PMT and NPER solve for the other unknowns.

Note: Spreadsheet and calculator conventions

Spreadsheet functions follow a sign convention: money you receive is positive and money you pay is negative. A loan you receive (+24,000) is repaid by payments you make (−1,050). If both are entered with the same sign, no rate can make them balance and RATE returns an error. The optional type argument sets the timing: 0 or omitted for payments at the end of each period (an ordinary annuity), 1 for payments at the start (an annuity due). The rate argument and the number of periods must use the same period: for monthly payments, a monthly rate and the number of months. Financial calculators use the same ideas with keys usually labelled N, I/Y, PV, PMT and FV, and a BEGIN/END switch.

Assumptions and limits

  • The payments are equal (or, for a growing annuity, grow at a constant rate) and fall at regular intervals.
  • One interest rate applies to every period. If rates are expected to change, discount each payment at its own rate, as in the present-and-future-value lesson.
  • The rate period matches the payment period. Monthly payments with quarterly compounding, for example, need the rate converted first.
  • The value depends on the rate chosen. A present value at 7.2% answers “what is this worth to someone who can borrow or lend at 7.2%?”; a different rate answers a different question.
  • The payments are certain. Risky payments are discounted at a higher rate, a topic for F05 Corporate Finance.

Common mistakes

  • Mixing an annual rate with monthly payments

    With monthly payments you need the monthly rate and the number of months: 0.6% and 24 for the supplier’s plan, not 7.2% and 24, or 0.6% and 2. Using the annual rate with 24 payments values the plan at about $11,834; using 0.6% with two payments values it at about $2,081. If a result looks absurd, check the periods first.

  • Using the ordinary formula for payments made in advance

    Rent, leases and premiums are usually paid at the start of each period. Valuing them as an ordinary annuity understates their present value by one period’s interest. Read the timing from the contract and multiply by (1 + r) for an annuity due.

  • Discounting a deferred annuity one period too far

    The annuity formula already values the stream one period before the first payment. If the first payment is in year 10, the formula gives a value at year 9, so discount by nine years, not ten. Check with the difference of two annuities.

  • Adding up the payments

    “The plan costs $25,200” treats a dollar in two years as worth a dollar today. Payments at different dates can be compared only after moving them to the same date. The sum of the payments is useful for one thing only: the gap between it and the price (or the amount borrowed) is the total interest, in undiscounted dollars.

  • Believing “0%” when there is a cash price

    If a buyer who pays cash pays less than the instalments add up to, the plan charges interest whatever the advertisement says. Find the implied rate from the cash price, the payment and the number of payments, and compare it with what borrowing would cost.

  • Entering everything as positive in a spreadsheet

    RATE, NPER and PV need the sign convention: money received positive, money paid negative. =RATE(24, 1050, 24000) has no answer, because it describes receiving both the $24,000 and the payments. Write =RATE(24, -1050, 24000).

In practice

Annuity arithmetic sits behind every decision that swaps a lump sum for a stream of payments, or a stream for a lump sum.

Leasing or buying equipment. The finance manager of a fictional battery-storage installer must choose between buying a $90,000 crane truck and leasing it for $2,100 a month, paid in advance, for four years. She values the lease payments as an annuity due at the rate the company would pay to borrow the purchase price, then adds what the company would lose by not owning the truck at the end of the lease (its expected resale value, discounted to today). The cheaper option in present value wins, unless the lease brings servicing or flexibility worth paying for. Adding up the 48 lease payments ($100,800) and comparing them with $90,000 would answer the wrong question.

Lump sum or instalments. A fictional court settlement offers an injured cyclist either $400,000 now or $30,000 a year for 20 years. At 6% the instalments are worth about $344,097.64, so the lump sum is worth more to anyone who can invest at 6%; at 4% they are worth about $407,709.79 and the instalments win. The decision turns on the rate, so a careful adviser shows the value at several rates and asks how safe the payer is. Lottery prizes and pension options pose the same question.

Saving for a known cost. A fictional hotel knows it must refurbish its rooms in five years at a cost of about $250,000. Rather than borrow at the last minute, its owners set up a sinking fund: equal monthly deposits that, with interest, reach the target on time. The payment formula tells them the deposit, and the future-value formula lets them check each year whether the fund is on track.

Summary

  • An annuity is a series of equal payments at equal intervals. In an ordinary annuity each payment is at the end of its period; in an annuity due, at the start.
  • PV = C × [1 − (1 + r)^−n] ÷ r and FV = C × [(1 + r)^n − 1] ÷ r, with r and n in the same period as the payments. FV = PV × (1 + r)^n.
  • An annuity due is worth an ordinary annuity × (1 + r). A deferred annuity is valued with the annuity formula one period before its first payment, then discounted to today.
  • A growing annuity is worth C₁ ÷ (r − g) × [1 − ((1 + g) ÷ (1 + r))^n], where C₁ is the first payment.
  • Rearranging gives the payment; logarithms give the number of payments; the rate needs trial and error or a spreadsheet’s RATE function.
  • The supplier’s 24 × $1,050 plan is worth $23,404.43 at 7.2%, $595.57 less than $24,000 in cash, but it is not free: its implied rate is about 4.73% a year (APR).

Terms introduced

Annuity
A series of equal payments made at equal intervals for a fixed number of periods, such as loan instalments, rent or a regular savings deposit. Also the name of an insurance product that pays an income.
Ordinary annuity
An annuity whose payments are made at the end of each period, so the first payment comes one period from now. The default meaning of “annuity”.
Annuity due
An annuity whose payments are made at the start of each period, so the first payment is made now. Worth an ordinary annuity × (1 + r).
Annuity factor
The present value of $1 a period for n periods at rate r: [1 − (1 + r)^−n] ÷ r. Multiplied by the payment, it gives the present value of an ordinary annuity.
Deferred annuity
An annuity whose first payment comes later than one period from now. Valued with the annuity formula one period before the first payment, then discounted to today.
Growing annuity
A stream of payments for a fixed number of periods in which each payment is a constant percentage larger than the one before.
Implied interest rate
The interest rate at which the present value of a stream of payments equals the amount borrowed or the cash price: the rate a credit or instalment arrangement really charges.
