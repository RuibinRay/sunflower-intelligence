Inflation and real returns

A dollar next year is not the same unit as a dollar today. Measure inflation over several years, convert amounts into today’s dollars, use the exact Fisher relation, and discount nominal cash flows at nominal rates and real cash flows at real rates, so that every comparison across time is made in one kind of dollar.

About 85 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate yearly, cumulative and average annual inflation from a price index over several years.
  • Convert a nominal amount at any date into real dollars of a chosen base date, and back again.
  • Apply the exact Fisher relation to find a real rate, a nominal rate or an inflation rate, using effective rates for the same period.
  • Calculate the present value of a cash-flow stream both in nominal terms and in real terms, and explain why consistent discounting gives the same answer.
  • Calculate the real growth of a salary or a price, and explain how Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) keep their purchasing power.

Review first

  • Interest and inflation — It introduced inflation, the CPI, purchasing power and the exact real return; this lesson extends them to several years and to whole cash-flow streams.
  • Present and future value — You need discount factors and the idea of moving amounts to a common date.
  • Discounting cash flows — You will value an uneven stream of cash flows by discounting each one.
  • Annuities — The growing annuity gives a quick check on the value of a payment that rises with inflation.

The idea

Fictional case

March 2027. Lantern Coffee Co. is a year old, and a law firm in the city (fictional, like everything in the case) wants Lantern to run its office coffee service for three years. It offers two ways to pay, each fee paid at the end of each contract year: a fixed $42,000 a year, or $40,000 a year in March 2027 dollars, raised every year in line with the Consumer Price Index. Ana still assumes, as she did in F00, that prices will rise by about 2.8% a year, and she wants Lantern’s contracts to earn 10% a year, the return she judges fair for the risk of the business. The indexed fee starts lower but grows. Which is worth more today? Answering means comparing dollars paid in different years when each year’s dollar buys a little less than the last.

Two kinds of dollars

In F00 you met inflation as a sustained rise in prices and saw that it reduces the purchasing power of money. Over one year that was a single division. Over several years, and with cash flowing in and out at different dates, it pays to be explicit about which kind of dollar each number is measured in.

Nominal amount
An amount in the dollars of the date it is actually paid or received: the number on the cheque. Also called current dollars. Prices, wages, loan payments and bank balances are all quoted in nominal terms.
Real amount
The same amount restated in the purchasing power of a chosen base period, by dividing out the inflation between the base and the date of payment. Also called constant dollars, or “today’s dollars” when the base is today.

A real amount answers the question “what would this buy at base-period prices?” Lantern’s indexed fee is set in real terms: $40,000 of March 2027 purchasing power every year. The fixed fee is set in nominal terms: $42,000 in each year’s dollars, which buy less as time passes. Neither form is wrong; mixing them without converting is.

Restating amounts in one kind of dollar

To restate a nominal amount paid at date ttt in dollars of a base date, multiply by the ratio of the index at the base to the index at ttt. Suppose a fictional technician earned $45,000 in Year 0 and $52,000 in Year 5 on the index above. In Year 0 dollars, the Year 5 salary is 52,000×100.0÷122.9≈42,310.8252{,}000 \times 100.0 \div 122.9 \approx 42{,}310.8252,000×100.0÷122.9≈42,310.82 dollars. Her pay rose 15.56% in dollars, but in purchasing power it fell about 5.98%. To have kept pace with prices she would have needed 45,000×1.229=55,30545{,}000 \times 1.229 = 55{,}30545,000×1.229=55,305 dollars in Year 5.

The same ratio works in either direction, and the base can be any date: today, the start of a contract, or a year you want to compare against. What matters is that every amount in one calculation is measured against the same base.

Nominal rates, real rates and the Fisher relation

F00 gave the exact link between a nominal return, inflation iii and a real return. This course calls it the Fisher relation: 1+rnom=(1+rreal)(1+i)1 + r_{\text{nom}} = (1 + r_{\text{real}})(1 + i)1+rnom​=(1+rreal​)(1+i). It can be solved for whichever rate is unknown. Ana wants Lantern’s contracts to earn 10% a year in nominal terms; with 2.8% inflation that is a real return of 1.10÷1.028−1≈7.00%1.10 \div 1.028 - 1 \approx 7.00\%1.10÷1.028−1≈7.00%. Run the other way, an investor who wants 7% real when inflation is 2.8% needs 1.07×1.028−1≈10.00%1.07 \times 1.028 - 1 \approx 10.00\%1.07×1.028−1≈10.00% nominal.

Key idea: Discount like with like

Nominal cash flows are discounted at a nominal rate; real cash flows are discounted at a real rate. Done consistently, the two routes give exactly the same present value, because inflation is divided out of both the numerator and the denominator. Mixing them, real flows with a nominal rate or nominal flows with a real rate, is one of the most common and most expensive errors in valuation.

For Lantern’s indexed offer, the nominal route grows the fee with expected inflation, 40,000×1.028t40{,}000 \times 1.028^t40,000×1.028t in year ttt, and discounts at 10%. The real route keeps the fee at $40,000 and discounts at the real rate of about 7.00%. Both give about $104,965.18. The fixed $42,000, already nominal, is worth 42,000×2.486852≈104,447.7842{,}000 \times 2.486852 \approx 104{,}447.7842,000×2.486852≈104,447.78 at 10%. On Ana’s inflation assumption the indexed offer is worth about $517.40 more today, and it also protects Lantern if inflation turns out higher. Worked example 2 sets out every step.

In depth

Price indexes over time

Write CPIt\text{CPI}_tCPIt​ for the index level at date ttt. Inflation over any interval is the ratio of the index levels minus one, however many years the interval spans. Because the ratio over a long interval is the product of the ratios over the shorter intervals inside it, yearly rates compound: (1+i1)(1+i2)⋯(1+in)=CPIn÷CPI0(1 + i_1)(1 + i_2)\cdots(1 + i_n) = \text{CPI}_n \div \text{CPI}_0(1+i1​)(1+i2​)⋯(1+in​)=CPIn​÷CPI0​. The average annual rate is the nnnth root of that product minus one, a geometric average, for the same reason that the average growth rate of an investment is.

Monthly figures annualise the same way. If an index rises 0.3% in a month, the annualised rate is 1.00312−1≈3.66%1.003^{12} - 1 \approx 3.66\%1.00312−1≈3.66%, not 12×0.3%=3.6%12 \times 0.3\% = 3.6\%12×0.3%=3.6%. Over a full year, the year-on-year change in the index (this month’s level against the same month a year earlier) is usually the more reliable figure, because single months are noisy.

A price index is an average over a basket, so it is a measure, not a law of nature. The CPI tracks what urban consumers buy; a business’s own costs, such as coffee beans or rent, may rise faster or slower. When a contract is linked to an index, the parties must name the exact series. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics, which publishes the CPI, recommends the US City Average index for escalation clauses and calls seasonally adjusted figures inappropriate for them; seasonally adjusted data are also revised for up to five years after release.

The Fisher relation in full

The exact relation 1+rnom=(1+rreal)(1+i)1 + r_{\text{nom}} = (1 + r_{\text{real}})(1 + i)1+rnom​=(1+rreal​)(1+i) holds over any period, provided all three rates are measured over the same period and compound in the same way. Two consequences follow.

  • Use effective rates. A savings account quoted at 4.5% a year compounded monthly has an effective annual rate of (1+0.045/12)12−1≈4.594%(1 + 0.045/12)^{12} - 1 \approx 4.594\%(1+0.045/12)12−1≈4.594%. Its real return at 2.8% inflation is 1.04594÷1.028−1≈1.745%1.04594 \div 1.028 - 1 \approx 1.745\%1.04594÷1.028−1≈1.745%. Using the quoted 4.5% would give 1.654%, understating the real return by about 0.09 percentage points.
  • It works over several years. Over nnn years the growth factors multiply: (1+rnom)n=(1+rreal)n(1+i)n(1 + r_{\text{nom}})^n = (1 + r_{\text{real}})^n (1 + i)^n(1+rnom​)n=(1+rreal​)n(1+i)n. The cumulative real return is the cumulative nominal growth divided by cumulative inflation, minus one. Approximating by subtraction each year and adding up the errors can go badly wrong over long horizons, as F00 showed.

The approximation rreal≈rnom−ir_{\text{real}} \approx r_{\text{nom}} - irreal​≈rnom​−i drops the term rreal×ir_{\text{real}} \times irreal​×i. OpenStax’s Principles of Finance presents that subtraction as the working definition of the real interest rate; this course uses the exact form for every calculation and the subtraction only as a mental check.

Caution: Which route to choose

Use whichever route matches how the cash flows are forecast. Prices quoted “in today’s money”, budgets prepared without inflation and CPI-linked contracts are naturally real; loan payments, bond coupons, fixed-price contracts and anything written as a number of dollars on a date are nominal. A fixed nominal payment must be converted before it can be discounted at a real rate, and the conversion needs an inflation forecast.

Real growth of salaries and prices

The Fisher relation applies to any growth rate, not only to interest. Suppose Lantern hires a barista trainer on $42,000 with raises of 3.5% a year (a fictional offer). At 2.8% inflation the real raise is 1.035÷1.028−1≈0.68%1.035 \div 1.028 - 1 \approx 0.68\%1.035÷1.028−1≈0.68% a year. After four years the salary is 42,000×1.0354≈48,195.9742{,}000 \times 1.035^4 \approx 48{,}195.9742,000×1.0354≈48,195.97 dollars, which buys what about 48,195.97÷1.0284≈43,155.7148{,}195.97 \div 1.028^4 \approx 43{,}155.7148,195.97÷1.0284≈43,155.71 dollars buys today: a 14.75% rise in dollars, a 2.75% rise in purchasing power.

Inflation-protected bonds

An ordinary bond promises fixed nominal payments, so its holder bears inflation risk: if inflation is higher than expected, the payments buy less. An inflation-protected bond moves that risk to the issuer by indexing the principal. In the United States the best known are Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS), issued by the US Treasury.

Standards: How US TIPS work (US Treasury, checked October 2026)

The principal of a TIPS goes up with inflation and down with deflation, using the Consumer Price Index from the Bureau of Labor Statistics; Treasury’s TIPS data identify it as the non-seasonally adjusted CPI-U. TIPS pay interest every six months at a fixed rate, set at auction and never less than 0.125%, and each payment is the adjusted principal times half the annual rate, so the dollar amount of interest changes as the principal changes. At maturity the holder receives the adjusted principal or the original principal, whichever is greater. TIPS are sold in terms of 5, 10 and 30 years, in multiples of $100. Interest is subject to federal income tax each year but not to state or local income tax, and changes in the principal during the year may also affect federal tax.

A simplified illustration (fictional numbers; Treasury’s own calculation uses a daily index ratio): $10,000 of TIPS with a fixed rate of 1.75%. If the index ratio is 1.015 at the first payment date, the adjusted principal is $10,150 and the payment is 10,150×0.0175÷2≈88.8110{,}150 \times 0.0175 \div 2 \approx 88.8110,150×0.0175÷2≈88.81 dollars. If the ratio reaches 1.032 a year in, the principal is $10,320 and the payment $90.30. If, at maturity, deflation had left the adjusted principal at $9,800, the holder would still receive $10,000.

Because a TIPS fixes a real rate and an ordinary Treasury of the same term fixes a nominal rate, comparing the two reveals the break-even inflation rate: (1+nominal yield)÷(1+real yield)−1(1 + \text{nominal yield}) \div (1 + \text{real yield}) - 1(1+nominal yield)÷(1+real yield)−1. If inflation turns out higher than break-even, the TIPS does better; if lower, the ordinary bond does. Worked example 3 makes the comparison.

Limits of real analysis

Every real calculation about the future rests on an inflation forecast, and forecasts are uncertain. A plan made at 2.8% looks different at 5%. Good practice is to state the assumption, test two or three alternatives, and prefer contracts or investments whose real value does not hinge on getting it exactly right. Past inflation, measured from an index, is a fact; future inflation is an assumption.

Common mistakes

  • Dividing cumulative inflation by the number of years

    Prices that rose 22.9% over five years did not rise 4.58% a year: inflation compounds. The average annual rate is the fifth root of 1.229, minus one, about 4.21%.

  • Discounting real cash flows at a nominal rate

    A forecast in today’s prices has no inflation in it; a nominal rate does. Lantern’s indexed fee discounted at 10% is undervalued by about $5,491. Convert the flows to nominal, or the rate to real, before discounting.

  • Discounting nominal cash flows at a real rate

    The opposite mix counts inflation in the cash flows but not in the rate, and overvalues: about $5,850 too much for Lantern’s offer. Fixed loan payments, coupons and fixed-price contracts are nominal.

  • Putting an APR into the Fisher relation

    The Fisher relation links effective rates over the same period. A 4.5% APR compounded monthly is an effective 4.594% a year; use that, or convert everything to monthly rates.

  • Calling a pay rise a real rise

    A 3.5% raise with 2.8% inflation is a 0.68% real raise; with 4% inflation it is a real cut. Always ask what the increase is worth after prices.

  • Thinking a TIPS pays its fixed rate on the original principal

    Interest is the adjusted principal times half the fixed annual rate, so the dollar amount of interest rises with inflation and falls with deflation. Only the final repayment has a floor: never less than the original principal.

Fictional case

Ana takes the indexed fee. Her own costs, beans, milk, rent and wages, rise with prices, so a fee that keeps its purchasing power is a better match for them, and on her 2.8% assumption it is also worth slightly more. Before signing she asks the law firm to name the index (the US City Average CPI, not seasonally adjusted, as the Bureau of Labor Statistics recommends for escalation) and the month used for each year’s adjustment. Lantern’s budget for the contract is prepared in March 2027 dollars and discounted at the real rate, so that her forecast of costs and the fee are measured the same way.

Budgeting in constant dollars. The facilities director of a fictional university plans roof and boiler replacements over twenty years. Contractors quote today’s prices, so she keeps the whole plan in real terms and discounts at a real rate. Converting to nominal would add an inflation forecast to every line without changing the answer, as long as the rate is converted the same way. When a contractor quotes a fixed nominal price for work three years away, she converts that single line to real terms before adding it in.

Pay negotiations. An HR manager at a fictional regional bus operator compares a union’s claim for a 5% rise with the company’s offer of 3% plus a cost-of-living adjustment linked to the CPI. She restates both offers in real terms under several inflation scenarios: at 2% inflation the fixed 5% is the better deal for staff; at 6% the indexed offer is. Escalation clauses of this kind, common in labour agreements, rental contracts and long supply contracts, move inflation risk from the payee to the payer.

Protecting savings. A financial planner helping a fictional retired teacher compares ordinary Treasury securities with TIPS. She computes the break-even inflation rate from the two yields, explains that TIPS fix the real return while ordinary Treasuries fix the nominal one, and points out that the teacher’s pension is fixed in dollars, so some inflation protection elsewhere reduces her overall risk.

The same habit runs through all four: write down whether each number is nominal or real, keep one kind of dollar within a calculation, and state the inflation assumption so others can test it. The next lesson, Mortgages, applies this to the largest fixed nominal payment most households ever make.

Summary

  • Nominal amounts are in the dollars of the date paid; real amounts are restated in the purchasing power of a base date. Convert with the ratio of index levels.
  • Inflation compounds: cumulative inflation = CPI_n ÷ CPI_0 − 1; average annual inflation = (CPI_n ÷ CPI_0)^(1/n) − 1, not cumulative ÷ n.
  • Fisher relation (exact): 1 + nominal = (1 + real)(1 + inflation), with effective rates over the same period.
  • Discount nominal cash flows at nominal rates and real cash flows at real rates; done consistently, both give the same present value. Lantern’s indexed fee is worth about $104,965.18 either way.
  • Mixing them is costly: real flows at a nominal rate undervalue, nominal flows at a real rate overvalue.
  • Real growth of a salary or price = (1 + nominal growth) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1.
  • US TIPS index their principal to the CPI, pay a fixed rate on the adjusted principal every six months, and repay at least the original principal; break-even inflation = (1 + nominal yield) ÷ (1 + real yield) − 1.

Terms introduced

Nominal amount
An amount of money in the dollars of the date it is paid or received, with no adjustment for inflation. Also called current dollars.
Real amount
An amount of money restated in the purchasing power of a chosen base period, by dividing out the inflation since that period. Also called constant dollars.
Base period
The date or period whose prices serve as the yardstick for real amounts; a price index is often set to 100 in it.
Cumulative inflation
The total percentage rise in prices over an interval of several periods: the end index level divided by the start level, minus 1.
Average annual inflation rate
The constant yearly inflation rate that would produce the same cumulative inflation over n years: (end index ÷ start index)^(1/n) − 1.
Fisher relation
The exact link between a nominal rate, a real rate and inflation over the same period: (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation).
Escalation clause
A contract term that changes a payment in line with a named price index, such as the CPI, so that the payment keeps its purchasing power.
Deflation
A sustained fall in the general level of prices: negative inflation.
Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities
US Treasury securities whose principal is adjusted with the Consumer Price Index and which pay a fixed rate of interest on the adjusted principal every six months; at maturity the holder receives the greater of the adjusted and the original principal. Often shortened to TIPS.
Break-even inflation rate
The inflation rate at which an inflation-protected bond and an ordinary bond of the same term give the same return: (1 + nominal yield) ÷ (1 + real yield) − 1.
