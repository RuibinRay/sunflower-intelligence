Mortgages

A home loan is an annuity with a house attached. Calculate a fixed-rate mortgage payment, compare 30- and 15-year terms, find the balance after any number of years, decide whether discount points and extra payments pay off, see what inflation does to a fixed payment, and read how an adjustable-rate mortgage resets.

About 85 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate the monthly principal-and-interest payment, the total interest and the balance after k years on a fixed-rate mortgage.
  • Compare a 30-year and a 15-year mortgage on payment, total interest and how fast the balance falls.
  • Evaluate discount points by their simple break-even time and by the present value of the savings over the time the loan is kept.
  • Calculate how much time and interest regular extra payments save, using logarithms to find the new term.
  • Calculate the real value of a fixed mortgage payment under inflation, and the new payment when an adjustable-rate mortgage resets within its caps.

Review first

  • Loan amortisation — Mortgages use its payment formula, schedules and balance outstanding, and its explanation of how fees enter the APR.
  • Annuities — You need the annuity factor, solving for the number of periods with logarithms, and finding a rate by trial.
  • Inflation and real returns — You will restate fixed nominal payments in today’s dollars.

The idea

Fictional case

March 2027. Jonah, a secondary-school teacher and an old friend of Ana’s, has agreed to buy a house for $360,000. He will pay $72,000 (20%) from his savings and borrow the other $288,000. His credit union (fictional, as are all the rates in this lesson) offers three fixed-rate loans: 30 years at 6.5%; 30 years at 6.125% if he pays 1.5 discount points upfront; or 15 years at 5.875%. Ana, who hopes to buy a flat of her own one day, is watching closely: the course project asks you to make the same kind of choice for her, with her own numbers.

A mortgage is an amortising loan

A mortgage is a loan to buy a property, secured on that property: if the borrower does not repay as agreed, the lender can take it. With a fixed-rate mortgage the interest rate never changes, so the borrower pays the same amount every month for the whole term. Each payment covers the interest due for the month and repays some principal, so the balance falls to zero by the last payment: the loan is repaid by amortisation, exactly as in the loan-amortisation lesson. The only differences are scale and length: hundreds of thousands of dollars over 180 or 360 months.

The rate is quoted per year and charged monthly, so the monthly rate is the annual rate ÷ 12. For Jonah’s 30-year offer, r=0.065÷12≈0.00541667r = 0.065 \div 12 \approx 0.00541667r=0.065÷12≈0.00541667 and n=360n = 360n=360 payments. The payment is the level annuity whose present value at rrr equals the loan:

C=288,000×0.005416671−1.00541667−360≈$1,820.36C = 288{,}000 \times \frac{0.00541667}{1 - 1.00541667^{-360}} \approx \$1{,}820.36C=288,000×1−1.00541667−3600.00541667​≈$1,820.36

In the first month the interest is 288,000×0.065÷12=1,560.00288{,}000 \times 0.065 \div 12 = 1{,}560.00288,000×0.065÷12=1,560.00 dollars, so only $260.36 of the $1,820.36 repays principal. Interest is charged on the balance, the balance starts large, and so early payments are mostly interest. As the balance shrinks, the interest part shrinks and the principal part grows; by the last payment it is almost all principal. Over 30 years Jonah would pay about $655,328.13 in total, of which about $367,328.13 is interest: more than the $288,000 he borrows.

Caution: Principal and interest are not the whole monthly bill

The formula gives the payment of principal and interest only. Many lenders also collect property taxes and homeowners insurance through an escrow account funded from each monthly payment, and the CFPB notes that because taxes and premiums change from year to year, the escrow part, and so the total payment, changes too. If Jonah’s taxes and insurance come to a fictional $540 a month, his total monthly payment would be about $2,360.36 at first, and only the $1,820.36 is fixed for 30 years.

Thirty years or fifteen

A shorter term means larger payments but far less interest, because the balance is paid down faster and there are fewer months on which to charge interest. Jonah’s credit union also charges a lower rate for the shorter term.

The 15-year loan costs $590.55 a month more but saves about $221,365.90 of interest, and after five years Jonah would owe about $51,209 less. Total interest overstates the saving, though: much of that gap is the result of repaying the loan in half the time, and each loan’s payments are worth exactly $288,000 at its own rate. The real gain is the lower rate, which is worth far less than the interest gap suggests. The 30-year loan’s advantage is flexibility: a lower required payment leaves room in the budget, and, as the theory section shows, he can still choose to pay it off faster.

Buying a lower rate: discount points

Discount points are an upfront fee paid at closing in exchange for a lower interest rate. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) explains that one point costs 1% of the loan amount and that how much a point lowers the rate varies by lender, loan type and market conditions. Jonah’s 1.5 points would cost 0.015×288,000=4,3200.015 \times 288{,}000 = 4{,}3200.015×288,000=4,320 dollars and cut his rate from 6.5% to 6.125%, which lowers the payment to about $1,749.92, a saving of about $70.44 a month.

The simple break-even time divides the cost by the monthly saving: 4,320÷70.44≈61.34{,}320 \div 70.44 \approx 61.34,320÷70.44≈61.3 months, a little over five years. If Jonah sells or refinances before then, the points lose him money; if he keeps the loan longer, they pay off. The opposite trade also exists: a lender credit lowers closing costs in exchange for a higher rate, which suits a borrower who expects to move or refinance soon.

Key idea: Every mortgage decision is a time-value decision

Points trade cash today for smaller payments later; a shorter term trades higher payments now for less interest later; extra payments trade spending now for a shorter loan. Each is judged by comparing the present value of what you give up with the present value of what you save, over the time you actually expect to keep the loan.

Points, break-even and the APR

The simple break-even (cost ÷ monthly saving) ignores two things that pull in opposite directions. The lower rate also pays the balance down a little faster, so a seller owes slightly less; and a dollar of saving in year five is worth less than a dollar of points paid today. A sounder test values every cash flow of the points loan at the rate Jonah would otherwise pay, 6.5%. The no-points loan is worth exactly $288,000 at 6.5% for any holding period, by construction. The points loan, kept kkk months and then repaid, costs:

PVpoints loan=4,320+C1×1−(1+r)−kr+Bk(1+r)k,r=0.065÷12\text{PV}_{\text{points loan}} = 4{,}320 + C_1 \times \frac{1 - (1 + r)^{-k}}{r} + \frac{B_k}{(1 + r)^k}, \qquad r = 0.065 \div 12PVpoints loan​=4,320+C1​×r1−(1+r)−k​+(1+r)kBk​​,r=0.065÷12

If that is less than $288,000, the points were worth buying. Valuing at 6.5% treats the alternative to paying points as borrowing the $4,320 at the loan rate, or using it to prepay the loan. If the cash would otherwise earn less in savings, discounting at that lower rate makes the points look better; the rate is a judgement, so state it. For Jonah it is about $614.50 more than $288,000 if he keeps the loan four years, and about $3,107.38 less if he keeps it ten. The crossover comes at about 58 months, close to the simple estimate here because the two omitted effects nearly cancel. With higher rates or a larger rate cut, they need not.

Standards: Points and the APR in US mortgage disclosures (CFPB)

For a mortgage, the APR that lenders disclose under Regulation Z (Truth in Lending) is broader than the interest rate: the CFPB explains that it reflects the interest rate, any points, mortgage broker fees and other charges paid to get the loan, so it is usually higher than the rate. Regulation Z lists points and loan fees among the components of the finance charge (12 CFR §1026.4(b)(3)). On a Loan Estimate the interest rate appears on page 1, points on page 2 (section A) and the APR on page 3. For an adjustable-rate loan, the CFPB warns, the APR does not reflect the maximum rate the loan could reach.

Treating the $4,320 of points as the only finance charge, the APR of Jonah’s points loan is the rate at which 360 payments of $1,749.92 are worth what he actually receives, 288,000−4,320=283,680288{,}000 - 4{,}320 = 283{,}680288,000−4,320=283,680 dollars. By trial, or with a spreadsheet’s RATE function, that is about 6.27%, against 6.5% for the no-points loan. The APR spreads the points over the full 30 years, so it flatters points for anyone who will not keep the loan that long: kept for four years, the same loan costs about 6.56% a year. Compare APRs between loans of the same type and term, and then ask how long you will really keep the loan.

Prepayment, paying principal ahead of schedule, saves interest because every extra dollar stops being charged interest from that month onward. The CFPB explains that not all mortgages have one; where a loan does, it typically applies when the whole balance is paid off within a set period, usually three or five years, for example by selling or refinancing, and small extra principal payments normally do not trigger it.

A fixed payment in an inflating world

A fixed-rate mortgage payment is a fixed nominal amount: $1,820.36 in 2027 and $1,820.36 in 2056. With inflation, later payments are made in dollars that buy less. Measured in today’s dollars with the method of the previous lesson, the payment made at the end of year ttt is worth C÷(1+i)tC \div (1 + i)^tC÷(1+i)t. The real burden falls further if pay rises with prices. On a fictional salary of $92,000 a year, $1,820.36 is about 23.7% of Jonah’s gross monthly pay at the start; if his pay rises 3% a year, it is about 17.7% ten years later. With a fixed rate, the lender bears the risk that inflation turns out higher than expected; the borrower bears the risk that it turns out lower.

Adjustable-rate mortgages in outline

An adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) moves the interest-rate risk the other way. Its rate is fixed for an initial period (five years in worked example 4) and then resets at regular intervals. The CFPB explains the reset: the new rate is an index, an interest rate that moves with market conditions and that the lender chooses when you apply, plus a margin, a number of percentage points set in the loan agreement that does not change after closing. Index plus margin is called the fully indexed rate, and the actual new rate is that figure subject to the loan’s rate caps.

  • Initial adjustment cap: how far the rate can move at the first reset; the CFPB says two or five percentage points is common.
  • Subsequent adjustment cap: how far it can move at each later reset; most commonly one or two points.
  • Lifetime cap: how far it can move in total over the life of the loan; most commonly five points.

At each reset the payment is recalculated with the payment formula: the current balance, the new monthly rate and the months remaining. Worked example 4 shows the jump a borrower can face at the first reset, and why the right question about an ARM is not “what is the starting payment?” but “what is the highest payment the caps allow, and could I afford it?”

Common mistakes

  • Using the annual rate with monthly payments

    The payment uses the monthly rate (annual ÷ 12) and the number of months (12 × years). Using 6.5% with 360 periods gives about $18,720 a month, which is absurd; working out an annual payment over 30 years and dividing by 12 gives about $1,837.86, which looks plausible but is still wrong, because interest is charged monthly.

  • Treating the payment as the whole housing cost

    The formula covers principal and interest only. Property taxes and homeowners insurance, often collected through escrow, come on top and can change every year.

  • Judging points by the APR alone

    The APR spreads points over the full term. If you will sell or refinance in a few years, the cost of the loan over the time you actually keep it can be higher than the no-points loan, even though its APR is lower.

  • Thinking the balance falls evenly

    After a third of the term, Jonah would have repaid less than a sixth of the loan. Interest dominates early payments; find the balance with the balance formula, not by subtracting payments.

  • Comparing an ARM’s starting payment with a fixed payment

    The starting payment lasts only for the fixed period. Compare the fixed payment with the payment the caps allow after resets, and plan for the worst case.

  • Forgetting inflation when judging a 30-year commitment

    A fixed payment of $1,820.36 is worth about $750 in today’s dollars by year 30 at 3% inflation. The real burden falls over time, though only inflation that turns out higher than expected helps the borrower beyond what the rate already allowed for.

Summary

  • A fixed-rate mortgage is an amortising loan: C = PV × r ÷ [1 − (1 + r)^−n] with r = annual rate ÷ 12 and n = months. Taxes, insurance and escrow are extra.
  • Early payments are mostly interest; the balance after k payments is the present value of the remaining payments.
  • A 15-year loan has higher payments and far less total interest than a 30-year loan; a 30-year loan with voluntary extra payments buys flexibility at a price.
  • One discount point costs 1% of the loan. Simple break-even = cost ÷ monthly saving; the sounder test values the points loan’s cash flows over the expected holding period.
  • The disclosed mortgage APR includes points and other finance charges but assumes the loan runs its full term.
  • Extra payments shorten the term: n = −ln(1 − rPV ÷ C) ÷ ln(1 + r). Check for a prepayment penalty first.
  • Inflation reduces the real value of a fixed payment: C ÷ (1 + i)^t.
  • An ARM resets to index + margin within initial, subsequent and lifetime caps; judge it by the capped worst-case payment.

Terms introduced

Mortgage
A loan to buy a property that is secured on that property, so the lender can take the property if the borrower does not repay as agreed.
Fixed-rate mortgage
A mortgage whose interest rate stays the same for the whole term, so the monthly payment of principal and interest never changes.
Down payment
The part of a property’s price that the buyer pays from their own funds; the mortgage finances the rest.
Discount points
Fees paid to a lender at closing in exchange for a lower interest rate; one point costs 1% of the loan amount. Often called points.
Lender credit
Money a lender contributes to a borrower’s closing costs in exchange for a higher interest rate: the opposite of discount points.
Escrow account
An account set up by a mortgage lender and funded from part of each monthly payment, from which property taxes and homeowners insurance are paid. Also called an impound account.
Adjustable-rate mortgage
A mortgage whose interest rate is fixed for an initial period and then resets at intervals to an index plus a margin, within rate caps. Often shortened to ARM.
Fully indexed rate
For an adjustable-rate mortgage, the index plus the margin: the rate the loan would reset to before any caps apply.
Rate cap
A limit on how much an adjustable rate can change at the first reset, at each later reset, or in total over the life of the loan.
