Loan amortisation

An instalment loan is an annuity seen from the borrower’s side. Learn to calculate the payment, build a schedule that splits each payment into interest and principal, find the balance owed at any date, and use those tools to compare loans and instalment plans, to judge prepaying or refinancing, and to see what fees do to the APR.

About 90 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate the level payment on an amortising loan and the total interest it costs.
  • Construct an amortisation schedule showing the interest, principal and balance for each payment.
  • Calculate the balance outstanding after any payment by the prospective and the retrospective methods, and the interest paid between two dates.
  • Compare an interest-only loan with an amortising loan, and a bank loan with an instalment plan, without being misled by total interest.
  • Evaluate prepayment and refinancing, and calculate the APR of a loan with an up-front fee.

Review first

  • Annuities — The loan payment is the annuity formula solved for the payment, and the supplier’s plan was valued there.
  • Effective annual rates — Loan rates are quoted as APRs and compounded monthly; you need the periodic rate and the effective annual rate.
  • Interest and inflation — This lesson uses its meaning of APR, including the broader APR disclosed on US closed-end consumer loans under Regulation Z.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans and its own cafés, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

March 2027. Coastline Bank offers to lend Lantern the $24,000 for the cold-brew system at 7.2% a year compounded monthly, repaid in 48 equal monthly payments, the first one month after the loan is made. Lantern already owes Coastline $10,000 on a different kind of loan: the note it signed on 1 March 2026, on which it pays only the interest, $150 a quarter, and repays the whole $10,000 on 1 March 2028. Before deciding between the bank and the supplier’s instalment plan, Ana wants to know four things: the monthly payment, what the loan costs in interest, how quickly the debt falls, and how the two offers really compare.

Two ways to repay a loan

Every loan payment does one or both of two jobs: it pays the interest that has built up since the last payment, and it repays some of the principal, the amount borrowed; the principal not yet repaid is the outstanding balance. Loans differ in how they divide the work.

Interest-only loan
Each payment covers only the interest, so the balance stays the same until the end, when the whole principal is repaid in one large final payment, a balloon payment. Lantern’s Coastline note works this way: 10,000×0.06÷4=15010{,}000 \times 0.06 \div 4 = 15010,000×0.06÷4=150 dollars every quarter, then $10,000 on 1 March 2028.
Amortising loan
Each payment covers the interest and repays part of the principal, so the balance falls to zero by the last payment. Car loans, most equipment loans and standard mortgages work this way. Repaying a debt gradually in this way is called amortisation.

The payment

From the bank’s side, an amortising loan is an annuity: it hands over the loan today and receives equal payments at the end of each month. It sets the payment so that, at the loan’s rate, the payments are worth exactly what it lends. That is the annuity present-value formula solved for the payment. For the brewer loan, with r=0.072÷12=0.006r = 0.072 \div 12 = 0.006r=0.072÷12=0.006 and n=48n = 48n=48:

C=PV×r1−(1+r)−n=24,000×0.0061−1.006−48=1440.249593≈576.94C = \frac{\text{PV} \times r}{1 - (1 + r)^{-n}} = \frac{24{,}000 \times 0.006}{1 - 1.006^{-48}} = \frac{144}{0.249593} \approx 576.94C=1−(1+r)−nPV×r​=1−1.006−4824,000×0.006​=0.249593144​≈576.94

Lantern would pay $576.94 a month for four years. The 48 payments add up to about 48×576.939452≈27,693.0948 \times 576.939452 \approx 27{,}693.0948×576.939452≈27,693.09 dollars (keeping the payment unrounded), so the loan costs about $3,693.09 in interest. That total is worth knowing, but, as this lesson shows, it is a poor guide to whether a loan is cheap or dear.

Inside each payment

Interest is always charged on the balance outstanding. In the first month Lantern owes $24,000, so the interest is 24,000×0.006=144.0024{,}000 \times 0.006 = 144.0024,000×0.006=144.00 dollars. The rest of the payment, 576.94−144.00=432.94576.94 - 144.00 = 432.94576.94−144.00=432.94 dollars, repays principal, and the balance falls to $23,567.06. In the second month interest is charged on that smaller balance, 23,567.06×0.006≈141.4023{,}567.06 \times 0.006 \approx 141.4023,567.06×0.006≈141.40 dollars, so a little more of the same payment, $435.54, goes to principal. Month by month the interest part shrinks and the principal part grows. A table that sets this out for every payment is an amortisation schedule.

Key idea: Interest is charged on what is still owed

Each period’s interest is the periodic rate times the balance at the start of the period; whatever is left of the payment repays principal. Any schedule, for any loan, follows from that one rule. Paying principal back sooner, by amortising or by paying extra, means less is owed and so less interest is charged; it does not mean the rate is lower.

The balance at any date: two methods

You do not need the whole schedule to find the balance after kkk payments. Two methods give it directly, and they always agree.

  • Prospective (looking forward): the balance is the present value, at the loan rate, of the payments still to come. After kkk of nnn payments, n−kn - kn−k remain: Bk=C [1−(1+r)−(n−k)]÷rB_k = C\,[1 - (1 + r)^{-(n-k)}] \div rBk​=C[1−(1+r)−(n−k)]÷r. For the brewer loan after 24 payments: 576.939452×22.289933≈12,859.94576.939452 \times 22.289933 \approx 12{,}859.94576.939452×22.289933≈12,859.94.
  • Retrospective (looking back): the balance is what was borrowed, grown at the loan rate, minus the payments made, each grown at the loan rate to the same date: Bk=PV(1+r)k−C [(1+r)k−1]÷rB_k = \text{PV}(1 + r)^k - C\,[(1 + r)^k - 1] \div rBk​=PV(1+r)k−C[(1+r)k−1]÷r. For the brewer loan: 24,000×1.154387−576.939452×25.731215≈27,705.295−14,845.353≈12,859.9424{,}000 \times 1.154387 - 576.939452 \times 25.731215 \approx 27{,}705.295 - 14{,}845.353 \approx 12{,}859.9424,000×1.154387−576.939452×25.731215≈27,705.295−14,845.353≈12,859.94.

They agree because the loan was fair at the start: the payments were worth exactly the amount borrowed. Moving that equality forward kkk periods splits it into the payments already made and the payments still due, and the balance is the gap between them. The prospective method is quicker when you know the payment and the remaining term; the retrospective method works even when payments have been irregular, as long as you grow each one to the same date. The lender’s rounded schedule shows $12,859.95, a cent more, for the reason given above.

The balance also gives the interest paid over any stretch of time: the payments made, minus the fall in the balance. In the second year Lantern would pay 12×576.939452−(18,629.807−12,859.942)≈6,923.273−5,769.865≈1,153.4112 \times 576.939452 - (18{,}629.807 - 12{,}859.942) \approx 6{,}923.273 - 5{,}769.865 \approx 1{,}153.4112×576.939452−(18,629.807−12,859.942)≈6,923.273−5,769.865≈1,153.41 dollars of interest. Businesses need that figure for their accounts, where interest is an expense and repaid principal is not.

Interest-only versus amortising: why total interest misleads

On Lantern’s Coastline note the interest is 1.5% a quarter on $10,000 for eight quarters: 8×150=1,2008 \times 150 = 1{,}2008×150=1,200 dollars in total. Suppose the same $10,000 at the same 6% had been amortised over the same eight quarters. The quarterly payment would be 10,000×0.015÷(1−1.015−8)≈1,335.8410{,}000 \times 0.015 \div (1 - 1.015^{-8}) \approx 1{,}335.8410,000×0.015÷(1−1.015−8)≈1,335.84 dollars, and the total interest only about $686.72.

The interest-only note is not dearer. Both loans charge exactly 1.5% a quarter on whatever is owed, and at 1.5% the payments of each are worth exactly $10,000 today. The note costs more interest because Lantern keeps the whole $10,000 for two years instead of handing it back bit by bit. Total interest grows with how much is borrowed and for how long, not only with the rate. To compare the cost of two loans, compare their rates (as effective annual rates, including fees) or the present value of their payments at a common rate; never their total interest alone.

Interest-only loans suit borrowers who expect a large inflow at the end, or who want low payments now; their risk is the balloon payment, which must be repaid or refinanced on a fixed date whatever conditions are then. Lantern must find $10,000 on 1 March 2028.

Paying early: prepayment

A prepayment repays principal earlier than the schedule requires. Because interest is charged on the balance, every extra dollar of principal saves interest at the loan rate for the rest of the loan. Suppose Lantern paid $676.94 a month on the brewer loan, $100 more than required. The formula for the number of payments gives n=−ln⁡(1−24,000×0.006÷676.94)÷ln⁡1.006≈39.98n = -\ln(1 - 24{,}000 \times 0.006 \div 676.94) \div \ln 1.006 \approx 39.98n=−ln(1−24,000×0.006÷676.94)÷ln1.006≈39.98: 39 payments of $676.94 and a 40th of about $664.66, eight months sooner. Total interest would fall from about $3,693.09 to about $3,065.30, a saving of about $627.80.

Prepaying is like investing at the loan’s rate with no risk: worth doing if the money has no better use that earns more, but not if it leaves the business short of cash. Read the loan agreement first. Some loans charge a prepayment penalty; the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, for example, describes mortgages whose terms charge a fee if the borrower pays off all or a large part of the loan early, typically within the first few years.

Refinancing

Refinancing replaces an existing loan with a new one, usually to get a lower rate, and uses the new loan to pay off the old balance. It is worth doing when the present value of the payment savings exceeds the costs of switching, such as an arrangement fee. Discount the monthly savings at the new loan’s rate: they are as certain as the payments themselves. The quick check is the break-even time, the fee divided by the monthly saving, which ignores interest but shows how long the borrower must keep the new loan for the switch to pay.

Short, small balances rarely repay the costs. If, after 24 payments, another lender offered to refinance Lantern’s $12,859.94 balance at 6.0% for the remaining 24 months for a $150 fee, the new payment would be about $569.96, saving only about $6.98 a month. The savings are worth about $157.47 at 0.5% a month, barely more than the fee, and the break-even time is more than 21 months. Worked example 3 shows a refinancing that does pay.

Fees and the APR of a closed-end loan

An up-front fee raises the cost of a loan even though the rate and the payments stay the same. If Coastline charged a 1.5% arrangement fee, $360, deducted from the loan, Lantern would receive $23,640 but still make 48 payments of $576.94. The rate that makes those payments worth $23,640 is about 0.6653% a month: 12 times that is about 7.98%, against the 7.2% quoted. This is the same search for an implied rate as in the annuities lesson.

Standards: The APR on US consumer loans (Regulation Z)

For closed-end consumer credit, such as car loans and mortgages, the US Truth in Lending rules (Regulation Z) require lenders to disclose an APR described as “a measure of the cost of credit, expressed as a yearly rate” that relates the amount and timing of what the consumer receives to the amount and timing of the payments (§1026.22). Under the actuarial method set out in Appendix J (the rule also allows the United States Rule method), the APR is the rate per payment period multiplied by the number of periods in a year: a nominal annual rate, like the 7.98% above. It reflects the finance charge, which includes interest and charges such as points and loan fees (§1026.4), so it is usually higher than the interest rate, and a disclosed APR within one-eighth of a percentage point of the exact figure is treated as accurate for regular transactions (§1026.22(a)(2)), and within one-quarter of a point for irregular ones, such as loans with multiple advances or irregular payment periods or amounts. Regulation Z does not cover credit extended primarily for business purposes or to anyone other than a natural person (§1026.3), so Lantern’s loans fall outside it. The calculation is still the right way for any borrower to compare offers with different fees.

Assumptions and limits

  • The rate is fixed for the life of the loan. Variable-rate loans need the payment or the schedule recalculated when the rate changes; the mortgages lesson outlines adjustable rates.
  • Payments are made on time and on schedule. Late payments add interest and often fees; prepayments shorten the loan or lower later payments, depending on the agreement.
  • The payment period matches the compounding period. If not, convert the rate to the rate per payment period first.
  • Taxes are ignored. For a business, interest is usually a tax-deductible expense, which lowers the after-tax cost of borrowing; F05 Corporate Finance takes this up.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the balance falls in a straight line

    Halfway through a level-payment loan, more than half is still owed, because early payments are mostly interest. Lantern would owe $12,859.94 after 24 of 48 payments, not $12,000. Find the balance with the prospective or retrospective method, never by subtraction.

  • Charging interest on the original amount

    Interest on an amortising loan is charged on the balance still owed, not on the amount first borrowed. Charging 0.6% of $24,000 every month would give $144 × 48 = $6,912 of interest, almost twice the true $3,693.09.

  • Comparing loans by total interest

    Total interest grows with the term and the amount still owed, so an interest-only or longer loan always shows more of it, even at the same rate. Compare rates (as EARs or APRs including fees) or present values at a common rate.

  • Ignoring fees

    A fee deducted at the start means you receive less but repay the same. A 7.2% loan with a $360 fee on $24,000 over four years costs about 7.98% a year. Compare offers by APR including fees, not by the interest rate alone.

  • Using the wrong number of payments for the balance

    The prospective method discounts the payments still to come: n − k of them, not n or k. The retrospective method must grow both the loan and the payments to the same date.

  • Prepaying or refinancing without counting the costs

    Prepaying saves interest at the loan rate but uses cash the business may need; refinancing pays only if the present value of the savings exceeds the fees and any prepayment penalty. Work out both before deciding.

In practice

Amortisation schedules are working documents for lenders, borrowers and accountants.

Lending to a business. A credit officer at a fictional regional bank reviews an application from a battery-storage installer for a $400,000 equipment loan over five years. She builds the schedule to see the monthly payment and the balance at each year end, then checks that the installer’s forecast cash flow covers the payments with room to spare. Because the equipment loses value over time, she also checks that the balance outstanding stays below what the equipment could be sold for, so the bank is protected if the borrower defaults.

Recording a loan in the accounts. The accountant of a fictional restaurant group uses each loan’s schedule to split every payment: the interest part is an expense on the income statement, the principal part reduces the liability on the balance sheet. At the year end she uses the balance due within the next twelve months to split the loan between current and noncurrent liabilities.

Choosing between “0% finance” and a cash discount. A fictional car dealer offers a buyer either 0% finance on the $30,000 list price over 48 months ($625 a month) or a $2,000 discount for paying cash. A buyer who can borrow from a credit union at 5% compares the two: the dealer’s payments are worth about $27,139.35 at 5%, so the 0% finance is better than taking the $28,000 cash price with money borrowed at 5%, by about $861. With a $3,500 discount, the cash price, $26,500, would win. The answer depends on the size of the discount and the rate the buyer can get elsewhere.

Summary

  • An interest-only loan pays interest each period and repays the principal in a final balloon payment; an amortising loan repays principal with every payment, so the balance reaches zero.
  • The level payment is the annuity formula solved for C: C = PV × r ÷ [1 − (1 + r)^−n]. Lantern’s $24,000 brewer loan at 0.6% a month for 48 months costs $576.94 a month and about $3,693.09 in interest.
  • Each row of a schedule: interest = r × balance at the start; principal = payment − interest; new balance = old balance − principal. Interest shrinks and principal grows by (1 + r) each period.
  • The balance after k payments is the present value of the n − k payments left (prospective) or the loan grown to date minus the payments grown to date (retrospective); the two agree.
  • Total interest measures how much and for how long you borrow, not how dear the loan is; compare loans by rates including fees, or by present value at a common rate.
  • Prepayment saves interest at the loan rate; refinancing pays when the present value of the savings exceeds the fees. Up-front fees raise the APR above the quoted rate.
  • The supplier’s plan (about 4.73%) beats Coastline’s loan (7.2%) by about $595.57 in today’s money, at the price of a higher monthly payment for two years.

Terms introduced

Interest-only loan
A loan whose regular payments cover only the interest, so the principal is repaid in full at the end in one balloon payment.
Balloon payment
A large final payment that repays all or most of a loan’s principal at maturity, as on an interest-only loan.
Amortising loan
A loan repaid by regular payments that each cover the interest due and repay part of the principal, so the balance falls to zero by the last payment.
Amortisation schedule
A table showing, for each payment on a loan, the interest, the principal repaid and the balance still owed.
Outstanding balance
The principal still owed on a loan at a given date: the present value of the remaining payments at the loan rate.
Prepayment
Repaying principal earlier than the schedule requires, which reduces the interest charged and, if the payment is kept the same, shortens the loan.
Prepayment penalty
A fee some loan agreements charge if the borrower repays all or a large part of the loan early.
Refinancing
Replacing an existing loan with a new one, usually at a lower rate or for a different term, and using the new loan to pay off the old balance.
Finance charge
Under the US Truth in Lending rules (Regulation Z), the cost of consumer credit as a dollar amount, including interest and charges such as points and loan fees.
