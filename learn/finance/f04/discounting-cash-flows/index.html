Discounting cash flows

Most real streams of money are uneven: a loan pays interest and then its principal, a project costs money now and returns different amounts later. Learn to value any stream by discounting each cash flow, to judge a decision by its net present value, and to find the rate of return that makes that value zero.

About 90 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Construct a timeline for an uneven stream of cash flows and calculate its present value, or its value at any other date, by discounting each flow.
  • Calculate the net present value of a decision and use it to accept or reject the decision, explaining what a positive NPV means.
  • Price a fixed-rate note at the current market rate and explain why its value moves in the opposite direction to interest rates.
  • Estimate an internal rate of return by trial and interpolation, and explain how it relates to NPV and where it can mislead.
  • Use spreadsheet NPV and IRR functions correctly, including the timing of the first cash flow.

Review first

  • Present and future value — You need discount factors, timelines and moving single amounts to a comparison date.
  • Effective annual rates — You need a rate per period that matches the spacing of the cash flows.
  • Six principles behind financial decisions — It explains incremental cash flows and why sunk costs are ignored, which decide what goes into an NPV.

The idea

Fictional case

On 1 March 2026 Lantern borrowed $10,000 from Coastline Bank on a two-year note at 6% a year: $150 of interest every quarter and the $10,000 repaid on 1 March 2028. It is now 1 March 2027 and the quarterly payment due today has just been made. Four payments remain: $150 on 1 June, 1 September and 1 December 2027, and $150 plus the $10,000 principal on 1 March 2028. A similar loan now costs 8% a year compounded quarterly (fictional, like Coastline itself). Ana wants to know what the note is worth today, both to Coastline, which could sell the loan to another lender, and to Lantern, which owes it. The previous lesson valued the principal alone at $9,238.45. Now the interest comes in too.

A stream is a set of single amounts

A cash-flow stream is a series of amounts due at different dates. When the amounts are equal and evenly spaced, the stream is an annuity, and module 3 gives formulas that value it in one step. Most real streams are uneven: a project’s cash flows rise and fall, a contract pays different sums at different dates, and a note like Lantern’s pays small amounts of interest and then one large sum. The method for any stream is the one you already have, applied once per amount. Put each cash flow on the timeline, discount it to time 0 with its own discount factor, and add the present values. Adding is now allowed, because every value is at the same date.

In March 2027 the note is worth $9,809.61 at the market rate. It promises $10,600 in all, $600 of interest and the $10,000 principal, but those dollars arrive over the coming year, and the market now pays 8% for waiting while the note pays only 6%. That shortfall makes the note worth $190.39 less than its $10,000 face value.

The two sides see the same number. If Coastline sold the loan, a buyer who can earn 8% compounded quarterly elsewhere would pay no more than about $9,810 for it, so Coastline would take a loss of about $190. Lantern sees the mirror image: its obligation is worth $9,809.61 at today’s rates, and the $190.39 is what it gains, in today’s money, from having borrowed at 6% in 2026 instead of at 8% now. That is a good reason to keep the note to maturity rather than replace it with a new loan.

Key idea: When rates rise, fixed payments are worth less

A note, loan or bond with fixed payments falls in value when market rates rise and rises when they fall. At exactly its own rate, 6% compounded quarterly, Lantern’s note is worth exactly $10,000: discounting the interest at the rate it is paid gives back the principal. Above 6% the note is worth less than its face value; below 6%, more.

From value to decision: net present value

A stream’s present value says what it is worth. A decision usually has money going out as well as coming in, typically a cost now and benefits later. The net present value (NPV) of a decision is the present value of all its cash flows, with money received counted as positive and money paid as negative, discounted at the discount rate: the return you could earn elsewhere on an investment of similar risk.

NPV=C0+C11+r+C2(1+r)2+⋯+Cn(1+r)n=∑t=0nCt(1+r)t\text{NPV} = C_0 + \frac{C_1}{1 + r} + \frac{C_2}{(1 + r)^2} + \cdots + \frac{C_n}{(1 + r)^n} = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{C_t}{(1 + r)^t}NPV=C0​+1+rC1​​+(1+r)2C2​​+⋯+(1+r)nCn​​=t=0∑n​(1+r)tCt​​

C0C_0C0​ is the cash flow today, usually a cost and so negative. It is not discounted, because it is already at time 0. A positive NPV means the decision returns more than the next-best use of the money, by that many dollars in today’s money: it adds value. A negative NPV means the money would do better elsewhere. The rule follows: accept a decision with a positive NPV and reject one with a negative NPV.

Fictional case

Lantern faces exactly this question this month. A cold-brew brewing system costs $24,000. Ana estimates the extra cash it would bring in, after paying for the beans, milk, bottles and staff time: $6,000 in the first year, $8,500 in the second and $9,000 in each of the third and fourth years, when she expects to sell the brewer for about $2,500. These estimates are fictional, and uncertain. For a risky small-business project she asks a return of 12% a year, the figure she judges she could earn elsewhere at similar risk. Worked example 3 discounts the estimates: the NPV is about +$1,848, so on her numbers the brewer is worth buying. How to pay for it, with Coastline’s 48-month loan or the supplier’s instalment plan, is a separate financing question, which the lessons on annuities and loan amortisation answer.

The rate that makes NPV zero

NPV depends on the discount rate. The higher the rate, the less the later inflows are worth and the lower the NPV, so somewhere it crosses zero. The rate at which it does is the decision’s internal rate of return (IRR): the rate of return the investment itself earns, period by period. For the brewer it is about 15.3% a year. Because 15.3% is above the 12% Ana requires, the IRR gives the same verdict as the NPV.

There is no general formula for the IRR, because the rate sits inside several powers at once. The classic method is trial and error: try a rate and compute the NPV; if it is positive, try a higher rate, if negative a lower one, and close in on zero. Spreadsheets and financial calculators do the same thing, only faster. This lesson introduces the idea; F05 Corporate Finance uses NPV and IRR to make investment decisions in depth.

In depth

The value of a stream at any date

A stream can be valued at any comparison date TTT, not only today: each cash flow is moved to TTT, compounded if it comes earlier and discounted if it comes later, and the results are added: VT=∑tCt(1+r)T−tV_T = \sum_t C_t (1 + r)^{T - t}VT​=∑t​Ct​(1+r)T−t. Values at different dates are linked by a single factor: VT=PV×(1+r)TV_T = \text{PV} \times (1 + r)^TVT​=PV×(1+r)T. That gives a useful check.

Valued at 1 March 2028 (quarter 4), the note’s payments are worth 150×1.023+150×1.022+150×1.02+10,150=10,618.24150 \times 1.02^3 + 150 \times 1.02^2 + 150 \times 1.02 + 10{,}150 = 10{,}618.24150×1.023+150×1.022+150×1.02+10,150=10,618.24 dollars: what a holder would have if she reinvested each interest payment at 2% a quarter until the note matured. And 9,809.61×1.024=10,618.249{,}809.61 \times 1.02^4 = 10{,}618.249,809.61×1.024=10,618.24, the same. Choosing the date where the question lives, today for a price and the end for a savings balance, saves work; it never changes the answer.

Matching the rate to the timing

The formulas assume each cash flow arrives at the end of a period, that time 0 is today, and that the rate is the rate per period of the same length as the spacing. With quarterly cash flows you need a quarterly rate. If the rate you are given is quoted differently, convert it first, as the lesson on effective annual rates shows: an APR compounded quarterly is divided by 4; an effective annual rate becomes a quarterly rate of (1 + EAR)^(1/4) − 1.

When cash flows fall at irregular dates, measure each date in years from today, tit_iti​, and discount with the effective annual rate: PV=∑iCi÷(1+EAR)ti\text{PV} = \sum_i C_i \div (1 + \text{EAR})^{t_i}PV=∑i​Ci​÷(1+EAR)ti​. Worked example 2 does this for payments due in 0, 9, 12 and 24 months. Annual cash flows in a business plan are a simplification: the brewer’s sales arrive week by week, not in one lump at each year end. Treating them as year-end amounts slightly understates their value, and analysts accept that in exchange for a simpler model, or switch to monthly periods when the difference matters.

Signs and whose point of view

Every cash flow is someone’s inflow and someone else’s outflow. From Coastline’s side the note’s payments are inflows worth +$9,809.61; from Lantern’s side the same payments are outflows worth −$9,809.61. Pick one point of view for a whole problem and keep it. Financial calculators and spreadsheets use the same convention, money received positive and money paid negative, which is why a cost today must be entered with a minus sign.

Net present value in more depth

Three choices decide whether an NPV means anything.

  1. The rate. The discount rate is the return the money could earn in its next-best use at similar risk: its opportunity cost. A riskier stream is discounted at a higher rate. For a business this is linked to its cost of capital, but a project riskier than the business as a whole deserves a higher rate. The rate on a loan used to pay for the project is not, by itself, the right rate: the loan rate prices the lender’s fairly safe claim, while the project’s cash flows are less certain.
  2. The cash flows. Only incremental cash flows count: cash that changes because of the decision. Money already spent and unrecoverable is a sunk cost and is left out. NPV uses cash, not accounting profit: depreciation, for example, is not a cash flow.
  3. The horizon. Every cash flow the decision causes belongs in the stream, including those at the end, such as the brewer’s resale value or the cost of removing equipment.

NPVs add up. The NPV of two independent projects together is the sum of their NPVs, and the NPV of a project financed at a fair market rate is not changed by the financing. When options exclude each other, such as two different brewers for the same counter, choose the one with the highest NPV. Plotting NPV against the discount rate gives an NPV profile. For a conventional project, one cost followed by inflows, the profile slopes down and crosses zero once, at the IRR.

Estimates are uncertain, so an NPV should be recalculated under other assumptions. If every year’s cash from the brewer were 10% lower than Ana expects, its NPV at 12% would fall from +$1,847.77 to −$578.13. Without the $2,500 resale value it would be only +$258.98. A positive NPV that disappears under a small change in the estimates is a close call, not a clear win.

Internal rate of return: definition and trial

The IRR is the rate that solves 0=∑t=0nCt÷(1+IRR)t0 = \sum_{t=0}^{n} C_t \div (1 + \text{IRR})^t0=∑t=0n​Ct​÷(1+IRR)t. Take a two-year investment of $1,000 that returns $600 at the end of each year. At 10% its NPV is −1,000+600÷1.1+600÷1.21=41.32-1{,}000 + 600 \div 1.1 + 600 \div 1.21 = 41.32−1,000+600÷1.1+600÷1.21=41.32; at 15% it is −24.57-24.57−24.57. The IRR lies between them. A straight line between the two points crosses zero at

IRR≈10%+(15%−10%)×41.3241.32−(−24.57)=13.14%\text{IRR} \approx 10\% + (15\% - 10\%) \times \frac{41.32}{41.32 - (-24.57)} = 13.14\%IRR≈10%+(15%−10%)×41.32−(−24.57)41.32​=13.14%

With only two periods the equation is a quadratic and can be solved exactly: the IRR is 13.07%. Linear interpolation overshoots slightly, because the NPV profile curves (it is convex), so the straight line lies above it. Checking the NPV at 13.14% gives −$0.96, slightly negative, and a second interpolation closes the gap. With more periods there is no neat formula, and calculators and spreadsheets repeat this kind of step until the NPV is close enough to zero.

For a conventional project, an IRR above the required return and a positive NPV are the same statement, so the two rules agree. The IRR has limits. When cash flows change sign more than once, for example a project with a large clean-up cost at the end, the equation can have two IRRs or none. When choosing between options of different sizes or timing, the one with the higher IRR can have the lower NPV: a 50% return on $1,000 adds less value than a 20% return on $100,000. In those cases trust the NPV. F05 Corporate Finance treats these issues fully.

For a loan or a bond, the IRR has another name: its yield. A buyer who paid $9,809.61 for Lantern’s note on 1 March 2027 and received the four payments would earn exactly 2% a quarter, 8% a year compounded quarterly, because that is the rate used to price it. A buyer who paid $10,000 would earn the note’s own 6%.

Spreadsheets: NPV, IRR and the first cash flow

Spreadsheet functions save time but carry a trap. Excel’s NPV(rate, value1, value2, …) treats the first value as arriving one period from now, at the end of period 1. A cash flow at time 0 must be left out of the range and added separately: =C0 + NPV(r, C1:Cn). Microsoft’s own documentation says the first value must be added to the NPV result rather than included in the values. Put the time-0 cost inside the range and every cash flow is discounted one period too many, so the result is the true NPV divided by 1 + r: for the brewer, $1,649.80 instead of $1,847.77.

IRR(values), by contrast, expects the whole stream including the time-0 flow, and returns the rate per period. For cash flows at irregular dates, XNPV and XIRR take a list of dates as well as amounts. Whatever the tool, lay out the timeline first, so you know which cash flow sits at which date before you type a formula.

Common mistakes

  • Adding cash flows from different dates

    The note’s payments add up to $10,600, but that number mixes dollars from four dates and is not its value. Discount each flow to one date first; only then add.

  • Discounting the time-0 cash flow

    A cost paid today is already at time 0: it is not divided by (1 + r). The same slip happens in spreadsheets when the time-0 cost is put inside Excel’s NPV range, which discounts every flow one period too many and divides the true NPV by 1 + r.

  • A rate that does not match the spacing

    Quarterly cash flows need a quarterly rate. Discounting the note’s quarterly payments at 8% per quarter, or at 8% with t counted in quarters, gives a value far too low. Convert the rate to the period of the cash flows first.

  • Using the loan rate as the discount rate for a risky project

    Discounted at 7.2% a year, roughly the rate on Lantern’s bank loan, the brewer’s NPV would be about $5,007, far more than at the 12% its risk calls for. The rate should reflect the risk of the project’s cash flows, not the cost of the loan that pays for it.

  • Choosing by IRR between options that exclude each other

    A small project with a high IRR can add less value than a large one with a lower IRR. When only one option can be chosen, pick the higher NPV. And when cash flows change sign more than once, the IRR may not be unique: use the NPV.

  • Treating an NPV estimate as a fact

    An NPV is only as good as its cash-flow estimates and rate. The brewer’s +$1,847.77 becomes −$578.13 if each year’s cash is 10% lower. Always recompute the NPV under less favourable assumptions before deciding.

In practice

Discounting uneven cash flows is the everyday tool of anyone who prices debt or decides on investments.

Selling a loan. Banks sometimes sell loans they have made to other lenders or investors. The buyer prices the remaining payments exactly as this lesson priced Lantern’s note: each payment discounted at the rate the buyer could earn on similar loans today. When market rates have risen since the loans were made, the price is below the amount still owed, and the selling bank records the difference as a loss. The same arithmetic, in reverse, gives a gain when rates have fallen. Nobody has defaulted; only the rate used to discount fixed payments has changed.

Choosing between investments. The finance team of a fictional regional airline must decide whether to refurbish the cabins of four aircraft. The spending comes in two bursts, the extra revenue builds up over several years, and the cabins must be refitted again before the aircraft are retired. The team sets out every incremental cash flow on a timeline, discounts at the airline’s required return for projects of this risk, and reports the NPV, the IRR and how both change if passenger demand is weaker than expected. Because the cash flows change sign more than once, the board is told to rely on the NPV.

Comparing payment plans. A fictional university buying a new library system is offered a price of $1.2 million paid on delivery, or a series of uneven payments over three years tied to milestones. The procurement office discounts the milestone payments at the university’s borrowing rate, because a promise to pay is close to a loan, and chooses the plan with the lower present cost. Here the cash flows are outflows, so the better option is the one with the smaller present value.

Summary

  • An uneven cash-flow stream is valued by discounting each cash flow to one date with its own discount factor and adding the results: PV = Σ C_t ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ.
  • A stream can be valued at any date; values at different dates differ by the factor (1 + r)ᵀ, which gives a check.
  • The rate per period must match the spacing of the cash flows; for irregular dates, use t in years and the effective annual rate.
  • Lantern’s Coastline note, four payments of $150 and the $10,000 principal, is worth $9,809.61 in March 2027 at 8% compounded quarterly: less than its face value because its 6% rate is below the market’s. Fixed payments lose value when rates rise.
  • NPV = Σ C_t ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ from t = 0, with costs negative and only incremental cash flows. Accept a positive NPV; between exclusive options, choose the highest NPV.
  • The IRR is the rate at which the NPV is zero, found by trial and interpolation or a spreadsheet. For a conventional project it agrees with the NPV rule; with several sign changes or exclusive options, rely on NPV.
  • In Excel, NPV() treats its first value as one period away: add the time-0 cash flow outside the function. IRR() takes the whole stream.

Terms introduced

Cash-flow stream
A series of cash flows due at different dates. An even stream (equal amounts at equal intervals) is an annuity; an uneven one is valued by discounting each cash flow separately.
Net present value
The sum of the present values of all the cash flows a decision causes, with money received positive and money paid negative, discounted at the return available elsewhere at similar risk. A positive NPV means the decision adds value. Often shortened to NPV.
Internal rate of return
The discount rate at which the net present value of a set of cash flows is zero: the rate of return the investment itself earns. For a loan or bond bought at a given price, its yield. Often shortened to IRR.
NPV profile
A graph of a decision’s net present value against the discount rate. For a conventional project it slopes down and crosses zero at the internal rate of return.
Face value
The amount a note or bond promises to repay at maturity, on which its interest is calculated; also called par value or principal.
