The trial balance

List every ledger balance in a debit or a credit column and the two totals should agree. Learn to prepare a trial balance, to use the gap between the totals to track down mistakes, and to recognise the errors that a trial balance in perfect balance can still hide.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Prepare a trial balance from ledger balances, listing accounts in chart order with each balance in its debit or credit column.
  • Explain why the two columns must agree, and what a balancing trial balance does and does not prove.
  • Identify errors a trial balance cannot detect: omissions, wrong accounts, reversals, compensating errors, and entries recorded twice or at the same wrong amount on both sides.
  • Locate an error from the difference between the column totals, using the halving test and the test for divisibility by 9.
  • Distinguish the unadjusted, adjusted and post-closing trial balances, and explain why none of them is a financial statement.

Review first

  • Posting to the general ledger — A trial balance lists ledger balances: you need to compute them and to follow posting references back to the journal.
  • Debits, credits and normal balances — You need the normal balance of each type of account to decide which column each balance belongs in.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

On 31 March Lantern Coffee Co.’s ledger holds sixteen accounts with balances. Before Ana Lim prepares March’s financial statements, she wants a quick test that nothing went wrong while fifteen journal entries became 31 postings. Checking every posting against the journal again would take an hour and could repeat the same slip. Instead she lists every account’s balance on one page, debit balances in one column and credit balances in another, and adds up both columns.

That list is a trial balance: every general ledger account with a balance, in chart-of-accounts order, each balance in its debit or its credit column, with both columns totalled. If every journal entry had equal debits and credits and every posting copied them faithfully, the two totals must agree. If they don’t, something went wrong between the journal and the list, and the size of the gap is the first clue to what.

Preparing a trial balance

  1. Work out the balance of every account in the ledger, on its normal balance side, as in the previous lesson.
  2. Head the page with the business’s name, the title and the date. A trial balance is a snapshot of the ledger at one moment, so it carries a single date, not a period.
  3. List the accounts in chart-of-accounts order, which for Lantern means by account number: assets, liabilities, equity and dividends, revenue, then expenses. Accounts with a zero balance are left out.
  4. Write each balance in the column of its side: debit balances on the left, credit balances on the right. Usually that is the account’s normal side; a balance on the other side goes in the other column, flagged for investigation.
  5. Add both columns and compare the totals.

Retained Earnings is missing because Lantern began on 1 March and has no profit from earlier periods. In later months it will appear with its balance at the start of the period, because the current period’s profit stays spread across the revenue and expense accounts until the books are closed.

Key idea: What the totals tell you

Equal totals show that the ledger’s debits equal its credits. They don’t show that the right transactions were recorded, in the right accounts, at the right amounts. A trial balance tests the arithmetic of double entry, not the accounting.

In depth

Why the columns must agree

The agreement is algebra, not luck. Every journal entry has equal debits and credits, and posting copies them, so the ledger’s debit postings and credit postings have the same total: for Lantern’s March, $61,110 each. What a balancing trial balance proves, and what it doesn’t

Equal totals prove three things: the debits posted equal the credits posted, each balance was worked out and placed in a column consistently, and the columns were added correctly. They prove nothing about whether the right things were recorded. A trial balance can balance and still contain errors, and some of the most expensive errors in accounting are exactly the ones it can’t see.

Bookkeeping courses in the British tradition give these undetectable errors standard names. The Cambridge International AS & A Level Accounting syllabus for 2026–2028 lists errors of omission, commission, principle and original entry, reversals and compensating errors, and ACCA’s study material defines all but the last in the same terms. The American textbooks cited in this lesson describe the same mistakes without the labels. Here they are with Lantern examples, each a hypothetical mistake in March’s records.

Error of omission
A transaction left out entirely. If the utility bill of 31 March had never been recorded, Utilities Expense and Accounts Payable would each be $260 too low, and both columns would total $46,600.
Error of commission
The right amount on the right side, but in the wrong account of the same type. Posting March rent to Utilities Expense leaves Rent Expense at zero and Utilities Expense at $1,460; both are debit balances, so the totals don’t move. Posting an invoice to the wrong customer’s account is the same error inside a subsidiary ledger.
Error of principle
The right side, but the wrong type of account, which breaks an accounting principle. Suppose a $400 repair to the espresso machine were debited to Equipment instead of Repairs Expense: assets would be $400 too high and expenses $400 too low, and the totals would still agree.
Error of original entry
A wrong amount in the journal, posted faithfully to both accounts. If the 20 March wages had been journalised as $1,060 instead of $1,600, Wages Expense would be $540 too low and Cash $540 too high. Both are debit balances, so the debit column would still total $46,860.
Reversal of entries
The right accounts and amount with the debit and credit swapped. Recording Meridian’s $3,000 payment as a debit to Accounts Receivable and a credit to Cash would leave Cash at $20,650 and Accounts Receivable at $7,200, and both columns at $46,860.
Compensating error
Two or more errors whose effects cancel. If Wages Expense were overstated by $100 and Catering Revenue were also overstated by $100, both columns would rise to $46,960 and still agree.
Duplicated entry
A whole entry recorded twice adds equal debits and credits again. Journalising the $350 advertising payment twice would leave Advertising Expense at $700 and Cash at $26,300, with the totals unchanged.

Every one of these leaves a debit for every credit, and that common thread gives you a test for any mistake you meet: if it keeps debits equal to credits, the trial balance can’t see it. Finding such errors needs other checks: reconciling Cash with the bank statement and each control account with its subsidiary ledger, comparing balances with what you expected, and following cross-references back to the source documents.

Using the difference to find the error

When the totals disagree, work through the checks in a fixed order, cheapest first. The sequence below follows the one in Edwards and Hermanson’s textbook (see the references), with one check, step 2, moved forward from its later list of causes:

  1. Re-add both columns. Addition slips are common and quick to rule out.
  2. Look for an amount equal to the difference: a balance missing from the list, or a journal line with a blank posting reference.
  3. Halve the difference and look for that amount on the wrong side. A $300 debit listed as a credit makes the debit column $300 short and the credit column $300 long, a gap of $600. Start by checking that every balance sits on its normal side.
  4. Divide the difference by 9. If it divides exactly, look for a transposition or a slide.
  5. If none of that finds it, work backwards: compare every trial balance figure with its ledger account, recompute each ledger balance, check each posting against the journal through its posting reference, and finally check that each journal entry balances.

Notice which column is short. If the debit column is the smaller one, the error either understated a debit or overstated a credit, which halves the number of places to look.

Why transpositions and slides leave a multiple of 9

A transposition error swaps two digits: 54 written as 45, or 1,600 as 1,060. Take a two-digit amount with tens digit aaa and units digit bbb. Its value is 10a+b10a + b10a+b; with the digits swapped it is 10b+a10b + a10b+a. The difference is

(10a+b)−(10b+a)=9a−9b=9(a−b)(10a + b) - (10b + a) = 9a - 9b = 9(a - b)(10a+b)−(10b+a)=9a−9b=9(a−b)

which is always a multiple of 9, and the multiplier, a−ba - ba−b, is the gap between the two swapped digits. When the pair sits further left, every place value is multiplied by 10, 100 or more, so the difference becomes 9(a−b)×10k9(a - b) \times 10^k9(a−b)×10k. Writing 1,600 as 1,060 swaps the 6 in the hundreds place with the 0 in the tens place: 9×(6−0)×10=5409 \times (6 - 0) \times 10 = 5409×(6−0)×10=540, and indeed 1,600−1,060=5401{,}600 - 1{,}060 = 5401,600−1,060=540.

A slide error puts the decimal point in the wrong place, so every digit shifts: $350 recorded as $35, or as $3,500. If the smaller figure is yyy, the larger is 10y10y10y and the difference is 10y−y=9y10y - y = 9y10y−y=9y. A slide of two places, $350 recorded as $3.50, gives 100y−y=99y100y - y = 99y100y−y=99y, also a multiple of 9.

A deeper reason covers every rearrangement of digits, not just neighbours. Each power of ten is one more than a multiple of 9 (10 = 9 + 1, 100 = 99 + 1, 1,000 = 999 + 1), so any whole number equals a multiple of 9 plus the sum of its digits. Two numbers made of the same digits have the same digit sum (1,600 and 1,060 both have 7), so they leave the same remainder when divided by 9, and their difference is a multiple of 9. Adding or removing zeros, which is what a slide does, doesn’t change the digit sum either. When amounts include cents, apply the test to the difference in cents: $350 against $3.50 differs by 34,650 cents, which is 9×3,8509 \times 3{,}8509×3,850.

Caution: A clue, not a proof

One whole number in every nine is a multiple of 9, so a difference divisible by 9 can come from an error that has nothing to do with digits, and every even difference passes the halving test. Two errors at once produce a difference that may match neither. Use each test to decide where to look first, and confirm by finding the actual mistake.

A trial balance is not a financial statement

A trial balance looks official, with a heading, a date and double-underlined totals, but it is an internal working document. It mixes accounts that belong on different statements: assets and liabilities from the balance sheet, revenue and expenses from the income statement, dividends from the statement of changes in equity. It has no subtotals, doesn’t show net income, and doesn’t separate current from noncurrent items. Its jobs are to check the ledger and to feed the statements; turning Lantern’s adjusted trial balance into financial statements is the subject of lesson 10.

Common mistakes

  • “The trial balance balances, so the books are right.”

    Agreement proves only that debits equal credits. A transaction never recorded, rent posted to utilities, a repair added to equipment, an entry made twice or a wrong amount on both sides all leave the totals equal. Balancing is necessary, not sufficient.

  • “Dividends are an expense, so they belong with the expenses.”

    Dividends do sit in the debit column, next to the expenses, but they are a distribution of profit to shareholders, not a cost of earning revenue. They never enter net income; they reduce retained earnings. Grouping them with expenses would understate Lantern’s March profit by $300.

  • “A contra account is listed with its asset, so it goes in the debit column.”

    A contra account has the opposite normal balance to the account it reduces. Lantern’s adjusted trial balance will include Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment with a credit balance of $200; it belongs in the credit column even though it is listed among the assets.

  • “Retained Earnings on the trial balance includes this period’s profit.”

    Until the books are closed, the period’s revenue and expenses sit in their own accounts. The Retained Earnings figure on an unadjusted or adjusted trial balance is the balance at the start of the period; Lantern’s is zero in its first month.

  • “A difference divisible by 9 proves a transposition.”

    It is a lead, not proof. One whole number in nine is a multiple of 9 anyway, and a slide, or two unrelated errors, can produce one too. Confirm by finding the figure that doesn’t match the ledger.

  • “The trial balance is the balance sheet.”

    A trial balance lists every account, including revenue, expenses and dividends, with no classification or subtotals. It is a working document that feeds the balance sheet, the income statement and the statement of changes in equity; it isn’t any of them.

In practice

In accounting software a trial balance is a report you can run for any date, and it always balances: as ACCA’s study material for its Financial Accounting exam notes, a computerised system will usually refuse to post an unbalanced journal entry. The error-hunting skills of this lesson still matter wherever figures are kept by hand or in spreadsheets, wherever balances are copied from one system into another, and in professional exams. Reviewing a trial balance that balances matters everywhere.

An experienced accountant reads a trial balance the way a doctor reads a set of test results, looking for anything outside the expected range. A typical review asks four questions:

  • Is every balance on its normal side? A credit balance in Cash means an overdraft; a debit balance in Accounts Payable means a supplier was overpaid or a payment was misposted; a debit balance in a revenue account means refunds exceed sales or something is wrong.
  • Does every balance make sense in size? Compare with last month, with the budget, and with what you know happened. Lantern’s Supplies shows $1,500, the cost of everything bought in March, though Ana knows much of it has been used. That isn’t an error; it is a sign that an adjustment is due (lesson 9).
  • Are the accounts that should be empty, empty? A suspense account, used to hold amounts while they are investigated, should be cleared before statements are prepared.
  • Do the control accounts agree with their subsidiary ledgers, and does Cash agree with the bank? These reconciliations catch many of the errors a trial balance can’t.

Scale doesn’t change the logic. An airline or a university may have thousands of accounts, extracted from the general ledger by software, and its finance team still starts the month-end review by scanning the trial balance for abnormal and unexpected balances before anything goes into the financial statements. External auditors usually start from the client’s trial balance too, because it lists every account they may need to test and links the ledger to the published statements.

For a small business owner the trial balance is the last checkpoint before the numbers leave the building. If Ana applies to Coastline Bank for the van loan she is considering (lesson 3), the bank will judge Lantern on statements built from its trial balance. A repair hidden in Equipment, or a supplier bill missing from Accounts Payable, would make Lantern look stronger than it is, and the trial balance’s agreement wouldn’t have warned her. The review would.

Summary

  • A trial balance lists every ledger account with a balance, in chart order, each balance in its debit or credit column, at one date. The two columns must total the same.
  • They agree because every entry posts equal debits and credits. Lantern’s unadjusted trial balance at 31 March totals $46,860 on each side.
  • Equal totals test the arithmetic of double entry, not the accounting: omissions, commission and principle errors, reversals, duplicated entries, wrong amounts on both sides and compensating errors all pass.
  • Unequal totals point to one-sided errors. Look for the difference itself, for half of it on the wrong side, and, if it divides by 9, for a transposition or a slide.
  • Transpositions and slides leave multiples of 9 because numbers with the same digits have the same digit sum. The test is a clue, not proof.
  • Unadjusted, adjusted and post-closing trial balances are prepared at three points in the cycle. None is a financial statement.
  • In software the trial balance always balances, so its value lies in review: abnormal sides, unexpected sizes, uncleared holding accounts and unreconciled control accounts.

Terms introduced

Trial balance
A list of every general-ledger account with a balance at a given date, each balance in its debit or credit column, with both columns totalled to check that they agree.
Unadjusted trial balance
A trial balance prepared after the period’s transactions are posted and before the end-of-period adjusting entries.
Transposition error
An error in which two digits of an amount are swapped, such as 1,600 written as 1,060. When it makes the totals differ, the difference is a multiple of 9.
Slide error
An error in which the decimal point is misplaced, so every digit shifts, such as $350 written as $35. When it makes the totals differ, the difference is a multiple of 9.
Compensating error
Two or more errors whose effects on the debit and credit totals cancel out, so the trial balance still balances.
Error of omission
A transaction left out of the records entirely. Both sides are missing, so the trial balance still balances.
Error of commission
An amount recorded on the correct side but in the wrong account of the same type, such as rent posted to utilities expense or an invoice posted to the wrong customer.
Error of principle
An amount recorded on the correct side but in the wrong type of account, such as a repair (an expense) debited to equipment (an asset).
Error of original entry
A wrong amount recorded in the journal and posted to both accounts, so debits still equal credits.
Reversal of entries
An error in which the correct accounts and amount are used but the debit and credit are swapped.
