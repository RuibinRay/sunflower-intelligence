The complete accounting cycle

Every period, every business runs the same nine steps, from analysing a transaction to proving that the ledger is ready for the next period. See the whole cycle at once, use the optional worksheet and reversing entries, correct errors after posting, and protect the records with internal control.

About 85 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • List the nine steps of the accounting cycle in order, and say which happen during the period and which at its end.
  • Record reversing entries for accruals and show that they lead to the same balances as recording without them.
  • Record correcting entries for errors found after posting: a wrong account, a wrong amount and an omitted entry.
  • Explain what internal control is, and apply segregation of duties, authorisation, documentation, physical safeguards and reconciliations to a small business.
  • Apply the whole cycle to a small business, from its first transaction to its post-closing trial balance.
  • Distinguish the steps that accounting software automates from the judgements it cannot make.

Review first

  • Closing entries — Closing and the post-closing trial balance are the last two steps of the cycle.
  • Adjusting entries — Reversing entries undo accrual adjustments, so you need to know how accruals are recorded.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern’s first month is complete. Since 1 March, Ana and her accountant have analysed fifteen transactions, recorded them in the journal, posted them to the ledger, tested the ledger with a trial balance, made seven adjustments, prepared the financial statements, closed the temporary accounts and proved the result with a post-closing trial balance. On 1 April the sequence begins again: the same steps, in the same order.

That sequence is the accounting cycle: the steps a business repeats every accounting period to turn its transactions into financial statements and to make its ledger ready for the next period. You have learned each step in its own lesson. This one puts them together, adds the optional tools that support them, and asks what keeps the whole process honest.

Nine steps, in order

  1. Analyse transactions. Decide from the source document whether an event is a transaction, which accounts it affects and by how much (lessons 3 and 5).
  2. Journalise. Record each transaction as a balanced journal entry, in date order (lesson 5).
  3. Post. Transfer each journal line to its account in the general ledger (lesson 6).
  4. Prepare an unadjusted trial balance. List every ledger balance and confirm that debits equal credits (lesson 7).
  5. Adjust. Record and post adjusting entries for deferrals, accruals and depreciation, so that revenue and expenses fall in the right period (lesson 9).
  6. Prepare an adjusted trial balance. The balances after adjustment, and the source for the statements (lesson 9).
  7. Prepare the financial statements. The income statement, statement of changes in equity, balance sheet and statement of cash flows (lesson 10).
  8. Close. Record and post the closing entries (lesson 11).
  9. Prepare a post-closing trial balance. Confirm that only permanent accounts remain and that they balance (lesson 11).

Steps 1 to 3 happen throughout the period, every time something happens: a sale, a purchase, a payment. Steps 4 to 9 happen once, at the end of the period, in a burst of work that accountants call the close. Between steps 4 and 7 some accountants use a worksheet, and on the first day of the new period some record reversing entries. Both are optional, and both are explained below; some textbooks, OpenStax among them, count reversing entries as an optional tenth step.

Why the order matters

Each step uses the output of the one before. The statements are prepared from the adjusted trial balance, so adjusting must come first: prepared from the unadjusted balances, Lantern’s income statement would have reported net income of $2,290 instead of $990. Closing empties the revenue and expense accounts, so it must come after the statements: close first, and there is nothing left to report.

The three trial balances are checkpoints, placed where errors are cheapest to find. The unadjusted trial balance tests the period’s posting before adjustments are piled on top. The adjusted trial balance tests the adjustments before anything is published. The post-closing trial balance tests the closing before the next period builds on it. An error caught at its own checkpoint is traced through a handful of entries; the same error found months later has to be traced through hundreds.

Key idea: Three trial balances, three questions

Unadjusted: did the period’s entries post in balance? Adjusted: are the balances ready to report? Post-closing: is the ledger ready for the next period, with only permanent accounts open? Each answers its question about arithmetic only. None proves that the right accounts and amounts were used.

In depth

The worksheet (optional)

A worksheet is an internal working paper, today usually a spreadsheet, that lays the end-of-period steps side by side. The traditional ten-column worksheet has a debit and a credit column for each of five stages: the unadjusted trial balance, the adjustments, the adjusted trial balance, the income statement and the balance sheet. Each account’s balance is carried across the page to the statement it belongs on. The worksheet is not a financial statement and is never published, and using one is optional. It helps an accountant plan the adjustments and draft the statements before any adjusting entry is journalised.

Adjustments are shown as increases (+) or decreases (−) in each account’s normal balance. A ten-column worksheet has no columns for the statement of changes in equity, so Dividends is carried to the balance sheet columns.

The worksheet also produces net income before any statement is written. Across all of Lantern’s accounts, the income statement columns hold $6,110 of debits (the expenses) and $7,100 of credits (the revenue); the difference, $990, is net income. The balance sheet columns differ by the same amount: $43,000 of debits (assets of $42,700 before accumulated depreciation is deducted, plus dividends of $300) against $42,010 of credits (accumulated depreciation, liabilities and common stock). Adding the $990 to the credit side balances them, because the period’s profit is the one change in equity that has not yet reached the balance sheet accounts. Closing is what puts it there.

Reversing entries (optional)

A reversing entry is made on the first day of a new period and is the exact opposite of an adjusting entry made at the end of the previous one. It is optional and is normally used for accruals (accrued expenses and accrued revenues), which a later payment or receipt will settle. Its purpose is convenience: whoever records that later payment can do it the routine way, without having to know that part of it was accrued last period.

Take Lantern’s accrued wages. At 31 March, adjustment a5 recorded $1,400 of wages that the baristas earned on 16–31 March, to be paid on 5 April: debit Wages Expense, credit Wages Payable. Closing then took Wages Expense to zero. There are two ways to record the 5 April payroll, and they end in the same place.

April’s wages expense would then be 1,400+1,500+1,600=4,5001{,}400 + 1{,}500 + 1{,}600 = 4{,}5001,400+1,500+1,600=4,500, and Wages Payable would still include $1,400 that nobody is owed.

Reverse only accruals that a later cash payment or receipt will settle. Reversing depreciation, or the expiry of prepaid insurance, would simply cancel a correct adjustment, because no later transaction will record that expense again.

Correcting entries

Errors are found at every stage: when a trial balance doesn’t balance, when a reconciliation fails, when a reviewer asks why an account looks odd. Once an entry has been posted, it is not erased or overwritten. Instead the accountant records a correcting entry, a new journal entry that brings the accounts to the balances they would have had if the original entry had been right. This preserves the audit trail: anyone can see what was recorded, when it was fixed and why.

One method works for every correction. Write the entry that was made, write the entry that should have been made, and record the difference, account by account. Lines that were right in both drop out. Three errors Lantern could have made in March show the patterns. Each is hypothetical, and each is supposed to be found during the review of the unadjusted trial balance, before the adjusting entries.

  • Wrong account. Suppose the $350 for March advertising (10 March) had been debited to Prepaid Advertising instead of Advertising Expense. Cash was right; only the debit was misplaced. The correction moves it: debit Advertising Expense 350, credit Prepaid Advertising 350. Uncorrected, the error would overstate both assets and net income by $350.
  • Wrong amount. Suppose the $1,200 rent payment (28 March) had been keyed as $2,100 on both lines. Rent Expense and the cash paid are each $900 too large, so the correction debits Cash 900 and credits Rent Expense 900. The trial balance could not catch this error, because both sides were wrong by the same amount; the bank reconciliation would. Notice that 900 is divisible by 9, the signature of two transposed digits.
  • Omitted entry. Suppose the $260 utilities bill (31 March) had never been recorded. Nothing needs undoing: the correction is simply the missing entry, debit Utilities Expense 260, credit Accounts Payable 260, with a memo explaining why it is late.

If the journal entry was right and only the posting went astray, for example a correct amount posted to the wrong ledger account, a manual system fixes the ledger rather than adding a journal entry. In accounting software posting is automatic, so posting errors of that kind don’t arise; errors, and their corrections, are in the entries themselves.

Standards: Errors in statements already issued

Correcting entries fix errors found before the statements are issued. An error discovered in financial statements that have already been issued is treated differently. Under IFRS (IAS 8) a material error from a prior period is corrected retrospectively, by restating the comparative figures; US GAAP (ASC 250) likewise corrects material errors in previously issued statements by restating them. The correction does not pass through the current period’s profit. An immaterial error from an earlier period is usually corrected in the current period instead.

Internal control

The cycle produces reliable numbers only if the entries going into it are genuine, complete and accurate. Internal control is the set of processes an organisation uses to make sure that happens, and more broadly to protect its assets, comply with the law and run its operations well. A widely used framework is published by COSO, the Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission. First issued in 1992 and updated in 2013, it defines internal control in one sentence:

Internal control is a process, effected by an entity’s board of directors, management, and other personnel, designed to provide reasonable assurance regarding the achievement of objectives relating to operations, reporting, and compliance.

COSO, Internal Control — Integrated Framework: Executive Summary (May 2013)

Three parts of that definition matter. Internal control is a process carried out by people, not a binder of rules on a shelf. It gives reasonable assurance, not a guarantee: two employees who collude, or a manager who overrides a control, can defeat it, and some controls would cost more than the losses they prevent. And it serves three kinds of objective at once: running operations well, reporting reliably and complying with laws and regulations.

COSO organises internal control into five components: the control environment, the tone set by owners and managers and their commitment to integrity; risk assessment, working out what could go wrong; control activities, the specific checks; information and communication, getting the right information to the right people; and monitoring activities, checking that the controls keep working. The 2013 framework sets out seventeen principles under these five components.

In a small business, the control activities are where the framework becomes concrete:

  • Segregation of duties. No one person should handle every stage of a transaction: authorising it, recording it and holding the asset involved. If the person who receives customers’ cash also records it and reconciles the bank account, they can take cash and hide the shortfall. Split those tasks and a theft needs two people.
  • Authorisation. Transactions are approved by someone with the authority to approve them, within set limits: for example, the owner approves every purchase over $500 before it is ordered.
  • Documentation. Every entry is supported by a source document, and documents such as invoices and receipts are prenumbered, so that a missing one is noticed.
  • Physical safeguards. Cash, stock and equipment are locked away; computer records are protected by passwords and backed up.
  • Reconciliations and independent checks. The records are compared with an independent source: the ledger’s cash with the bank statement, Accounts Payable with suppliers’ statements, the Supplies account with a count. Lantern’s supplies adjustment (a2) began with exactly such a count.

Standards: Internal control in US public companies

In the United States, section 404 of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002, through the rules of the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), requires each public company’s annual report to include management’s assessment of its internal control over financial reporting. Larger companies (accelerated and large accelerated filers) must also include their auditor’s attestation report on it; the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 exempted smaller companies from the auditor’s part, but not from management’s assessment.

What software automates, and what still needs judgement

Accounting software has absorbed most of the mechanical steps. Posting happens the moment an entry is saved, so the journal and ledger cannot disagree. Trial balances and statements are reports, produced on demand. Year-end closing happens automatically. Bank feeds import transactions and suggest how to record them, and recurring entries such as monthly depreciation can be scheduled. What remains is the part that was always the real work: judgement.

  • Classification. Is a $4,000 overhaul of an oven a repair expense, or an improvement that extends its life and belongs in Equipment? Is a payment to the owner a dividend, a salary or a loan? Software records whatever account it is given.
  • Estimates. Depreciation needs a useful life and a residual value, and later courses add estimates such as the customers who won’t pay. These are forecasts, made by people and reviewed by auditors.
  • Cut-off. Which period does a transaction belong to? A breakfast catered on 31 March but invoiced on 2 April is March revenue (Lantern’s a7). Software uses whatever date it is given; choosing the right period is a judgement.
  • Completeness and existence. Is every transaction in the records, and is every recorded transaction real? A balanced ledger can still leave out a bill or include a payment to a supplier who doesn’t exist. Controls, reconciliations and review answer that question; arithmetic can’t.

Common mistakes

  • “The trial balance balances, so the books are right.”

    A trial balance tests arithmetic only. Lantern’s rent keyed as $2,100 on both lines, advertising debited to the wrong account and a bill never recorded all leave it in balance. Reconciliations, source documents and review find these errors; the trial balance can’t.

  • “Reversing entries change the next period’s results.”

    They change only how the later payment is recorded. With or without the reversal, Lantern’s April wages expense is $3,100 and Wages Payable at 30 April is $1,600. A reversing entry is optional; what it removes is the need for the payroll clerk to know about the accrual.

  • “Any adjustment can be reversed.”

    Reverse only accruals that a later payment or receipt will settle. Reversing depreciation or the expiry of prepaid insurance would cancel a correct expense, because no later transaction will record it again.

  • “Fix a mistake by deleting the entry.”

    In a manual journal, a posted entry is never erased: the correction is a new, dated entry with a memo, so the audit trail shows what happened. Software may let you edit an entry in an open period, but it keeps a history of the change, and a locked period should be corrected only by a documented correcting entry.

  • “Internal control is about catching thieves.”

    Fraud is one risk, but most of what controls catch is honest error: a transposed figure, a bill entered twice, an expense coded to the wrong account. Controls aim at reliable reporting and well-run operations as well as safe assets, and they give reasonable assurance, not certainty. A three-person business can’t separate every duty, so it relies more on the owner’s review.

  • “The software does the accounting.”

    Software posts, totals, reports and closes. It doesn’t decide whether a purchase is equipment or an expense, how long equipment will last, which period a sale belongs to, or whether a supplier is real. Those judgements are the accountant’s, and errors in them pass straight through a perfectly balanced ledger.

In practice

Starting April at Lantern

On 1 April, Ana’s accountant opens the new period from the post-closing trial balance: thirteen permanent balances, with every revenue, expense and dividend account at zero. Lantern doesn’t use reversing entries: on 5 April it will debit Wages Payable, and on 2 April the Pier Nine invoice records nothing new, as lesson 9 showed. But suppose that before the first April transaction the accountant considered reversing March’s accruals. Two of them will be settled by ordinary April transactions: the wages (a5), paid on 5 April, and the Pier Nine breakfast (a7), invoiced on 2 April. Reversing a7 would mean crediting Accounts Receivable and debiting Catering Revenue for $600 on 1 April, so that the invoice could be recorded as a normal credit sale on 2 April. If Ana took on a part-time bookkeeper who didn’t know March’s history, reversing both would be the safer choice.

Controls for a growing business

Suppose that, as Lantern grows, Ana does hire the bookkeeper. For the first time someone other than her will record transactions. With three people, full segregation of duties is impossible, so she designs controls that fit the business: each task goes to someone who can do it without also being able to hide a mistake or a theft in it.

The aim is reasonable assurance at a sensible cost, which is exactly what COSO’s definition asks for. The controls also make the bookkeeper’s work easier to trust, which matters when a lender asks to see the statements.

Summary

  • The accounting cycle has nine steps: analyse, journalise, post, unadjusted trial balance, adjust, adjusted trial balance, statements, close, post-closing trial balance. Steps 1–3 happen throughout the period; steps 4–9 at its end.
  • The order matters: statements come from adjusted balances, and closing comes after the statements. Each trial balance is a checkpoint on arithmetic, not on correctness.
  • A worksheet is an optional working paper that carries each balance from the trial balance to the statements; its income statement columns differ by net income.
  • A reversing entry, made on the first day of a new period, is the exact opposite of an accrual adjustment. It is optional, changes no result, and lets the later payment or receipt be recorded the routine way.
  • A correcting entry is the difference between the entry that should have been made and the entry that was made. Posted entries are never erased; errors in statements already issued are corrected by restatement.
  • Internal control is a process giving reasonable assurance about operations, reporting and compliance. COSO’s five components are the control environment, risk assessment, control activities, information and communication, and monitoring.
  • Key control activities: segregation of duties, authorisation, documentation, physical safeguards, and reconciliations and independent checks.
  • Software automates posting, trial balances, statements and closing; classification, estimates, cut-off and completeness still need judgement.

Terms introduced

Accounting cycle
The sequence of steps a business repeats every accounting period, from analysing transactions to preparing a post-closing trial balance.
Worksheet
An optional internal working paper, usually a spreadsheet, that carries each account from the trial balance through the adjustments to the income statement and balance sheet. It is not a financial statement.
Reversing entry
An optional entry made on the first day of a new period that is the exact opposite of an accrual adjustment made at the end of the previous period, so that the later payment or receipt can be recorded in the routine way.
Correcting entry
A journal entry that fixes an error in an entry already posted, by recording the difference between what was recorded and what should have been.
Internal control
The processes, carried out by an organisation’s board, managers and staff, that give reasonable assurance that its operations run well, its reporting is reliable and it complies with laws and regulations.
Segregation of duties
An internal control that divides the authorisation, recording and custody of assets for a transaction among different people, so that no one person can both commit and conceal an error or a theft.
