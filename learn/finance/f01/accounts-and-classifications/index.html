Accounts, terminology and classifications

An account is where a business keeps every increase and decrease in one item. Learn the five kinds of account, how accounts are grouped as current or noncurrent and as permanent or temporary, and how a chart of accounts organises them.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain what an account is and why a business keeps a separate account for each item it tracks.
  • Classify accounts as assets, liabilities, equity, revenue or expenses, using the definitions in the FASB and IASB conceptual frameworks.
  • Classify assets and liabilities as current or noncurrent, and accounts as permanent or temporary.
  • Explain how a contra account works, using accumulated depreciation as the example.
  • Read and extend a chart of accounts, recognising the US and IFRS or UK names for the same items.
  • Justify the classification of borderline items such as prepaid insurance, unearned revenue and the current portion of a loan.

Review first

  • What accounting is and why businesses need it — You need the idea of accounting as a record of a business’s own economic events, measured in money and reported for periods (the economic entity, monetary unit and time period assumptions).

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

By the evening of 8 March, Lantern Coffee Co. is a week old and has completed six transactions: Ana’s $20,000 investment, the $10,000 bank loan, the $12,000 equipment purchase, the $1,800 insurance policy, $1,500 of supplies bought on account and Harbor Tech’s $2,400 deposit. Ana could keep these as a diary, one line per event. But a diary is hard to question. When the bank asks how much cash Lantern has, or the equipment supplier asks how much it is still owed, Ana would have to reread every line and add up the right ones. Lantern needs a better way to store the same events.

The better way is to give every item the business needs to track a record of its own. An account is a record of all the increases and decreases in one item, such as cash, equipment or the amount owed to suppliers, together with the balance that results. Each event is entered in every account it affects, so the diary’s six lines become entries spread across eight accounts, and any balance can be read at a glance. Here is Lantern’s first account, Cash, for its first week.

Lantern’s Cash account, 1–8 March (fictional)
DateEventIncrease ($)Decrease ($)Balance ($)
1 MarAna invests in exchange for common stock20,00020,000
1 MarLoan from Coastline Bank10,00030,000
3 MarCash part of the equipment purchase4,00026,000

Three features of this record carry through every account you will meet. It has a name that says exactly what it tracks. It keeps increases apart from decreases, so you can see the whole history, not just the latest figure. And it has a balance: the starting amount plus the increases minus the decreases. The supplies bought on 5 March do not appear here, because they were bought on account and no cash moved; that event lives in the Supplies and Accounts Payable accounts instead. In lesson 4 this table becomes the accountant’s standard format, the T-account, and the words increase and decrease give way to debit and credit. The idea stays the same.

Five kinds of account

Every account belongs to one of five types, called the elements of financial statements. Three describe the business’s position at a moment; two describe its performance over a period.

  • Assets are resources the business controls that are expected to bring it economic benefits: Cash, Accounts Receivable (amounts customers owe the business), Supplies, Prepaid Insurance, Equipment.
  • Liabilities are present obligations to transfer resources to others: Accounts Payable (amounts owed to suppliers), Notes Payable (a loan backed by a written promise to repay), Wages Payable, and Unearned Revenue (customers’ advance payments for work not yet done).
  • Equity is the owners’ residual claim: what would be left of the assets if every liability were settled. In a corporation such as Lantern it is kept in accounts such as Common Stock, what the owners paid in for their shares, and Retained Earnings, the profit kept in the business.
  • Revenue is the increase in the business’s resources from doing what it exists to do: delivering goods or services to customers. Lantern has Catering Revenue and Training Revenue.
  • Expenses are the resources used up, or obligations incurred, in earning revenue: Wages Expense, Rent Expense, Supplies Expense, Advertising Expense.

Revenue and expenses are really equity seen over a period. Earning revenue increases what the owners have a claim on; incurring an expense decreases it. Keeping them in accounts of their own during the period lets the business report how equity changed, which is the job of the income statement. One more type of account sits alongside them: Dividends, which records distributions of profit to the shareholders. Dividends reduce equity, as expenses do, but they are not expenses: they are not a cost of earning revenue but a payment to the owners out of what has been earned. Lesson 3 develops this.

Key idea: Position or performance?

Assets, liabilities and equity describe where the business stands at a moment, so their balances carry forward from one period to the next. Revenue, expenses and dividends describe what happened during a period, so each period they start again from zero. That single distinction drives the split between permanent and temporary accounts later in this lesson.

Grouping accounts for readers

Knowing an account’s element is the first step. Readers also want to know when assets will turn into cash and when liabilities must be paid, so assets and liabilities are split further. A current asset is cash, or an asset the business expects to turn into cash, sell or use up within one year (or within its operating cycle, if that is longer): Cash, Accounts Receivable, Supplies, Prepaid Insurance. A noncurrent asset is any other asset, typically one the business will use for several years: equipment, a van, a building, land. A current liability is an obligation due within the same period, such as Accounts Payable, Wages Payable or Unearned Revenue for events next month. A noncurrent liability falls due later, such as most of a multi-year bank loan.

This split answers one of a lender’s first questions: can the business meet the obligations falling due soon out of the resources it will have soon? At 31 March, after its month-end adjustments, Lantern’s current assets are $30,700 and its current liabilities $11,810, so it has about $2.60 of current assets for every dollar it must pay within the year. The section “In practice” shows what the bank makes of that.

In depth

What the standard setters say

The plain-language definitions above follow the conceptual frameworks of the FASB and the IASB, which define the elements precisely so that every standard uses them in the same way. Their wording differs in places that matter.

The elements as the standard setters define them (paraphrased, except the FASB’s asset definition)
ElementFASB, Concepts Statement No. 8, Chapter 4 (December 2021)IASB, Conceptual Framework (March 2018)
Asset“A present right of an entity to an economic benefit.”An economic resource that the entity controls now, as a result of past events; the resource is a right that could produce economic benefits.
LiabilityAn obligation, existing now, to transfer an economic benefit to others.An obligation, existing now as a result of past events, to transfer an economic resource; an obligation is a duty the entity has no practical ability to avoid.
EquityThe interest in the assets that remains after deducting the liabilities (also called net assets).The same idea: the residual interest in the assets after deducting all the liabilities.
Revenue (FASB) / Income (IASB)Revenues: inflows or other increases in assets, or settlements of liabilities, that come from delivering or producing goods, rendering services or carrying out other activities.Income: increases in assets or decreases in liabilities that increase equity, other than contributions from the owners. Revenue is income arising from the entity’s ordinary activities (IFRS 15).
ExpensesOutflows or other using up of assets, or the incurring of liabilities, from delivering or producing goods, rendering services or carrying out other activities.Decreases in assets or increases in liabilities that decrease equity, other than distributions to the owners.

Three differences are worth noticing. First, the IASB defines an asset through control; the FASB’s 2021 wording leaves the word out, though its explanation still requires that the entity can obtain the benefit and restrict others’ access to it. Either way, a loyal customer base or a talented barista is not an asset in the accounting sense. Second, the IASB’s element is income, which covers both revenue from ordinary activities and other gains; the FASB defines revenues and gains separately and says that which one an inflow is depends largely on the nature of the business and the activity involved. Selling coffee catering is revenue for Lantern; selling its old grinder for more than its recorded amount would be a gain. Third, the IASB writes the residual nature of equity into the other definitions: income and expenses are changes in equity other than those from the owners’ contributions or distributions.

That last point is why dividends are not an expense under either framework. The IASB excludes distributions to holders of equity claims from expenses by definition, and the FASB defines distributions to owners as a separate kind of change in equity. A dividend reduces equity because the owners take resources out, not because the business used anything up in earning revenue.

Current and noncurrent: the rules

Under US GAAP, current assets are cash and other assets reasonably expected to be turned into cash, sold or consumed during the business’s normal operating cycle. The operating cycle is the average time between acquiring the materials or services a business works with and finally collecting cash from its customers. Where several cycles fit into a year, as at Lantern (buy beans, cater an event, collect the invoice, all within about a month), one year is used instead. Where the cycle is longer than twelve months, as the US standards note for the tobacco, distillery and lumber businesses, the longer cycle is used. Hence the usual summary: one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer. Current liabilities are obligations expected to be settled out of current assets or by creating other current liabilities, which in practice means those due within the same period.

IFRS reaches almost the same place by a slightly different route. An asset is current if the entity expects to realise, sell or consume it in its normal operating cycle, holds it mainly for trading, expects to realise it within twelve months after the reporting date, or it is cash not restricted for at least twelve months; every other asset is noncurrent. A liability is current if it will be settled in the normal operating cycle, is held mainly for trading, is due within twelve months after the reporting date, or the entity has no right at that date to defer settlement for at least twelve months. IFRS also allows a business to present all its assets and liabilities in order of liquidity instead, when that is more useful, as it often is for banks.

Standards: Where the IFRS rules live

The criteria above are in IAS 1 Presentation of Financial Statements. IFRS 18 Presentation and Disclosure in Financial Statements, issued in April 2024, replaces IAS 1 for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027 and carries the current and noncurrent criteria forward (paragraphs 99–101). US balance sheets conventionally list current assets in order of liquidity, cash first, which is also the order of Lantern’s chart.

Contra accounts

A contra account is an account paired with another account whose balance it reduces. The two are reported together, with the contra balance subtracted from its partner. The most common example is accumulated depreciation. When Lantern bought its espresso machine and grinder, it recorded them at their $12,000 cost in Equipment. Each month a slice of that cost is treated as used up; lesson 9 calculates the slice, which for Lantern is $200 a month. Instead of reducing the Equipment account, Lantern adds each slice to a separate account, Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment. At 31 March the balance sheet shows both: Equipment $12,000, less accumulated depreciation $200, giving a net amount of $11,800.

Why not cut Equipment to $11,800 directly? Because the two figures tell a reader different things. The cost shows how much was invested; the accumulated depreciation shows how much of that investment has been used up, which hints at how old the equipment is and when it may need replacing. Keeping them apart also keeps the original cost on record for when the asset is eventually sold or scrapped. A contra account keeps its own balance, moves in the opposite direction from its partner (lesson 4 shows that this means it carries its balance on the opposite side), and belongs to its partner’s group: Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment is a noncurrent contra-asset because Equipment is noncurrent. Later courses meet other contra accounts, such as an allowance for doubtful accounts, which reduces accounts receivable by the amount not expected to be collected, and sales returns and allowances, which reduce revenue.

Permanent and temporary accounts

A permanent account is one whose balance carries forward from one period to the next. Every asset, liability and equity account is permanent, and so is every contra-asset account: accumulated depreciation keeps growing, month after month, until the asset is disposed of. Permanent accounts are also called real accounts. A temporary account collects one period’s activity and is then emptied, so that it starts the next period at zero. Every revenue and expense account is temporary, and so is Dividends. Temporary accounts are also called nominal accounts.

At the end of each period the temporary accounts are closed: their balances are transferred into Retained Earnings, the permanent equity account where accumulated profit lives. Lantern’s chart also contains Income Summary, a temporary account used only during that closing process. Lesson 11 teaches the closing entries; for now the point is classification. The reason for the split is simple. Revenue for April must not include March’s revenue, but cash at 1 April is the same cash as at 31 March: performance is measured period by period, while position accumulates.

The chart of accounts

A chart of accounts is the list of every account a business uses, each with a name and usually a number, arranged by type. It is the business’s vocabulary: it makes sure the same kind of event is always recorded in the same account, whoever records it, so that balances can be added up and statements prepared automatically. Here is Lantern’s chart, walked through by group.

The first digit tells you the type: 1 for assets, 2 liabilities, 3 equity, 4 revenue, 5 expenses. Within each type, the numbers leave gaps (101, 110, 112, 115, 120…) so that new accounts can be slotted into the right place later. The chart also lists accounts Lantern does not use in March, such as Van, Land and Building: a chart holds the accounts a business expects to need, and grows when it needs more.

This numbering is a common convention, not a rule. Neither US GAAP nor IFRS prescribes account names or numbers. Many businesses use four or more digits, give cost of sales and operating expenses separate ranges, or add segments to each code for a department, location or product line, so that a hotel chain can see the rooms revenue of one hotel as easily as of the whole group. What the standards do govern is how accounts are classified and presented in the statements.

Same item, different names

This course uses US account names, as Lantern does. Under IFRS, and in UK company law and practice, several of the same items go by other names. The names change nothing about classification: trade receivables are current assets whatever they are called.

Judgement calls

Most accounts classify themselves. A few need thought, and they are the ones that students, and sometimes businesses, get wrong. In each case the test is the same: what right or obligation exists at the reporting date, and when will it turn into cash, be used up or be settled?

  • Prepaid insurance is an asset, not an expense. On 4 March Lantern paid $1,800 for cover running from 1 March 2026 to 28 February 2027. On that day it holds a right to twelve months of protection, an economic benefit it controls, so the payment creates an asset, Prepaid Insurance. As each month passes, a twelfth of the cover expires and becomes Insurance Expense: $150 a month. Because the policy runs for twelve months, all of it is current. If a business paid for three years of cover in advance, then by the same definitions the part covering the months beyond the next twelve would be noncurrent.
  • Unearned revenue is a liability, not revenue. Harbor Tech’s $2,400 deposit arrives before Lantern has catered any of the three $800 events. Lantern now owes Harbor Tech either the events or the money back: a present obligation, so a liability. Each event catered turns $800 of the liability into revenue, the first on 26 March. The events all fall within the next two months, so the liability is current.
  • Equipment or supplies? Both are things Lantern buys to use, but they are classified by how long they last. Beans, milk, cups and lids are used up within weeks: they are Supplies, a current asset that becomes Supplies Expense as it is used. The espresso machine and grinder will serve for about five years: they are Equipment, a noncurrent asset whose cost is spread over those years. Between the two lies a grey zone of cheap but durable items, such as a milk jug or a tamper. Tracking each for years would cost more than the information is worth, so most businesses set a policy, often a minimum cost below which durable items are expensed when bought: materiality and the cost constraint from lesson 1 at work.
  • The current portion of a long-term loan. Classification follows the dates on which each part of an obligation falls due, not the loan’s original length. Lantern’s $10,000 note runs for two years from 1 March 2026, with interest paid every three months and the principal repayable when the note matures on 1 March 2028, so at 31 March 2026 the whole $10,000 is noncurrent. A year later, at 31 March 2027, it will be due within twelve months and will have become current. Suppose instead the bank had required $5,000 back on 1 March 2027 and $5,000 on 1 March 2028. Then at 31 March 2026 the balance sheet would show a current liability of $5,000 and a noncurrent liability of $5,000. Showing the whole loan as noncurrent would hide a large payment due within the year.
  • Future interest is not a liability yet. Over its two years the note will cost $1,200 of interest (10,000×6%×210{,}000 \times 6\% \times 210,000×6%×2). At 31 March Lantern owes only March’s interest, $50, because only March has passed; the obligation for April’s interest arises in April, as Lantern goes on using the bank’s money. Liabilities are present obligations, not every payment a business expects to make.

Common mistakes

  • “Unearned revenue is a kind of revenue.”

    Despite its name, Unearned Revenue is a liability: the business has been paid for work it still owes. It becomes revenue only as the work is done. The US and international revenue standards call it a contract liability, which describes it better.

  • “Prepaid insurance is an expense, because the money has been paid.”

    Paying cash does not make something an expense; using up a resource does. On the day Lantern pays $1,800 it has twelve months of cover still to come, so it holds an asset. Only the expired part, $150 a month, becomes an expense.

  • “Accumulated depreciation is a liability, or a temporary account.”

    Accumulated depreciation is a contra-asset: it reduces Equipment on the asset side and is not owed to anyone. And it is permanent: it keeps growing from period to period until the asset is disposed of. Its companion, Depreciation Expense, is the temporary account that starts again at zero each period.

  • “A long-term loan is always a noncurrent liability.”

    Classification follows the payment dates, not the loan’s name or original length. The part of a loan due within twelve months of the balance sheet date, the current portion, is a current liability, and a two-year note becomes entirely current in its final year.

  • “Dividends are an expense, so they belong on the income statement.”

    Dividends are a distribution to owners, not a cost of earning revenue, and both frameworks exclude them from expenses. Like expenses, they reduce equity and are temporary accounts, but they are reported in the statement of changes in equity, not the income statement.

  • “Account numbers are set by the accounting standards.”

    Neither US GAAP nor IFRS prescribes account names or numbers; each business designs its own chart. The 100s-to-500s pattern in Lantern’s chart is a common convention, and many businesses use longer codes. What the standards govern is how items are classified and presented in the statements.

In practice

When Coastline Bank reviews Lantern’s figures at 31 March, the loan officer starts with classification, because it turns a list of balances into answers about risk. Lantern’s current assets total $30,700: cash of $26,650, accounts receivable of $1,800, supplies of $600 and prepaid insurance of $1,650. Its current liabilities total $11,810: $8,760 owed to suppliers, $1,400 of wages earned but unpaid, $50 of interest and $1,600 of unearned revenue. Current assets exceed current liabilities by $18,890, about 2.6 times over. In F03 you will meet this comparison as the current ratio; for now, notice that it rests entirely on classification.

Suppose a bookkeeper had put the $10,000 note among the current liabilities. Current liabilities would be $21,810, the surplus would shrink to $8,890 and the cover to about 1.4 times. Nothing about Lantern would have changed except one label, yet the bank would see a business with much less room to absorb a bad month. The opposite mistake is the more dangerous one: a company that shows next year’s loan instalment as noncurrent looks safer than it is. Loan agreements often set limits on exactly these figures, so lenders check how large items are classified, especially the current portion of debt, before they check the arithmetic.

Designing the chart of accounts is a professional decision too. The controller of a fictional clothing brand that sells through its own website, two shops and wholesale to department stores knows that a single Sales Revenue account would produce a correct income statement but would hide which channel makes money. So she opens three revenue accounts, one per channel, and gives each channel’s costs (shipping, shop rent, sales commissions) their own accounts or department codes. She leaves gaps in the numbering for a fourth channel the brand is considering, and she resists opening an account for every small cost, since each extra account has to be maintained and checked. The published statements will combine the three revenue accounts into one line. The chart serves internal decisions as well as external reporting, and the classification rules make sure that, however much detail is added, every account still rolls up into the right element and group.

Summary

  • An account records every increase and decrease in one item, together with its balance; a business keeps one for each item it needs to track.
  • The five elements are assets (resources the business controls), liabilities (present obligations), equity (the owners’ residual claim), revenue (from delivering goods and services) and expenses (resources used up). Dividends reduce equity but are not expenses.
  • Assets and liabilities are current if they will be turned into cash, used up or settled within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer; the rest are noncurrent. The part of a loan due within a year is current.
  • A contra account, such as accumulated depreciation, is paired with another account and reduces it; it is reported with its partner and shares its classification.
  • Permanent accounts (assets, liabilities, equity and contra-assets) carry their balances forward; temporary accounts (revenue, expenses and dividends) are closed to retained earnings at the end of each period.
  • A chart of accounts lists a business’s accounts with names and numbers; numbering conventions vary, and the same item can have different US and IFRS or UK names.
  • Borderline items are classified by asking what right or obligation exists now: prepaid insurance is an asset, unearned revenue a liability, and future interest not yet a liability.

Terms introduced

Account
A record of all the increases and decreases in one item, such as cash or accounts payable, together with its balance.
Asset
A resource the business controls as a result of past events and that is expected to bring it economic benefits. The FASB defines it as a present right of an entity to an economic benefit.
Liability
A present obligation of the business to transfer an economic resource to others, arising from past events.
Equity
The owners’ residual claim on a business. In the accounts it is assets minus liabilities (book value); in finance it is money invested in exchange for ownership, paid after lenders and other creditors, with no promised payments and no limit on the upside.
Revenue
Increases in assets, or decreases in liabilities, from delivering goods or services to customers in the business’s ordinary activities. Revenue increases equity.
Expense
Decreases in assets, or increases in liabilities, from using up resources in delivering goods or services. Expenses decrease equity; distributions to owners, such as dividends, are not expenses.
Current asset
Cash, or an asset expected to be turned into cash, sold or used up within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer, such as receivables, inventory and prepayments.
Noncurrent asset
Any asset that is not current, typically one the business expects to use for more than a year, such as equipment, vehicles, buildings and land.
Current liability
An obligation expected to be settled within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer, usually out of current assets, such as amounts owed to suppliers, wages, taxes and loan repayments due within the year.
Noncurrent liability
An obligation not due for settlement within one year or the operating cycle, such as the later instalments of a long-term loan.
Operating cycle
The average time between acquiring the materials or services a business works with and collecting cash from the customers it sells to.
Contra account
An account paired with another account whose balance it reduces, such as Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment, which reduces Equipment. The two are reported together.
Chart of accounts
The list of all the accounts a business uses, each with a name and usually a number, arranged by type.
Permanent account
An account whose balance carries forward from one period to the next: every asset, liability and equity account, including contra-asset accounts. Also called a real account.
Temporary account
An account that collects one period’s activity and is closed to retained earnings at the end of the period: revenue, expense and dividends accounts. Also called a nominal account.
Common stock
The equity account that records what shareholders have paid the corporation in exchange for its common shares (share capital under IFRS). Some companies split this amount between the shares’ par value and additional paid-in capital, a detail later courses cover.
