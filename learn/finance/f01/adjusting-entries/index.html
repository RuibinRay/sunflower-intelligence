Adjusting entries

Some changes arrive without a transaction: insurance expires, wages build up, equipment wears. Learn to find, calculate and record the five kinds of month-end adjustment, and to prepare the adjusted trial balance that the financial statements are built from.

About 95 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain why adjusting entries are needed at the end of each period and why they never involve Cash.
  • Record adjusting entries for prepaid expenses, unearned revenue, accrued revenue, accrued expenses and depreciation.
  • Calculate straight-line depreciation, accumulated depreciation and book value, including for part of a year.
  • Calculate accrued interest with I = P × r × t.
  • Prepare an adjusted trial balance from an unadjusted trial balance and the adjusting entries.
  • Analyse the effect of omitting an adjustment on net income, assets, liabilities and equity.

Review first

  • Accrual versus cash accounting — Adjustments put the revenue and expense recognition principles into practice, using the deferrals and accruals you met there.
  • Journal entries — Every adjustment is recorded as a journal entry with the debit and credit rules.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

At the close of business on 31 March, Lantern’s trial balance balances at $46,860. Every transaction of the month has been journalised and posted, yet several balances are out of date. Prepaid Insurance still shows the full $1,800 although a month of cover has gone. Supplies shows $1,500, but a count finds $600 on the shelves. Unearned Revenue still holds all of Harbor Tech’s $2,400 although the first event was catered on 26 March. And nothing records the wages the baristas earned in the second half of March, the interest building up on the bank loan, a month’s wear on the espresso machine, or the breakfast Lantern catered for Pier Nine Studio that morning.

None of these changes came with a transaction. Ledger accounts change only when an entry is posted, and entries are prompted by source documents: invoices, receipts, bank records. Time passing and assets being used up produce no document. An adjusting entry is a journal entry made at the end of a period to bring account balances up to date, so that revenue and expenses fall in the period to which they belong (the accrual basis of lesson 8) and the balance sheet shows the assets and liabilities that actually exist at the period end. Because Lantern reports monthly for this course, it adjusts every month; a business that reports yearly adjusts at least once a year.

Five kinds of adjustment

Every adjustment falls into one of the four cells of the timing map from lesson 8, or spreads the cost of a long-lived asset as depreciation. Many textbooks, OpenStax among them, count four kinds and treat depreciation as a kind of prepaid expense, because the cost was paid or owed in advance; this course lists it separately because its entry credits a contra account. The table shows each kind, what is wrong before the adjustment, the shape of the entry and Lantern’s examples, numbered a1 to a7.

Two features are common to every row. Each adjustment changes at least one income statement account (a revenue or an expense) and at least one balance sheet account (an asset, a contra-asset or a liability). And none of them touches Cash.

Key idea: Why Cash is never in an adjusting entry

Cash is kept up to date by transactions: every receipt and payment is recorded when it happens, and the bank statement confirms the balance. An adjustment exists because cash and recognition fall in different periods. For a deferral the cash side was recorded earlier, when Lantern paid for the insurance or received the deposit; for an accrual it will be recorded later, when the wages are paid or the customer pays. The adjustment records only the other half, the recognition. If you find yourself writing Cash into an adjusting entry, you are recording a transaction, not an adjustment.

To find the adjustments, accountants walk through the trial balance asking two questions of each account. Has part of this balance been used up or earned since it was recorded? That question finds the deferrals: Prepaid Insurance, Supplies, Unearned Revenue, Equipment. Has anything been earned or incurred that isn’t recorded at all? That question finds the accruals, which have no balance to prompt you, so they come from checklists, contracts, timesheets and loan agreements: the wages owed, the interest building up, the breakfast not yet invoiced.

In depth

Prepaid expenses: insurance and supplies

A prepaid expense is a cost paid in advance for benefits the business will receive in later periods; until then it is an asset. Lantern’s policy cost $1,800 for 12 months from 1 March. By 31 March one month has expired, so 1,800÷12=1501{,}800 \div 12 = 1501,800÷12=150 becomes insurance expense (a1). The other $1,650 is still an asset: cover Lantern will receive from April 2026 to February 2027.

Supplies work the same way but are measured by counting. Lantern doesn’t record each cup and bag of beans as it is used; that would cost more than the information is worth. Instead it counts what is left at the end of the month and works out what must have been used: supplies used = supplies at the start + purchases − supplies on hand, here 0+1,500−600=9000 + 1{,}500 - 600 = 9000+1,500−600=900 (a2). After the adjustment the Supplies account shows $600, the amount actually on the shelves.

Depreciation

Depreciation is the allocation of the cost of a long-lived asset, less its residual value, over its useful life. The residual value is what the business expects to get for the asset at the end of the time it plans to use it; the useful life is how long it expects to use it. Depreciation applies the expense recognition principle: the machine helps earn revenue for years, so each period bears part of its cost. It is not an attempt to measure what the asset is worth today.

The straight-line method charges the same amount every period: cost minus residual value, divided by useful life. Lantern’s $12,000 of equipment is expected to last five years with no residual value, so depreciation is (12,000−0)÷5=2,400(12{,}000 - 0) \div 5 = 2{,}400(12,000−0)÷5=2,400 a year, or 2,400÷12=2002{,}400 \div 12 = 2002,400÷12=200 a month (a3). Lantern’s policy takes a full month in the month of purchase, so March bears the whole $200 although the machine arrived on 3 March.

The credit doesn’t go to Equipment. It goes to Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment, a contra account with a credit balance that collects all the depreciation recorded so far: its total is the asset’s accumulated depreciation. Equipment keeps showing the original cost of $12,000, and the two together give the book value (called the carrying amount under IFRS): 12,000−200=11,80012{,}000 - 200 = 11{,}80012,000−200=11,800 at 31 March. After 12 months accumulated depreciation will be $2,400 and book value $9,600. After 60 months, at the end of February 2031, the machine will be fully depreciated, with a book value of zero, its expected residual value. Keeping cost and accumulated depreciation apart shows a reader both how much was invested and roughly how much of the asset’s life has been used.

Note: Part-period conventions

Lantern’s policy of a full month in the month of purchase is one convention among several. Others charge a full month only for assets bought in the first half of a month, count the exact number of days, or charge half a year in the year of purchase whatever the date. Counting days, Lantern’s machine, bought on 3 March, was in service for 29 of March’s 31 days, giving 200×29÷31≈187.10200 \times 29 \div 31 \approx 187.10200×29÷31≈187.10. No convention is more correct than another: a business chooses one, applies it consistently, and the total depreciation over the asset’s life is the same.

Unearned revenue

Harbor Tech paid $2,400 on 8 March for three events, and Lantern recorded the deposit as Unearned Revenue, a liability. In lesson 8 the five-step model showed that $800 was earned on 26 March, when the first event was catered. The adjustment (a4) moves that $800 from the liability to Catering Revenue; the remaining $1,600 stays as a liability until the April events.

Note: Another way to record advance payments

Some businesses record a customer’s advance payment straight into a revenue account, or a prepaid cost straight into an expense account, and adjust at the period end for the part not yet earned or used. The adjusting entry then runs the other way (for example, debit the revenue and credit Unearned Revenue for the part not yet earned), but the adjusted balances end up the same. This course records advances in the balance sheet first, as Lantern does.

Accrued revenue

Accrued revenue is revenue earned in the period but not yet recorded, because no invoice has been sent or no payment is due yet. Lantern catered Pier Nine Studio’s breakfast on 31 March and will send the $600 invoice on 2 April. The performance obligation has been satisfied, so March has earned the revenue, and Lantern has a right to be paid: an account receivable (a7). Sending the invoice in April records nothing new, because the receivable already exists; when Pier Nine pays, Lantern debits Cash and credits Accounts Receivable. Interest earned on money Lantern had lent would be accrued the same way, through Interest Receivable and Interest Revenue.

Accrued expenses: wages and interest

An accrued expense is an expense incurred in the period but not yet paid or recorded. The baristas were paid on 20 March for 1–15 March. By 31 March they have earned another $1,400 for 16–31 March, to be paid on 5 April. March used their work, so March bears the expense; Lantern owes the money, so a liability, Wages Payable, appears (a5). On 5 April Lantern will debit Wages Payable and credit Cash for $1,400: the payment settles the liability and is not a second expense.

Interest builds up day by day on a loan. Simple interest for part of a year is I=P×r×tI = P \times r \times tI=P×r×t: principal times the annual rate times the time in years. Lantern’s $10,000 note at 6% a year accrues 10,000×0.06×112=5010{,}000 \times 0.06 \times \tfrac{1}{12} = 5010,000×0.06×121​=50 in March (a6). The first payment, due on 1 June, will cover three months: 10,000×0.06×312=15010{,}000 \times 0.06 \times \tfrac{3}{12} = 15010,000×0.06×123​=150. If Lantern accrues $50 at the end of March, April and May, Interest Payable will stand at $150 on 31 May, and the payment on 1 June (debit Interest Payable, credit Cash) clears it without any new expense. Loan agreements may also measure time in days over a 360-day or 365-day year; the contract says which.

The total rose from $46,860 to $49,110, by $2,250, although the adjustments add up to $4,100. Three of them only move an amount between two accounts on the same side: a1 and a2 move debit balance from an asset to an expense, and a4 moves credit balance from a liability to a revenue, so they leave the totals unchanged. The other four add a new amount to both sides: 200+1,400+50+600=2,250200 + 1{,}400 + 50 + 600 = 2{,}250200+1,400+50+600=2,250. A balanced adjusted trial balance shows only that each adjustment was recorded with equal debits and credits; it can’t show that an adjustment was forgotten.

If an adjustment is missed

An omitted adjustment leaves out both its debit and its credit, so the trial balance still balances and the error has to be found by thinking, not by adding up. Each omission misstates an income statement total and a balance sheet total by the same amount, and until the books are closed, equity is misstated by the same amount as net income.

Two patterns make the table easy to rebuild. Leaving out an expense overstates net income and equity; leaving out revenue understates them. Omitted deferral adjustments leave a balance sheet figure too high (an asset not yet used up, a liability not yet earned off), while omitted accruals leave one too low (a receivable or payable that is missing). With all seven left out, Lantern would report net income of $2,290 instead of $990, total assets of $43,150 instead of $42,500, liabilities of $21,160 instead of $21,810 and equity of $21,990 instead of $20,690.

Estimates and judgement

Several adjustments rest on estimates: the machine’s five-year life and nil residual value, the supplies count, the date on which an event counts as delivered. If Ana later expects the machine to last only four years, the depreciation charge changes from then on; later courses show how. Estimates should be reasonable, supported by evidence and applied consistently. They are also where auditors look hardest, because a small change in a useful life or an accrual can move profit without any cash changing hands.

Common mistakes

  • “Accrued wages: debit Wages Expense, credit Cash.”

    Nothing has been paid on 31 March, so Cash can’t change. The credit goes to a liability, Wages Payable. Cash appears on 5 April, when the payment settles that liability. If an entry contains Cash, it is a transaction, not an adjustment.

  • “Supplies expense is what the count shows.”

    The count is what is left: it becomes the new balance of the Supplies asset. The expense is what was used: start + purchases − count. Lantern counted $600, so it used $1,500 − $600 = $900, not $600.

  • “Depreciation: credit Equipment.”

    Equipment stays at its cost; depreciation is credited to the contra account Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment, so a reader can see both the original cost and how much has been allocated. And depreciation is not a fall in market value: it spreads cost over the years of use.

  • “A 6% loan of $10,000 costs $600 of interest this month.”

    Interest rates are quoted per year. One month is 1/12 of a year: 10,000×0.06×112=5010{,}000 \times 0.06 \times \tfrac{1}{12} = 5010,000×0.06×121​=50. Always convert the time into years before multiplying.

  • “When the accrued wages are paid, record Wages Expense again.”

    The expense was recognised on 31 March by the adjustment. On 5 April the payment only settles the liability: debit Wages Payable, credit Cash. Debiting Wages Expense again would count March’s wages twice, once in March and once in April.

  • “The adjusted trial balance balances, so no adjustment is missing.”

    An omitted adjustment leaves out both its debit and its credit, so the totals still agree. Only a systematic review (each balance questioned, a checklist of accruals) finds what is missing.

In practice

In a business of any size, adjustments are part of a routine called the month-end close. The accountant works from a checklist of recurring items: prepayments are expensed from a schedule that lists each policy and contract, depreciation comes from a fixed-asset register that records each asset’s cost, life and convention, accrued payroll comes from timesheets, interest from the loan agreements, and unbilled revenue from records of work completed but not invoiced. Many of these entries are prepared in the same way every month, which is why Lantern’s a1, a3 and a6 will recur in April with the same amounts.

The items that are not routine call for judgement, and that is where the risk lies. Whether a job was finished on 31 March or 1 April, whether a deposit has been earned, and what useful life to give a new van all move profit between periods without any cash changing hands. Auditors therefore test adjustments with evidence: they look at delivery records near the period end to check that revenue is in the right period, compare accrued wages with the next payroll, and ask whether useful lives match how long similar assets have actually lasted.

Decisions depend on these numbers. The hotel’s bonus in the applied example would have been overpaid by $4,560 on unadjusted figures. Loan covenants work the same way: Coastline Bank tests Lantern’s liabilities against its equity, and on unadjusted figures Lantern would appear to owe $650 less and own $1,300 more equity than it really does. When Ana prices her April events or decides whether to hire, she should use March’s adjusted net income of $990, not the $2,290 her unadjusted trial balance shows.

Summary

  • Adjusting entries bring accounts up to date at the end of a period for changes that come with no transaction: time passing, assets used, revenue earned and costs incurred.
  • There are five kinds: prepaid expenses and unearned revenue (deferrals), accrued revenue and accrued expenses (accruals), and depreciation.
  • Every adjustment changes at least one income statement account and one balance sheet account, and never Cash.
  • Straight-line depreciation = (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life, credited to accumulated depreciation, a contra-asset; book value = cost − accumulated depreciation.
  • Interest accrues as I = P × r × t, with r per year and t in years. Supplies used = start + purchases − count.
  • Lantern’s seven adjustments reduce net income from $2,290 to $990 and raise the trial balance total from $46,860 to $49,110.
  • A missed adjustment doesn’t unbalance the trial balance; it misstates net income and equity, and one of assets or liabilities, by the same amount.

Terms introduced

Adjusting entry
A journal entry made at the end of a period to bring an account up to date, so that revenue and expenses are recognised in the right period. It involves at least one income statement account and one balance sheet account, and never Cash.
Prepaid expense
A cost paid in advance for benefits to be received in later periods, such as insurance or rent. It is an asset until it is used up.
Accrued revenue
Revenue earned in the period but not yet received or recorded, for example work done but not yet invoiced. It is recorded with a receivable.
Accrued expense
An expense incurred in the period but not yet paid or recorded, such as wages or interest owed. It is recorded with a payable.
Depreciation
The systematic allocation of the cost of a long-lived asset, less its residual value, over its useful life. It is an expense in the accounts but uses no cash in the period it is recorded, and it is not a measure of market value.
Accumulated depreciation
All the depreciation recorded on an asset since it was acquired, held in a contra-asset account with a credit balance.
Book value
An asset’s cost minus its accumulated depreciation; called the carrying amount under IFRS.
Straight-line method
A depreciation method that charges the same amount in every full period: (cost − residual value) ÷ useful life.
Residual value
The amount a business expects to receive for an asset at the end of its useful life. Also called salvage value.
Useful life
The period over which a business expects to use an asset, in years or units of output.
Adjusted trial balance
A list of every account and its balance after the adjusting entries have been posted, used to prepare the financial statements.
