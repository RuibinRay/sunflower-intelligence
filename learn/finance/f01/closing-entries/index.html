Closing entries

At the end of a period, the revenue, expense and dividend accounts are emptied into Retained Earnings so that the next period starts from zero. Learn the four closing entries, record Lantern’s for March, and prove the result with a post-closing trial balance.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Distinguish temporary accounts, which are closed at the end of each period, from permanent accounts, which carry their balances forward.
  • Explain the two jobs closing entries do: resetting the temporary accounts and bringing Retained Earnings up to date.
  • Record the four closing entries through Income Summary, for a net income and for a net loss.
  • Prepare a post-closing trial balance and use it to confirm that only permanent accounts remain open.
  • Calculate retained earnings after closing and reconcile it with the statement of changes in equity and the balance sheet.
  • Identify common closing errors and correct them.

Review first

  • Financial statements from the adjusted trial balance — Closing records in the ledger the net income and dividends you reported on the income statement and the statement of changes in equity.
  • Accounts, terminology and classifications — You need the difference between temporary and permanent accounts.
  • Adjusting entries — Closing starts from the adjusted balances.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

It is 31 March 2026 and Lantern’s first month is complete on paper. The adjusting entries are posted and the statements are done: net income of $990, a $300 dividend to Ana, and retained earnings of $690 on the balance sheet. Yet the Retained Earnings account in the general ledger still shows a zero balance, because no entry has ever touched it. Meanwhile Catering Revenue holds $5,600, Wages Expense $3,000 and Dividends $300. If April’s transactions were simply posted on top, April’s income statement would report two months of revenue and wages as though they were one.

Two things are out of step. The ledger disagrees with the balance sheet it was used to prepare, and the accounts that measure March’s performance are still full of March. The last two steps of the accounting cycle put both right: the closing entries, and a final check called the post-closing trial balance.

Accounts that measure a period, and accounts that carry forward

Recall the split from lesson 2. Temporary accounts, also called nominal accounts, measure what happened during a period: revenue, expenses and dividends. Permanent accounts, also called real accounts, measure what the business has and owes at a moment: assets, contra-assets such as accumulated depreciation, liabilities, common stock and retained earnings. Lantern’s cash at the end of 31 March is its cash at the start of 1 April, so permanent balances carry forward. March’s catering revenue is not April’s, so temporary balances must start again from zero.

A car’s dashboard makes the difference concrete. The odometer shows the total distance the car has ever travelled and is never reset. The trip meter shows the distance of the current journey and is set back to zero before the next one. Permanent accounts are odometers; temporary accounts are trip meters, and closing is the moment you press reset, after writing down how far the trip went.

Where does that figure go? Into Retained Earnings. Revenue, expenses and dividends are all changes in equity that the business tracks in separate accounts so that it can report them. You met the link in the expanded accounting equation: ending retained earnings equal beginning retained earnings plus revenue, minus expenses, minus dividends. During the period those pieces sit in their own accounts. At the end, their net effect belongs in Retained Earnings.

What closing entries do

Closing entries are journal entries made at the end of an accounting period that transfer the balances of the temporary accounts into Retained Earnings, leaving every temporary account with a zero balance. They do two jobs at once:

  • Reset. Every revenue, expense and dividend account starts the next period at zero, so the next income statement measures that period alone.
  • Update. Retained Earnings absorbs the period’s net income and dividends, so its balance in the ledger equals the amount on the balance sheet.

Key idea: Closing moves amounts inside equity

No asset or liability changes when the books are closed, and neither does total equity. Closing moves amounts from one part of equity to another, the “equity up, equity down” pattern from lesson 3. No cash moves, nothing is earned and nothing is spent. Net income was measured by the income statement; closing records it where it belongs.

Income Summary: a checkpoint on the way

The traditional method routes revenue and expenses through a temporary account called Income Summary. It exists only for closing: its balance is zero before closing starts and zero when it ends, and it never appears on a financial statement. Revenue balances are moved into it on the credit side and expense balances on the debit side. Once both are in, its balance is the period’s net income (a credit) or net loss (a debit). That figure must agree with the income statement, which makes Income Summary a built-in check.

The method has four entries, made in this order:

  1. Close the revenue accounts. Debit each revenue account for its balance; credit Income Summary for total revenue.
  2. Close the expense accounts. Debit Income Summary for total expenses; credit each expense account for its balance.
  3. Close Income Summary. For a net income, debit Income Summary and credit Retained Earnings for the amount. For a net loss, do the opposite.
  4. Close Dividends. Debit Retained Earnings and credit Dividends for the period’s dividends.

One rule produces every line: to bring an account to zero, record its balance on the opposite side. Revenue accounts have credit balances, so they are debited. Expense accounts and Dividends have debit balances, so they are credited. Dividends go straight to Retained Earnings, not through Income Summary, because they are not an expense and are not part of net income. Passing them through Income Summary would make its balance differ from the net income on the income statement and spoil the check.

Retained Earnings now shows a credit balance of $690, the same figure as the ending balance on the statement of changes in equity: 0+990−300=6900 + 990 - 300 = 6900+990−300=690. Closing directly to Retained Earnings

Income Summary is a convenience, not a requirement. Entries 1 to 3 can be combined into one compound entry that debits each revenue account, credits each expense account and credits Retained Earnings for the difference, which is net income. Dividends are then closed exactly as before. The final balances are identical. What you lose is the separate checkpoint, although you can still compare the Retained Earnings line of the entry with net income.

Which method a business uses is a bookkeeping choice: the financial statements look the same either way. Manual systems and textbooks often keep Income Summary because its checkpoint catches mistakes. Accounting software usually shows no closing entries at all, as you will see under In practice.

When the period ends in a loss

If expenses exceed revenue, entries 1 and 2 leave Income Summary with a debit balance equal to the net loss. To bring it to zero, entry 3 credits Income Summary and debits Retained Earnings. A net loss therefore reduces retained earnings, just as a net income increases them.

Take a fictional ski school in July, when its only revenue comes from lessons on an artificial slope. It earns training revenue of $4,000 and pays wages of $2,800, rent of $1,200 and advertising of $600, so its expenses total $4,600 and it makes a net loss of $600.

Check Income Summary before writing entry 3: credits of 4,000 and debits of 4,600 leave a debit balance of 600, which entry 3 clears with a credit of 600. The common mistake is to copy the profitable pattern (debit Income Summary, credit Retained Earnings) without looking at the balance. That would leave Income Summary with a debit of $1,200 instead of zero, and it would increase retained earnings by $600 in a month when the business lost $600. Always close Income Summary on the side opposite its balance.

The post-closing trial balance

A post-closing trial balance lists the ledger balances after the closing entries have been posted. It is the last step of the accounting cycle and checks two things: that debits still equal credits, and that only permanent accounts still have balances. A revenue, expense, Dividends or Income Summary line on it means closing is incomplete.

Thirteen accounts, all permanent. Retained Earnings shows a balance for the first time.

Compare the totals of Lantern’s three trial balances: $46,860 before adjustment, $49,110 after it and $42,700 after closing. Closing removed $6,410 from each side. On the debit side, expenses of $6,110 and dividends of $300 disappeared: 6,110+300=6,4106{,}110 + 300 = 6{,}4106,110+300=6,410. On the credit side, revenue of $7,100 disappeared and a Retained Earnings balance of $690 appeared: 7,100−690=6,4107{,}100 - 690 = 6{,}4107,100−690=6,410.

Nor is $42,700 the total of the balance sheet, which shows total assets of $42,500. The difference is accumulated depreciation. On a trial balance it is a credit balance like any other, so it adds $200 to the credit column; on the balance sheet it is subtracted from equipment. Debits of 42,700 less the 200 of accumulated depreciation give total assets of 42,500. Credits of 42,700 are liabilities of 21,810 plus equity of 20,690 plus that same 200.

Caution: Balanced does not mean right

Like every trial balance, the post-closing trial balance proves only that the ledger is arithmetically in balance. If Lantern had closed Supplies along with the expenses by mistake, Supplies would vanish from the list, Retained Earnings would be $600 too low, and the two columns would still agree. Compare each line with the balance sheet you have just prepared: they should match account for account.

Note: Sole proprietors and partnerships

A business without shares closes in the same way. Income Summary is closed to the owner’s capital account instead of Retained Earnings, and the withdrawals account (also called drawings) is closed to capital in place of Dividends. In a partnership, net income is shared among the partners’ capital accounts according to their agreement.

Common mistakes

  • “Dividends are closed to Income Summary, like expenses.”

    Dividends are a distribution of profit, not an expense, so they bypass Income Summary. If Lantern’s $300 were closed to Income Summary, its balance would be $690 instead of the $990 of net income on the income statement, and the check Income Summary provides would be lost. Retained Earnings would happen to end at the right figure, but entry 3 would misstate what the period earned. Close Dividends straight to Retained Earnings.

  • “Anything with ‘revenue’ or ‘depreciation’ in its name gets closed.”

    Unearned Revenue is a liability, Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment is a contra-asset, and Supplies and Prepaid Insurance are assets. None of them is closed. Test each account by where it is reported: if it appears on the balance sheet, it is permanent and its balance carries forward.

  • “Entry 3 always debits Income Summary.”

    Only when there is a net income. Look at Income Summary’s balance after entries 1 and 2 before writing entry 3. A debit balance is a net loss, closed by crediting Income Summary and debiting Retained Earnings. Debiting Income Summary again would double its debit and add the loss to retained earnings instead of subtracting it.

  • “Closing changes profit, or moves cash.”

    Closing entries move balances within equity after net income has been measured. They change neither net income nor any asset or liability. Lantern’s cash is $26,650 before and after closing, and its retained earnings of $690 are a claim on all its assets, not money set aside in a drawer.

  • “You can close first and adjust afterwards.”

    Closing uses the adjusted balances, so it comes after the adjusting entries and the statements. Had Lantern closed its unadjusted balances, Retained Earnings would have received the unadjusted profit of $2,290 instead of $990, and the adjustments posted afterwards would have landed in accounts that are supposed to measure April.

  • “If the post-closing trial balance balances, every account was closed.”

    Suppose Interest Expense ($50) were left out of entry 2. Income Summary would show $1,040, Retained Earnings would end at $740 instead of $690, and the post-closing trial balance would still balance, at $42,750 on each side, because the $50 sits on both sides. Only reading the list of accounts reveals the error: Interest Expense should not be there.

In practice

How often businesses close

Lantern closes every month so that you can watch the whole cycle in a single month. Most businesses close their books once a year, at the end of their fiscal year. They still prepare monthly or quarterly statements for managers, lenders and investors, but they do so without formal closing entries. The income statement for a month simply reports the revenue and expenses recorded between two dates, and the balance sheet adds the profit earned so far this year to retained earnings.

Accounting software takes care of the mechanics. Nobody resets the revenue and expense accounts by hand; reports are calculated for whatever dates are chosen, and at the turn of the year the package rolls the old year’s profit into retained earnings itself. QuickBooks Online, for example, moves last year’s net income into its retained earnings account when a new fiscal year begins, and lets the business set a closing date that protects earlier transactions from being changed by accident.

“The close” in a finance team

In practice, then, “closing the books” means more than four journal entries. Finance teams speak of the month-end or year-end close: the routine of recording accruals and other adjustments, reconciling accounts with bank and supplier statements, reviewing the results, and then locking the period so that nobody can post into it by mistake. Locking matters because the statements have already gone to people who rely on them. A change after that point should be a deliberate, documented correction, not a quiet edit.

The post-closing trial balance has a practical afterlife too. Its balances are the opening balances of the next period. When a business moves to a new accounting system, these are the figures it enters as the starting point. And auditors check that a year’s opening balances agree with the closing balances of the year before, because an error carried across the year end would distort both years.

Ana signs off March

On 2 April Ana reviews the close with her accountant. Three questions take five minutes. Does Income Summary’s $990 agree with the income statement? Does the post-closing trial balance contain only permanent accounts? Does each balance on it match the balance sheet, with Retained Earnings at $690? With three yeses, March is locked, and April begins with every revenue, expense and dividend account at zero. When the board later considers a dividend, it will look at retained earnings, but it will look at cash as well, because $690 of retained earnings and $26,650 of cash are answers to different questions.

Summary

  • Temporary accounts (revenue, expenses and dividends) measure one period and are closed; permanent accounts (assets, contra-assets, liabilities and equity) carry their balances forward.
  • Closing entries reset the temporary accounts to zero and move the period’s net income and dividends into Retained Earnings, so the ledger agrees with the balance sheet. They change no asset, liability or total equity.
  • The four entries: close revenue to Income Summary; close expenses to Income Summary; close Income Summary to Retained Earnings; close Dividends to Retained Earnings.
  • After the first two entries, Income Summary equals net income (a credit) or net loss (a debit). Close it on the opposite side. Closing directly to Retained Earnings gives the same result without the checkpoint.
  • Lantern’s March close moves $990 of net income and $300 of dividends into Retained Earnings, leaving $690; its post-closing trial balance totals $42,700.
  • A post-closing trial balance lists only permanent accounts. Balancing proves the arithmetic, not the closing: compare every line with the balance sheet.
  • Most businesses close once a year and prepare monthly statements without closing; software rolls profit into retained earnings and locks closed periods.

Terms introduced

Closing entries
Journal entries made at the end of an accounting period that transfer the balances of the revenue, expense and dividend accounts to Retained Earnings, leaving those accounts at zero.
Income Summary
A temporary account used only during closing. Revenue and expenses are closed into it, so its balance equals net income or net loss; it is then closed to Retained Earnings. It never appears on a financial statement.
Post-closing trial balance
A trial balance prepared after the closing entries are posted. It contains only permanent accounts and confirms that debits equal credits as the next period begins.
