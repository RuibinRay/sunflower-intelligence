The accounting equation

Every business’s resources come from creditors or owners, so assets always equal liabilities plus equity. Learn to trace any transaction through that equation and to see how revenue, expenses and dividends change what the owners own.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • State the accounting equation and explain why it must balance after every transaction.
  • Analyse a business transaction into its effects on assets, liabilities and equity.
  • Use the expanded accounting equation to show how revenue, expenses and dividends change equity.
  • Distinguish contributed capital from retained earnings.
  • Calculate a missing element of the equation, including net income, from beginning and ending balances.

Review first

  • Accounts, terminology and classifications — You need the five kinds of account (assets, liabilities, equity, revenue and expenses) and what belongs in each.
  • Adding and subtracting positive and negative numbers.

The idea

Fictional case

On 1 March 2026 Ana Lim opens a bank account for her new company, Lantern Coffee Co., and pays in $20,000 of her own savings. In exchange the company issues her shares of common stock. The same day the company borrows $10,000 from Coastline Bank. Lantern now holds $30,000 of cash. Ask where that cash came from and there are only two answers: $10,000 came from a lender, who expects to be repaid, and $20,000 came from the owner, who expects nothing back except a share of whatever the business becomes.

That is the whole idea of this lesson. A business has resources, and every one of those resources was supplied by someone with a claim on it. Accounting records both sides of that fact: what the business has and who has a claim on it.

Three words, one equation

The resources are assets: things the business controls that are expected to bring it economic benefits, such as cash, equipment, supplies and amounts customers owe it. The claims come in two kinds. Liabilities are what the business owes to outsiders: a bank loan, a supplier’s unpaid bill, a deposit for a service not yet delivered. Equity is the owners’ claim, and it is a residual claim: the owners are entitled to whatever is left of the assets after every liability has been settled.

Because the claims account for every dollar of resources, the two must be equal. This is the accounting equation:

Assets=Liabilities+Equity\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}Assets=Liabilities+Equity

It holds for a coffee cart, a hospital and a multinational, and it holds at every moment, not just at the end of the year. It is not a rule that accountants enforce by adjusting numbers; it is true by construction, because equity is defined as what is left after liabilities. Rearranged, the equation says exactly that:

Equity=Assets−Liabilities\text{Equity} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities}Equity=Assets−Liabilities

For this reason equity is often called net assets. After its first day, Lantern has assets of $30,000, liabilities of $10,000 and equity of $20,000, and 30,000=10,000+20,00030{,}000 = 10{,}000 + 20{,}00030,000=10,000+20,000.

Every transaction has two sides

A transaction is an exchange or event that changes the business’s resources or the claims on them. Because the equation must stay true, no transaction can change just one number. If assets go up, something else must also change: another asset goes down, a liability goes up, or equity goes up. This is the dual aspect of every transaction, and it is why the system you will learn in the next module is called double-entry bookkeeping.

Look at Lantern’s first week, transaction by transaction. For each one, ask two questions: which items changed, and in which direction?

Three things in that table are worth slowing down for. Second, paying for insurance in advance (4 March) changes no totals at all: one asset, cash, turns into another, the right to a year of insurance cover. Third, the deposit from Harbor Tech (8 March) brings in cash but is not income yet. Until Lantern caters the events, it owes Harbor Tech either the service or the money back, so the deposit is a liability called unearned revenue.

Key idea: The test for any transaction

Name what changed, say whether each item went up or down, and confirm that the change in assets equals the change in liabilities plus the change in equity. If it doesn’t, you have missed an effect or put one on the wrong side.

In depth

What the definitions say

The equation rests on precise definitions, set by the bodies that write accounting standards. In the United States the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) defines an asset as “a present right of an entity to an economic benefit”, a liability as a present obligation to transfer an economic benefit, and equity as the residual interest in the assets after deducting liabilities (Concepts Statement No. 8, Chapter 4, issued December 2021). The International Accounting Standards Board (IASB), whose IFRS Accounting Standards are required in more than 140 jurisdictions, words it differently but builds the same structure: an asset is a present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events, a liability is a present obligation to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events, and equity is the residual interest (Conceptual Framework, March 2018).

Standards: US GAAP and IFRS

The two frameworks agree on the equation and on equity being a residual. They differ in wording and in some recognition rules elsewhere in the standards, which later courses cover. You will also meet different names for the same things: “stockholders’ equity” or “shareholders’ equity” in the United States, “share capital” for common stock and “statement of financial position” for the balance sheet under IFRS.

Two consequences of these definitions matter from the start. An item belongs on the asset side only if the business controls it and expects a benefit from it, so a loyal customer base or a talented barista, however valuable, is not recorded as an asset: the business neither owns nor controls them in the accounting sense. And a liability needs a present obligation, so a plan to buy a van next month is not a liability; nor is a signed order for one, until the van is delivered.

The expanded accounting equation

Equity changes for four reasons, and separating them tells a much richer story than one equity total. Owners can put money in, and they receive stock for it: that is contributed capital, recorded in Common Stock. The business can earn revenue by delivering goods or services, which increases equity. It incurs expenses in doing so, which decrease equity. And it can pay some of its profits back to the owners as dividends, which also decrease equity. Profit kept in the business accumulates in retained earnings. Putting the pieces into the equation gives the expanded accounting equation:

Assets=Liabilities+Common Stock+Retained Earningsbeginning+Revenue−Expenses−Dividends\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Common Stock} + \text{Retained Earnings}_{\text{beginning}} + \text{Revenue} - \text{Expenses} - \text{Dividends}Assets=Liabilities+Common Stock+Retained Earningsbeginning​+Revenue−Expenses−Dividends

Revenue minus expenses is net income for the period (a net loss if expenses are larger). At the end of the period, net income minus dividends is added to retained earnings, so the expanded equation collapses back into the basic one. Common stock is contributed capital; retained earnings are earned capital.

The nine patterns

Since a change in assets must equal the change in liabilities plus the change in equity, a transaction that touches two items can only follow one of nine patterns. Recognising the pattern is the fastest way to check your analysis. Some transactions combine several patterns, as Lantern’s equipment purchase did.

When does something enter the equation?

Not every business event is a transaction. On 20 March a hotel signs a contract with Lantern to cater its June conference for $5,000, payable after the event. Nothing has changed hands: Lantern hasn’t delivered any coffee and the hotel hasn’t paid anything. Each side has only promised to perform later, so Lantern records nothing yet. A contract like this, where neither party has performed, is called an executory contract, and it stays out of the equation until one side acts. If the hotel paid a deposit, Lantern would record cash and unearned revenue; when Lantern caters the conference, it records revenue.

The same test explains other non-events. Hiring a barista who starts next week creates no asset or liability today: wages become an expense, and a liability if unpaid, as the barista works. Placing an order for supplies that haven’t arrived and haven’t been paid for is a promise, not a transaction. What enters the equation is a change in resources or in obligations that already exist, measured reliably in money. This is why some important facts about a business, such as a large contract just signed or a key employee leaving, appear in the notes to the financial statements or not at all, rather than in the equation.

Caution: Measured in money, at a point in time

The equation records only what can be measured in a currency (the monetary unit assumption) and only for this business, separate from its owners’ personal affairs (the economic entity assumption). Ana’s personal car is not Lantern’s asset unless she transfers it to the company, and her personal mortgage is not Lantern’s liability.

What a balanced equation does and doesn’t prove

A balanced equation proves that every recorded transaction affected both sides consistently. It does not prove the records are right. If Lantern recorded its espresso machine as supplies, or forgot a transaction entirely, or recorded a $1,500 purchase as $150 on both sides, the equation would still balance. You will meet the same limitation again when you prepare a trial balance in lesson 7.

Nor does equity tell you what the business is worth. The amounts are mostly historical costs, what Lantern paid for things, and some valuable resources are not recorded at all. A buyer might pay far more, or far less, than Lantern’s $21,990 of equity (before the month-end adjustments). Equity is the owners’ accounting claim, not a market price and not a pile of cash: Lantern’s equity is spread across cash, equipment, supplies and what customers owe.

Note: Sole proprietors and partnerships

Lantern is a corporation, so owners’ investments are recorded as Common Stock and payments to them as Dividends. In a business owned by one person without incorporating, the same ideas use an owner’s capital account and withdrawals (also called drawings). The equation is identical.

Common mistakes

  • “Revenue means cash came in.”

    Revenue is earned when the business delivers the service, whether or not it has been paid. Lantern’s $4,200 conference invoice is revenue on 12 March although no cash arrived until later; Harbor Tech’s $2,400 deposit is cash but not revenue, because the events haven’t happened. Ask “has the business done what it was paid for?”, not “has money arrived?”.

  • “Dividends are an expense.”

    Dividends reduce equity, but they are a distribution of profit to owners, not a cost of earning revenue. They are left out of net income and subtracted from retained earnings afterwards. Treating them as an expense would make Lantern’s March profit look $300 smaller than it was.

  • “Paying a bill is an expense.”

    When Lantern pays the $1,000 it owes for supplies, assets and liabilities both fall and equity doesn’t move. The cost was recorded when the supplies were bought (as an asset) and becomes an expense when they are used. Paying the bill only settles the debt.

  • “Equity is the owner’s money in the bank.”

    Equity is a claim on all the assets, not a particular asset. Lantern’s $21,990 of equity at 31 March, before adjustments, is spread across cash, equipment, insurance cover, supplies and receivables. Nor is it a market value: someone buying Lantern would pay what they think its future is worth.

  • “If the equation balances, the records are correct.”

    The equation catches one-sided mistakes only. Recording equipment as supplies, omitting a transaction, or entering the same wrong amount on both sides all leave it balanced. Balancing is necessary, not sufficient.

In practice

Lenders read the equation first. When Ana asks Coastline Bank for more credit, the loan officer looks at how much of Lantern’s assets is financed by owners compared with creditors, because the owners’ equity absorbs losses before the bank does. Loan agreements often turn this into a covenant, a condition such as “total liabilities may not exceed 1.5 times equity”, and checking it means working the equation forward for the planned borrowing, exactly as in the applied example.

Investors and owners read the change in equity, not just its level. Kestrel’s equity grew by $60,000, but only $30,000 of that was earned. A business can grow its assets quickly by borrowing or by raising money from new shareholders; the expanded equation shows how much of the growth came from profitable operations, which is the part that can continue without more outside money.

Managers use the dual aspect as a planning check. Before Lantern commits to a $15,000 van, Ana can write the transaction into the equation and see its consequences: cash rises, then turns into a vehicle; a liability appears that must be repaid with interest; equity is untouched until the van starts earning revenue and costing fuel and depreciation. Financial models built in spreadsheets include a “balance check” row for the same reason: if projected assets ever differ from projected liabilities plus equity, the model has an error, and analysts don’t trust its numbers until it balances.

Summary

  • Assets = liabilities + equity, at every moment, because equity is defined as what is left of the assets after liabilities.
  • Every transaction changes at least two items, and the change in assets always equals the change in liabilities plus the change in equity.
  • Equity grows when owners invest or the business earns revenue, and shrinks with expenses and dividends: A = L + CS + RE(beginning) + R − E − D.
  • Net income is revenue minus expenses; dividends are not expenses. Ending retained earnings = beginning retained earnings + net income − dividends.
  • Cash and revenue are different things: a deposit for future work is a liability, and work invoiced on credit is revenue before any cash arrives.
  • A balanced equation proves consistency, not correctness, and equity is an accounting claim, not the market value of the business.

Terms introduced

Accounting equation
Assets = liabilities + equity: the business’s resources equal the claims of creditors and owners on them.
Net assets
Assets minus liabilities; another name for equity.
Dual aspect
The principle that every transaction affects at least two items in the accounting equation, so it stays in balance.
Unearned revenue
Cash received for goods or services not yet delivered. It is a liability until the business delivers them.
Contributed capital
Equity supplied by owners in exchange for shares, recorded in Common Stock (share capital under IFRS).
Retained earnings
Cumulative net income kept in the business since it began, after dividends.
Dividends
Distributions of profit to a corporation’s shareholders. They reduce equity but are not an expense.
Net income
Revenue minus expenses for a period. A negative result is a net loss.
Expanded accounting equation
Assets = liabilities + common stock + beginning retained earnings + revenue − expenses − dividends.
Executory contract
A contract in which neither party has yet performed. It is not recorded in the accounts until one side acts, for example by paying or delivering.
