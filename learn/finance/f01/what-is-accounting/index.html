What accounting is and why businesses need it

Accounting turns what happens to a business into information that owners, lenders, investors and governments can act on. Learn who relies on that information, the four statements it produces, who writes the rules, and the concepts that make the numbers worth trusting.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain accounting as an information system that identifies, records and communicates the economic events of a business.
  • Identify the main users of accounting information and the decisions each one makes with it.
  • Distinguish financial, managerial and tax accounting by their users, rules and purpose.
  • Describe the question each of the four financial statements answers.
  • Explain who sets accounting standards in the United States and internationally, and what the conceptual framework is for.
  • Apply the qualitative characteristics, the four basic assumptions and the basic principles to everyday reporting decisions.

Review first

  • None. This is the first lesson of the course and assumes no accounting background.

The idea

Fictional case

On 1 March 2026 Ana Lim opens Lantern Coffee Co., a small corporation that caters coffee for offices and events and runs barista-training workshops. She puts in $20,000 of her savings, and Coastline Bank lends the company another $10,000. Within a week Lantern has bought an espresso machine, paid for a year of insurance, ordered beans and cups, and taken a deposit from its first corporate client. Ana now faces a question every founder meets: what does she need to write down, and for whom? She wants to know whether the business is making money. The bank wants to know whether it will be repaid. The tax authority will want to know how much tax Lantern owes. All of them need information about the same events, and none of them can see inside Lantern’s bank account and invoices for themselves.

Accounting is the system that turns those events into information people can use. It does three things. It identifies the economic events that belong to the business: buying the espresso machine counts, while a friendly conversation with a possible client does not, and neither does Ana’s personal grocery shopping. It records each event in money, with a date and a description, and sorts it so that similar events can be added up. And it communicates the results, in reports and financial statements, to the people who need them. Accounting, in short, is an information system that identifies, records and communicates the economic events of an organisation to the people who make decisions about it.

Accounting is the process of organizing, analyzing, and communicating financial information that is used for decision-making.

Franklin, Graybeal and Cooper, Principles of Accounting, Volume 1: Financial Accounting (OpenStax, 2019), section 1.1

Recording is the part most people picture, and on its own it is called bookkeeping. Accounting is wider: it decides what should be recorded and how, checks that the records are complete, and interprets them. A bookkeeper can tell Ana that Lantern spent $1,200 on kitchen rent in March; an accountant can help her decide whether the business can afford a second kitchen.

Who uses accounting information

Different people read the same records to make different decisions. It helps to sort them into two groups. Internal users run the business: owners who manage it, like Ana, and the managers and staff who plan, price and control its work. External users are outside it and see only what the business chooses, or is required, to show them.

Users of accounting information and the decisions they make
UserTypical questionDecision it informs
Owners and managersWhich services make money? Can we afford to hire?Pricing, hiring, expanding, cutting costs

For Lantern, three of these users matter from the first month. Ana, as owner and manager, needs to know which of her two lines of business, catering or training, earns more. Coastline Bank needs to know whether Lantern can pay the interest due every three months and repay the $10,000 within the note’s two-year term. And the tax authority (the Internal Revenue Service in the United States) will expect a tax return.

Financial, managerial and tax accounting

Because the users differ, accounting has grown into three main branches. Financial accounting produces general-purpose reports for people outside the business, chiefly investors and lenders, following a common set of rules so that readers can trust and compare them. Managerial accounting (also called management accounting) produces whatever internal reports help managers plan and control the business: budgets, the cost of each catering event, the profit on each workshop. It follows no external rule book and freely mixes money with other measures, such as the number of events catered each week. Tax accounting applies tax law to work out taxable income and the tax due. Its rules come from legislation and the tax authority, not from accounting standard setters, so taxable income and accounting profit often differ.

Main usersInvestors, lenders and other outsidersOwners, managers and staffThe tax authority, and the business itself

The branches draw on the same records, but they can give different answers. Under US tax rules a business can elect to deduct the full cost of certain equipment in the year it starts using it (the section 179 deduction), while its financial statements spread that cost over the years the equipment is used. Neither figure is wrong: they answer different questions under different rules. This course is about financial accounting.

The four financial statements at a glance

Financial accounting communicates mainly through financial statements: structured reports that summarise what happened to a business during a period and where it stands at the end of it. A complete set has four main statements, each answering a different question, plus notes that explain them. You will prepare all four in lesson 10. For now, see what each is for, using the figures Lantern will report for March once its books are complete.

What each statement answers, with Lantern’s figures for March 2026 (fictional)
StatementThe question it answersLantern, March 2026
Income statementDid the business make a profit over the period?Revenue $7,100 − expenses $6,110 = net income $990
Statement of changes in equityHow did the owners’ claim on the business change, and why?Common stock $0 + $20,000 issued; retained earnings $0 + net income $990 − dividends $300 = $690; total equity $20,690
Balance sheetWhat does the business control, and what does it owe, at one moment?At 31 March: assets $42,500 = liabilities $21,810 + equity $20,690
Statement of cash flowsWhere did cash come from, and where did it go?Operating +$950, investing −$4,000, financing +$29,700: cash up $26,650

Each statement comes with notes that explain the figures and the methods behind them.

Two things stand out already. The income statement and the statement of cash flows cover a period (the month of March), while the balance sheet is a snapshot at one moment (the end of 31 March). And profit is not cash: Lantern earned $990 in March, yet its cash rose by $26,650, mostly because Ana and the bank put money in. Telling those two stories apart is one of the main reasons accounting exists.

Key idea: One set of events, many questions

Accounting does not make decisions; it supplies the information for them. The same March events tell Ana whether catering pays, tell the bank whether its loan is safe and tell the tax authority what is owed.

In depth

Who sets the rules

Outsiders can rely on financial statements only if businesses prepare them in a consistent way, so reporting in each jurisdiction follows an agreed body of rules, called generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP). This lesson introduces two standard setters: the American one and the international one.

In the United States the rules are written by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB), an independent, private-sector, not-for-profit organisation established in 1973 and based in Norwalk, Connecticut. Its standards are collected in the FASB Accounting Standards Codification, the source of authoritative US GAAP for nongovernmental entities: public and private companies and not-for-profit organisations.

The FASB’s authority over public companies comes through the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), the federal agency that protects investors in US securities markets. Under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 the SEC requires periodic reports from companies whose securities are publicly traded, and the securities laws let it set the accounting standards those reports use. Rather than write them itself, it recognises the FASB as the designated standard setter for public companies, a status it reaffirmed in 2003. A private company such as Lantern files nothing with the SEC, but its bank or a future investor may ask for GAAP statements, so Lantern keeps its books under US GAAP.

Many other jurisdictions use International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), formally IFRS Accounting Standards, which are written by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB). The IASB is an independent standard-setting body within the IFRS Foundation, a not-for-profit, public interest organisation that set up the IASB in 2001. Each jurisdiction decides whether to adopt them: the IFRS Foundation reports that companies in more than 140 jurisdictions are required to use them, but which companies must use them, and which merely may, varies. In the United States, the SEC requires domestic public companies to use US GAAP. Since a rule issued in December 2007, it has accepted statements prepared under IFRS as issued by the IASB from foreign private issuers (foreign companies whose securities trade in the United States), without a reconciliation to US GAAP.

Standards: US GAAP and IFRS in this course

The mechanics this course teaches (the accounting equation, double-entry recording and the accounting cycle) are the same under both sets of standards. They differ in vocabulary and in many detailed rules for recognising and measuring particular items; where a difference matters here, a box like this one says so.

The conceptual framework

Standards on individual topics, such as leases or revenue, rest on a shared foundation called a conceptual framework: a set of concepts that says what financial reporting is for and what makes information useful. Standard setters use it to keep their standards consistent with one another. Under IFRS, companies also use it to develop an accounting policy when no standard covers a transaction. The FASB’s framework is its Concepts Statement No. 8, issued in chapters, among them the objective and the qualitative characteristics in September 2010, at the end of a joint project with the IASB, and the elements of financial statements in December 2021. The IASB issued its current Conceptual Framework in March 2018. The two agree on most of what follows; the boxes note where they part.

The objective of financial reporting

Both frameworks start from the same objective. The FASB states it this way, and the IASB’s paragraph 1.2 is worded almost identically:

The objective of general purpose financial reporting is to provide financial information about the reporting entity that is useful to existing and potential investors, lenders, and other creditors in making decisions about providing resources to the entity.

FASB Concepts Statement No. 8, Chapter 1, paragraph OB2

Three phrases carry the weight. General purpose means the reports are designed for many readers at once rather than tailored to one. Existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors are the primary users, because they provide the business with money and many of them cannot insist on private reports. Decisions about providing resources covers buying, selling or holding shares and debt, making and settling loans and, the IASB adds, voting on or otherwise influencing how management uses the business’s resources. Both frameworks admit that such reports cannot give users everything they need; users also draw on other sources, such as the state of the economy and the industry.

What makes information useful: the qualitative characteristics

The frameworks name the qualities that make financial information useful. Two are fundamental, and information must have both. Four are enhancing: they make relevant, faithfully represented information more useful, but they cannot rescue information that lacks the fundamental two.

Relevance
Information is relevant if it is capable of making a difference to users’ decisions. It does so through predictive value (it helps users form expectations, as March’s catering revenue helps Ana and the bank estimate April’s), confirmatory value (it confirms or corrects earlier expectations), or both.
Materiality
Information is material if leaving it out or misstating it could change or influence the decisions of the people relying on the report. Whether something is material depends on the size and the nature of the item in one particular business, so no single dollar threshold fits every business.
Faithful representation
Information faithfully represents what it claims to describe if it is complete (nothing a user needs is left out), neutral (not slanted, by the choice of figures or how they are presented, to create a favourable or unfavourable impression) and free from error (no errors or omissions in the description, and a sound process behind any estimate). Free from error does not mean perfectly precise: an estimate, such as how many years the espresso machine will last, can be represented faithfully if it is made with care and clearly described as an estimate.

Standards: Materiality: two framings

The IASB calls materiality an entity-specific aspect of relevance, based on the nature or size of an item in one entity’s report, and says no uniform numerical threshold can be set. The FASB, after amending its framework in August 2018, keeps the two apart: relevance is a general notion about what type of information is useful, while materiality is entity specific, judged by whether the item is large enough, in the circumstances, that the judgement of a reasonable person relying on the report would probably have been changed or influenced. Neither thinks one materiality rule can fit every business.

Materiality is why accountants don’t chase every cent. If Lantern’s bookkeeper misfiled a $3 roll of tape, no reader’s decision would change. But materiality is measured against the reader’s decision, not against the number alone. A $150 error would be trivial for a hotel group earning millions and large for Lantern, whose March profit is $990: about 15% of it. In practice accountants and auditors often begin with a percentage rule of thumb, such as 5% of profit, and the SEC’s staff accepts such a rule as an initial step, but only as the beginning of the analysis. A small error can still be material because of its nature: for example, if it turns a loss into a profit, masks a change in a trend, affects whether a business complies with a loan agreement, or conceals an unlawful transaction (SEC Staff Accounting Bulletin No. 99, 1999).

Both frameworks also recognise a constraint: cost. Information costs time and money to prepare, check and read, and the cost should be justified by the benefit. That is why a small business would sensibly record a $40 milk jug as an expense when it buys it instead of tracking it for years as it does an espresso machine.

Standards: Prudence and conservatism: the frameworks disagree

Many introductory texts, OpenStax’s among them, describe a principle of conservatism: when in doubt, choose the figure that makes assets and profit lower. The FASB’s framework leaves prudence and conservatism out of faithful representation, because a deliberate lean in either direction conflicts with neutrality. The IASB’s 2018 framework reintroduced prudence, defined as caution when making judgements under uncertainty, and says it supports neutrality: assets and income should not be overstated, nor liabilities and expenses understated, but assets and income may not be deliberately understated either, because that only shifts profit into later periods.

The basic assumptions

Textbooks gather the starting points of financial accounting into four assumptions. Each one shapes what Lantern records.

Economic entity assumption
The business is treated as separate from its owners and from every other business, and its records contain only its own events. Ana’s savings account, her car and her mortgage are not Lantern’s, and if Lantern pays one of Ana’s personal bills, that is not a business expense. The assumption applies to a one-owner business too, even though the law does not separate a sole proprietor from her business.
Going concern assumption
Unless there is evidence to the contrary, the business is expected to keep operating for the foreseeable future. That is why Lantern records its espresso machine at cost and spreads that cost over the five years it expects to use it, instead of reporting what the machine would fetch in a forced sale tomorrow. If a business intends or is forced to stop trading, its statements may have to be prepared on a different basis, and they must say so.
Monetary unit assumption
Only events that can be measured in money are recorded, in one currency (US dollars for Lantern), and the currency is treated as a stable unit, so changes in its purchasing power through inflation are ignored. A loyal clientele or a talented barista may be valuable, but neither appears in Lantern’s accounts.
Time period assumption
The life of a business can be divided into artificial periods (months, quarters, years) so that results can be reported while they are still useful. In this course Lantern prepares statements every month so that you can see a whole cycle; most businesses produce monthly figures for their managers but close their books formally once a year, at the end of a twelve-month fiscal year that need not end on 31 December.

Standards: Going concern under US GAAP and IFRS

Both sets of standards require management to assess whether the going concern basis still holds when it prepares statements. Under US GAAP, management evaluates whether there is substantial doubt about the entity’s ability to meet its obligations as they fall due within one year after the date the statements are issued (or available to be issued). Under IFRS, management looks at least twelve months ahead from the end of the reporting period, and further where needed; these requirements move unchanged from IAS 1 to IAS 8 when IFRS 18 takes effect for periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027.

The basic principles

On top of the assumptions, four principles govern how events are recorded. Lesson 8 studies two of them in depth; here is what each says.

  • Historical cost. Under the historical cost principle, assets are recorded at what they cost to acquire: the price paid plus the costs of getting them ready for use. Lantern’s espresso machine and grinder enter the records at $12,000, and stay there even if a similar machine soon costs more or a buyer offers less. Cost is verifiable, since there is an invoice, but it can drift away from current value, which is why the standards require current values for some items, such as certain investments.
  • Revenue recognition. Revenue is recorded when the business delivers the goods or services it promised, not when cash arrives. Lantern earns $4,200 when it caters Meridian Partners’ conference on 12 March, although it is paid later; Harbor Tech’s $2,400 deposit is not revenue until the events take place.
  • Expense recognition. Expenses are recorded in the period in which resources are used up to earn revenue (often summarised as matching expenses with revenue), not when they are paid for. Lantern’s $1,800 insurance policy becomes an expense at $150 a month over the year it covers.
  • Full disclosure. Under the full disclosure principle, the statements and their notes should include everything a reader needs to understand them: the accounting methods used, the terms of a bank loan, a lawsuit filed against the business, a major event after the end of the period. The notes are part of the financial statements, not an optional extra.

Why trust matters

Financial statements are prepared by the managers whose performance they describe, which gives managers a reason to flatter the numbers. Two safeguards make the statements credible. The first is an audit: an independent accountant examines the records and the evidence behind them and gives an opinion on whether the statements present the business’s financial position and results fairly, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable framework, such as US GAAP or IFRS. Public companies in the United States must be audited, and since the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002, passed to combat corporate and accounting fraud, their auditors have been overseen by the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB). A private business such as Lantern is usually audited only when a lender, investor or regulator requires it.

The second safeguard is professional ethics. Certified public accountants in the United States follow the AICPA’s Code of Professional Conduct, whose principles include integrity, objectivity and independence, and due care; an auditor must be independent of the company audited, both in fact and in appearance. Ethics binds every accountant, not only auditors. A bookkeeper who records an owner’s holiday as a business expense, or books next month’s sales this month to meet a target, breaks faithful representation and the trust of every user who relies on the numbers.

Common mistakes

  • “Accounting is just bookkeeping.”

    Bookkeeping, the recording of transactions, is one part of accounting. Deciding what to record and how, checking the records and explaining what they mean for a decision are the rest, and that is where the judgement lies: whether a deposit is revenue yet, whether an error is material, whether a business is still a going concern.

  • “If the business made a profit, it has that much more cash.”

    Profit and cash flow measure different things. Lantern earned $990 in March, but its cash rose by $26,650 because Ana and the bank put money in; in another month a profitable business can run short of cash because its customers have not paid yet. That is why the income statement and the statement of cash flows are separate statements.

  • “The owner’s money and the business’s money are the same thing.”

    Under the economic entity assumption they are kept apart, even in a one-person business. Paying a personal bill from the business account does not create a business expense; it moves resources from the business to its owner, and it must be recorded as exactly that.

  • “US companies can choose GAAP or IFRS, and everyone else uses IFRS.”

    The SEC requires US domestic public companies to use US GAAP; it accepts IFRS statements only from foreign private issuers. Elsewhere, each jurisdiction decides. The IFRS Foundation reports that companies in more than 140 jurisdictions are required to use its standards, but which companies must use them, and which may, varies.

  • “Material means a large dollar amount.”

    Materiality depends on the business and on the nature of the item. $150 is material against Lantern’s $990 monthly profit and immaterial for a hotel group earning millions. A small error is also material if it turns a loss into a profit, masks a trend, decides whether a loan agreement is met or conceals something unlawful.

  • “The balance sheet shows what the business is worth.”

    Most assets are recorded at historical cost, and many valuable things, such as a loyal clientele or a strong reputation built up over time, are not recorded at all because they cannot be measured reliably in money. The balance sheet shows what the business has recorded, measured by the rules; what someone would pay for the business is a separate question.

In practice

Fictional case

Fernway Apparel, a fictional clothing brand with two shops and an online store, asks Saltmarsh Textiles, a fictional fabric supplier, to let it buy up to $60,000 of fabric a month on 60-day credit. Saltmarsh’s credit analyst asks for Fernway’s financial statements for its last two years.

The analyst’s first questions are about the statements themselves, not the numbers in them. Were they audited, reviewed by an outside accountant, or only prepared by Fernway’s owner? Audited statements are more verifiable, because an independent professional has examined the evidence. How old are they? Statements for a year that ended ten months ago are less timely than she would like, so she also asks for Fernway’s recent monthly management accounts, knowing that those are unaudited and follow no external rules. Were both years prepared on the same basis? If Fernway changed the way it measures its inventory of clothing between the two years, the figures are less comparable, and the notes should say so.

Then she reads the notes, which the full disclosure principle requires. One note says that a $400,000 bank loan falls due in eight months and that Fernway is negotiating to refinance it. That sentence matters more to Saltmarsh than any line of the income statement, because a supplier selling on 60-day terms is a creditor too: if the bank is not repaid, suppliers may not be either. She checks whether the auditor’s report or the notes mention substantial doubt about Fernway’s ability to continue as a going concern. They don’t, but she asks to see the bank’s refinancing offer before deciding.

Finally, she judges what is material to her own decision. Fernway’s net income fell from $310,000 to $180,000, which looks alarming, but the notes show that $95,000 of the fall came from the one-off cost of closing a third shop; without it, net income would have been $275,000. She approves credit of $40,000 a month rather than $60,000, with a review in six months, when the refinancing should be settled.

Inside a business the same records serve managerial decisions, under different rules. The manager of a fictional 120-room hotel deciding whether to close its restaurant on Mondays needs the restaurant’s Monday revenue, the food and staff costs that closing would save, and nonfinancial facts such as how many guests eat in on Mondays. No GAAP applies: her report only has to be relevant and accurate enough for that one decision.

Summary

  • Accounting identifies a business’s economic events, records them in money and communicates the results to people who make decisions.
  • Internal users (owners and managers) and external users (investors, lenders, suppliers, customers, employees, tax authorities and regulators) read the same records for different decisions.
  • Financial accounting serves outsiders under US GAAP or IFRS; managerial accounting serves insiders with no imposed rules; tax accounting follows tax law.
  • The income statement, statement of changes in equity, balance sheet and statement of cash flows each answer a different question, and profit is not the same as cash.
  • In the United States the FASB sets GAAP, which the SEC recognises for public companies; the IASB sets IFRS, which companies in more than 140 jurisdictions are required to use.
  • Useful information is relevant (including material) and faithfully represented (complete, neutral and free from error), and is made more useful by comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability, at a cost the benefits justify.
  • Four assumptions (economic entity, going concern, monetary unit, time period) and four principles (historical cost, revenue recognition, expense recognition, full disclosure) shape what is recorded; audits and professional ethics make the result trustworthy.

Terms introduced

Accounting
An information system that identifies the economic events of an organisation, records them in money and communicates the results to the people who make decisions about it.
Financial accounting
The branch of accounting that prepares general-purpose financial statements for outside users, such as investors and lenders, following accounting standards (US GAAP or IFRS).
Managerial accounting
The branch of accounting that prepares internal reports, such as budgets, cost reports and forecasts, to help managers plan, control and decide. It follows no externally imposed rules and often uses nonfinancial measures. Also called management accounting.
Tax accounting
The branch of accounting that applies tax law to work out a business’s taxable income and the tax it owes.
GAAP
Generally accepted accounting principles: the body of standards and conventions that businesses in a jurisdiction follow in financial reporting. US GAAP is set by the FASB.
IFRS
International Financial Reporting Standards (formally IFRS Accounting Standards), written by the IASB. Each jurisdiction decides whether and for which companies to require them.
FASB
The Financial Accounting Standards Board: the independent, private-sector, not-for-profit body, established in 1973, that sets US GAAP and is recognised by the SEC as the accounting standard setter for public companies.
IASB
The International Accounting Standards Board: the independent standard-setting body within the IFRS Foundation that develops IFRS Accounting Standards. It was set up in 2001.
SEC
The Securities and Exchange Commission: the US federal agency, founded in 1934, that protects investors, keeps securities markets fair, orderly and efficient, and facilitates capital formation, and has legal authority over the financial reporting of public companies.
Conceptual framework
A coherent set of concepts underlying accounting standards: the objective of financial reporting, the qualities that make information useful, and the definition, recognition and measurement of the elements of financial statements.
Relevance
The quality of information that is capable of making a difference to users’ decisions, through predictive value, confirmatory value or both. One of the two fundamental qualitative characteristics.
Materiality
Information is material if omitting or misstating it could influence the decisions of the people relying on the report. It depends on the size and nature of the item in a particular business; there is no universal threshold.
Faithful representation
The quality of information that depicts what it claims to depict: complete, neutral and free from error. One of the two fundamental qualitative characteristics.
Economic entity assumption
The assumption that a business is separate from its owners and from other businesses, so its records contain only its own transactions and events.
Going concern assumption
The assumption that, unless there is evidence to the contrary, a business will continue operating for the foreseeable future.
Monetary unit assumption
The assumption that only events measurable in money are recorded, in a currency treated as a stable unit, so changes in its purchasing power are ignored.
Time period assumption
The assumption that a business’s life can be divided into periods, such as months, quarters and years, for which reports are prepared.
Historical cost principle
The principle that assets are recorded at what they cost to acquire and get ready for use, and generally kept at that amount rather than updated to current value.
Full disclosure principle
The principle that the financial statements and their notes should include all the information a reader needs to understand them.
Financial statements
Structured reports of a business’s performance and cash flows for a period and its financial position at a date, prepared under accounting rules: the income statement, statement of changes in equity, balance sheet and statement of cash flows, together with their notes.
Audit
An independent examination of financial statements, after which the auditor gives an opinion on whether they are presented fairly, in all material respects, in accordance with the applicable financial reporting framework.
