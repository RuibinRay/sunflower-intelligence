Debits, credits and normal balances

Debit means the left side of an account and credit means the right, nothing more. Work out from the accounting equation which side increases each kind of account, read normal balances, and calculate the balance of any T-account.

About 85 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain what debit and credit mean in bookkeeping, and why neither means good or bad.
  • Explain, from the accounting equation, why each kind of account increases on the side it does.
  • State the normal balance of assets, liabilities, equity, revenue, expenses, dividends and contra-asset accounts.
  • Calculate the balance of a T-account, and find a missing figure from the other three.
  • Interpret a bank statement’s debits and credits from the bank’s point of view.

Review first

  • The accounting equation — The debit and credit rules are worked out from Assets = Liabilities + Equity and its expanded form.
  • Accounts, terminology and classifications — You need to recognise each account as an asset, liability, equity, revenue or expense account.
  • Adding columns of numbers and solving a one-step equation such as 600 + 2,100 − x = 750.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

By 15 March Lantern has made nine transactions touching twelve accounts, and Ana has been recording them the way you did in the last lesson: a spreadsheet with a column for every account and a plus or minus sign for each change. It already sprawls. To see how much cash Lantern has, she adds a column of mixed signs, and a single minus typed as a plus is easy to make and hard to find. Her accountant suggests the method merchants have used for more than five hundred years: give every account two sides, one for increases and one for decreases, and fix in advance which side each kind of change goes on. Then every amount is entered as a positive number, and one quick test catches a whole class of mistakes.

An account with two sides

An account collects every increase and decrease in one item, such as Cash or Accounts Payable. When accountants sketch an account by hand they draw it as a large letter T, called a T-account: the account’s name sits on the crossbar, and the vertical stroke divides the space below into a left side and a right side.

The left side is the debit side and the right side is the credit side. To debit an account means to enter an amount on its left; to credit an account means to enter an amount on its right. The words are often shortened to Dr and Cr. That is the whole of their meaning in bookkeeping: they name directions, the way port and starboard name the left and right of a ship.

The words come from Latin: debit from debitum, “something owed”, and credit from creditum, from credere, “to trust or entrust”. Centuries of use have worn those meanings away. In the accounts a debit is not a debt, a loss or bad news, and a credit is not income or good news. Whether a debit makes an account bigger or smaller depends entirely on what kind of account it is, and that is what the rest of this lesson works out.

Which side records an increase?

The rule for each kind of account comes straight from the accounting equation. Write it with the resources on the left and the claims on the right:

Assets⏟left=Liabilities+Equity⏟right\underbrace{\text{Assets}}_{\text{left}} = \underbrace{\text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}}_{\text{right}}leftAssets​​=rightLiabilities+Equity​​

The convention is that each account increases on the side where it sits in the equation. Assets are on the left, so an asset increases with a debit and decreases with a credit. Liabilities and equity are on the right, so they increase with a credit and decrease with a debit. When Ana invests $20,000, Cash (an asset) rises, so Cash is debited $20,000; Common Stock (equity) rises, so Common Stock is credited $20,000. One debit, one credit, equal amounts.

This choice is what makes the system work. Every transaction must keep the equation balanced, and with this convention a transaction keeps it balanced exactly when the amounts entered on the left of accounts equal the amounts entered on the right. So every transaction is recorded in at least two accounts with equal debits and credits. That is double-entry bookkeeping, and its rule, that debits equal credits, is the accounting equation in a bookkeeper’s form. The short proof is in the next part of the lesson.

Revenue, expenses and dividends

Revenue, expenses and dividends have accounts of their own, but in the expanded accounting equation all three live inside equity. Ask what each one does to equity and its rule follows:

  • Revenue increases equity. Equity increases with a credit, so revenue accounts increase with a credit. When Lantern invoices Meridian Partners $4,200 for catering, Catering Revenue is credited $4,200.
  • Expenses decrease equity. Equity decreases with a debit, so an expense account grows with debits: it adds up the cost used up, and the bigger it gets, the further equity has fallen. When Lantern pays $1,600 of wages, Wages Expense is debited $1,600.
  • Dividends also decrease equity, so the Dividends account grows with debits, even though dividends are not an expense. Lantern’s $300 dividend is a debit to Dividends.

You can see all of the rules at once by moving expenses and dividends to the other side of the expanded equation, so that nothing is subtracted:

Assets+Expenses+Dividends=Liabilities+Common Stock+Retained Earnings+Revenue\begin{aligned} &\text{Assets} + \text{Expenses} + \text{Dividends} \\ &\quad = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Common Stock} + \text{Retained Earnings} + \text{Revenue} \end{aligned}​Assets+Expenses+Dividends=Liabilities+Common Stock+Retained Earnings+Revenue​

Every kind of account on the left of this version increases with a debit; every kind on the right increases with a credit. Some students remember it as DEA–LER: dividends, expenses and assets on the debit side; liabilities, equity and revenue on the credit side. The mnemonic is a convenience; the equation is the reason.

Balances and normal balances

An account balance is the difference between an account’s total debits and its total credits, and it sits on the side with the larger total. Increases go on one fixed side, and most accounts can’t fall below zero, so their increases add up to at least as much as their decreases. The balance therefore usually sits on the increase side, which is called the account’s normal balance. Assets, expenses and dividends have normal debit balances; liabilities, equity and revenue have normal credit balances.

Here are Lantern’s accounts after its first six transactions, 1–8 March. Read each entry against the rules. On 3 March Equipment, an asset, rose by $12,000: a debit. Cash, an asset, fell by $4,000: a credit. Accounts Payable, a liability, rose by $8,000: a credit. That entry has one debit of $12,000 and two credits that add up to $12,000.

Add the debit balances: 26,600+1,500+1,800+12,000=41,90026{,}600 + 1{,}500 + 1{,}800 + 12{,}000 = 41{,}90026,600+1,500+1,800+12,000=41,900. Add the credit balances: 9,500+2,400+10,000+20,000=41,9009{,}500 + 2{,}400 + 10{,}000 + 20{,}000 = 41{,}9009,500+2,400+10,000+20,000=41,900. They agree, and they match lesson 3, where assets of $41,900 equalled liabilities of $21,900 plus equity of $20,000 after the same six transactions.

Why your bank statement looks backwards

When Harbor Tech’s $2,400 deposit reaches Lantern’s bank account on 8 March, Lantern debits Cash. Lantern’s bank statement, though, lists the same $2,400 as a credit. When Lantern pays its $350 advertising bill from the account, Lantern credits Cash, while the statement shows a debit. People who first meet these words on their own bank statements often learn the rules exactly backwards.

Nothing is wrong: the statement is written from the bank’s side. The money in Lantern’s account is money the bank owes Lantern. To the bank, a customer’s deposit is a liability, an IOU it must repay. When the deposit arrives, the bank’s liability to Lantern rises, and a liability increases with a credit, so the bank credits Lantern’s account. When Lantern spends, the bank owes it less, so the bank debits the account, which is what happens every time you pay with a debit card. A loan works the same way in reverse: Lantern’s $10,000 note is a liability in Lantern’s books and an asset, a loan it expects to be repaid, in Coastline Bank’s.

Key idea: Debit means left, nothing more

A debit is not an expense and a credit is not income. A debit to Cash is money coming in; a credit to Cash is money going out. Before deciding whether a debit is good or bad news, ask which account it is in and whose books you are reading.

In depth

Why debits must equal credits

The rule that each entry’s debits equal its credits isn’t a separate law of bookkeeping. It follows from the equation and the side convention. Every transaction keeps the equation in balance, so the changes it causes satisfy

Δ Assets=Δ Liabilities+Δ Equity\Delta\,\text{Assets} = \Delta\,\text{Liabilities} + \Delta\,\text{Equity}ΔAssets=ΔLiabilities+ΔEquity

Under the convention, the change in an asset account is its debits minus its credits, while the change in a liability or equity account is its credits minus its debits. Write DDD for the debits a transaction enters in a group of accounts and CCC for its credits, with AAA, LLL and EEE for assets, liabilities and equity (revenue, expenses and dividends count as equity here). Substituting:

DA−CA=(CL−DL)+(CE−DE)D_A - C_A = (C_L - D_L) + (C_E - D_E)DA​−CA​=(CL​−DL​)+(CE​−DE​)

Move every debit to the left and every credit to the right:

DA+DL+DE=CA+CL+CED_A + D_L + D_E = C_A + C_L + C_EDA​+DL​+DE​=CA​+CL​+CE​

The left-hand side is all the debits in the transaction and the right-hand side is all its credits. So a transaction keeps the equation balanced if, and only if, its debits equal its credits. Three consequences follow.

  • Equal amounts, not equal lines. An entry can have one debit and two credits, as Lantern’s equipment purchase did, or any other mix. Only the totals must match.
  • All the balances agree too. If every entry balances, the total of all debit balances equals the total of all credit balances at any moment. After all fifteen March transactions, Lantern’s debit balances add up to $46,860 and so do its credit balances. Lesson 7 builds the trial balance on this fact.
  • Signs would work as well. Store debits as positive numbers and credits as negative ones and every balanced entry adds up to zero. You will often meet this form in spreadsheets and in exports from accounting systems, where credit balances appear with minus signs. Before its month-end adjustments, Lantern’s Catering Revenue would show as −4,200: a credit balance of $4,200, not negative revenue.

Calculating an account balance

To find a balance, total each side, subtract the smaller total from the larger, and write the difference on the side of the larger total. If the account began the period with a balance, include it as the first amount on its side. Here is Lantern’s Cash account for the whole of March:

Debits: 20,000+10,000+2,400+1,500+3,000=36,90020{,}000 + 10{,}000 + 2{,}400 + 1{,}500 + 3{,}000 = 36{,}90020,000+10,000+2,400+1,500+3,000=36,900. Credits: 4,000+1,800+350+1,000+1,600+1,200+300=10,2504{,}000 + 1{,}800 + 350 + 1{,}000 + 1{,}600 + 1{,}200 + 300 = 10{,}2504,000+1,800+350+1,000+1,600+1,200+300=10,250. Balance: 36,900−10,250=26,65036{,}900 - 10{,}250 = 26{,}65036,900−10,250=26,650, a debit balance, as an asset should have. The account has seven credit entries and only five debit entries: the balance follows the larger total, not the side with more entries.

For an account with a normal debit balance, the ending balance is the beginning balance plus debits minus credits; for a normal credit balance, it is the beginning balance plus credits minus debits. Both say the same thing: start with what was there, add the increases and take away the decreases. Because the relationship has four parts, you can find any one of them from the other three, which is useful when a figure is missing.

Normal and abnormal balances

A balance on the side opposite the normal balance is called an abnormal balance. It is the first thing an experienced bookkeeper looks for, because most of the time it means an error: an amount entered on the wrong side, or in the wrong account. Some accounts can never have one. Supplies with a credit balance would mean Lantern held less than no coffee; Accumulated Depreciation with a debit balance would mean the equipment had been used up by less than nothing.

Other accounts can have an abnormal balance for a real reason, and then the balance tells you something:

  • Cash with a credit balance: the bank account is overdrawn, so the business owes its bank money. That is a liability, not an asset.
  • Accounts Receivable with a credit balance: a customer has paid more than it owed, so the business owes the customer.
  • Retained Earnings with a debit balance: the losses since the business began have been larger than its profits. A debit balance in retained earnings is called a deficit, and young companies often have one.

Contra accounts

A contra account is linked to another account and reduces it, so it takes the opposite normal balance. Lantern’s first one appears at the end of March. Its equipment cost $12,000 and is expected to last five years, and in lesson 9 you will record March’s depreciation of $200, the share of the cost used up in the month. Lantern won’t credit the Equipment account. It will credit Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment, a contra-asset account that grows with credits and has a normal credit balance:

The balance sheet shows the two together: equipment at its cost of $12,000, less accumulated depreciation of $200, a carrying amount (book value) of $11,800. Keeping the cost and the used-up part in separate accounts preserves both numbers, while crediting Equipment directly would lose the original cost. Other contra accounts work the same way, and you will meet them in later courses: an allowance for doubtful accounts reduces receivables and has a normal credit balance; sales returns reduce revenue and have a normal debit balance; treasury stock reduces equity and has a normal debit balance. Some textbooks call Dividends a contra-equity account for the same reason.

Standards: A convention, not a reporting standard

US GAAP and IFRS set out what the financial statements must report; the debit and credit rules in this lesson are bookkeeping conventions and are the same under both. What changes between frameworks and countries is the vocabulary: an IFRS or UK reporter may write share capital for common stock, trade payables for accounts payable and trade receivables for accounts receivable.

Note: Sole proprietors

In a business owned by one person without incorporating, an owner’s capital account takes the place of Common Stock and Retained Earnings, and withdrawals (drawings) take the place of Dividends. The rules don’t change: capital increases with a credit, and withdrawals reduce equity, so they increase with a debit.

Where double entry came from

Double entry grew up among the merchants of late-medieval Italy. Its first printed description appeared in Venice in 1494, in the Summa de arithmetica, geometria, proportioni et proportionalita of Luca Pacioli, a Franciscan friar and mathematician. Pacioli didn’t invent the method: he set out a practice merchants already used, including recording debits on the left and credits on the right so that each transaction balances. A handwritten bookkeeping manual of 1475 by Marino de Raphaeli, which shows how the method was taught in Venice, is older still (Sangster, 2015). More than five centuries on, the core of the method, equal debits and credits for every transaction, is unchanged.

Common mistakes

  • “A debit is an expense, or bad news.”

    Debit names the left side and nothing else. The largest debit Lantern made in March was $20,000 to Cash when Ana invested, which is good news by any standard. Expenses increase with debits because they reduce equity, but so do assets, for the opposite reason. Ask which account a debit is in before deciding what it means.

  • “Debit means increase and credit means decrease.”

    That is true only for assets, expenses and dividends. For liabilities, equity and revenue it is the other way round: paying the supplier $1,000 on 18 March was a debit to Accounts Payable, and it reduced the debt. Work from the account’s place in the equation, not from a single rule.

  • “My bank credited my account, so I should credit Cash.”

    The bank writes from its own side, where your deposit is a liability. A credit on the statement is a debit to Cash in your books, and a debit on the statement, such as a fee or a card payment, is a credit to Cash.

  • “Accumulated depreciation is an asset, so it increases with a debit.”

    A contra account takes the opposite rules from the account it reduces. Accumulated Depreciation—Equipment is listed with the assets, but it increases with a credit, has a normal credit balance and is subtracted from Equipment on the balance sheet.

  • “The balance goes on the side with more entries.”

    It goes on the side with the larger total. Lantern’s March Cash account has seven credits and five debits, but the debits total $36,900 against $10,250 of credits, so the balance is a debit of $26,650.

  • “Every entry has one debit and one credit.”

    Every entry needs at least one of each and equal totals, not equal numbers of lines. The 3 March equipment purchase has one debit of $12,000 and two credits, of $4,000 and $8,000.

In practice

Bookkeepers read bank statements every week, and the first skill is translating them. Each credit on Lantern’s statement should match a debit to Cash in Lantern’s books, and each debit on the statement a credit to Cash; the formal comparison, called a bank reconciliation, explains any difference as either timing or error. A bookkeeper who forgets whose books a statement describes will post every bank line to the wrong side. Lantern’s healthy $26,650 of cash at the end of March would then appear as a $26,650 credit balance: an overdraft that doesn’t exist.

Controllers and auditors look for wrong-side balances early in any review, because an abnormal balance is the cheapest error signal in accounting. A credit balance in Supplies, a debit balance in Unearned Revenue or a debit in Accumulated Depreciation almost always means an entry went to the wrong side or the wrong account, and it is far cheaper to correct in the books than after the statements are published. A credit balance in Cash or Accounts Receivable is a question rather than a verdict: the business may genuinely owe its bank or a customer, and then the amount belongs with the liabilities.

Analysts meet debits and credits in signed form. A finance team that pulls a list of account balances into a spreadsheet often receives credit balances as negative numbers, so revenue, liabilities and equity arrive with minus signs while assets and expenses are positive. A complete list should then add up to zero; if it doesn’t, rows are missing or duplicated. Before building a report the analyst reverses the signs of the credit-balance accounts so that revenue reads as a positive number, and notes having done so. Skipping that step is an easy way to misread a report: a rise in revenue shows up as a larger negative number.

Finally, the rule that debits equal credits is built into the tools. Accounting software typically refuses to post an entry whose debits and credits differ, so a balanced set of books tells you little about whether the right accounts were chosen. That choice starts with the source document, and it is the subject of the next lesson: journal entries.

Summary

  • Debit means the left side of an account and credit the right. Neither means good or bad, and a debit is not an expense.
  • Each account increases on the side where it sits in the accounting equation: assets with debits, liabilities and equity with credits.
  • Revenue increases equity, so it increases with a credit; expenses and dividends reduce equity, so they increase with a debit. Assets + Expenses + Dividends = Liabilities + Common Stock + Retained Earnings + Revenue.
  • Every entry’s debits equal its credits, which is the same as saying the equation stays balanced. Totals must match; numbers of lines need not.
  • An account’s balance is the difference between its debit and credit totals, on the side of the larger total. The normal balance is the side that records increases; a balance on the other side is usually an error, and occasionally a real overdraft, customer credit or deficit.
  • Contra accounts such as Accumulated Depreciation take the opposite normal balance to the account they reduce.
  • Bank statements are written from the bank’s side: your deposit is the bank’s liability, so the bank credits your account when money comes in.

Terms introduced

Debit
The left side of an account, or an amount entered there (abbreviated Dr). A debit increases assets, expenses and dividends and decreases liabilities, equity and revenue.
Credit
The right side of an account, or an amount entered there (abbreviated Cr). A credit increases liabilities, equity and revenue and decreases assets, expenses and dividends.
T-account
A sketch of an account shaped like a letter T, with debits to the left of the vertical line and credits to the right. Used for teaching and analysis; ledgers kept in practice use columns.
Normal balance
The side on which an account records increases, and so the side on which its balance usually sits: debit for assets, expenses and dividends; credit for liabilities, equity, revenue and contra-asset accounts.
Double-entry bookkeeping
The system of recording every transaction in at least two accounts with equal debits and credits, so that the accounting equation stays in balance.
Account balance
The difference between an account’s total debits and total credits, including any balance brought forward, stated on the side of the larger total.
