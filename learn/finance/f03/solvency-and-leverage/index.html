Solvency and leverage

Measure how far a business is financed by borrowing and how easily it carries the cost: debt and leverage ratios, interest and EBITDA coverage, net debt to EBITDA, leases and vendor finance as debt, and what to do when equity turns negative.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate the debt ratio, liabilities to equity, debt to equity, debt to capital and the equity multiplier, and explain how they relate.
  • Calculate interest coverage, EBITDA coverage and net debt to EBITDA, and interpret them for a lender.
  • Identify what counts as debt, including vendor finance such as an instalment plan and, as an adjustment, operating lease liabilities under ASC 842.
  • Adjust leverage and coverage ratios for leases consistently, so that debt and earnings are measured on the same basis.
  • Explain why debt to equity and return on equity lose their meaning when equity is negative, using Starbucks.

Review first

  • Liquidity — Liquidity looks at the coming year; solvency at the whole capital structure. Lantern’s 2027 balance sheet is the same.
  • Capital and liquidity — Debt, equity, leverage and the debt-to-equity ratio were introduced there.
  • Value and valuation words — EBIT, EBITDA and net debt are defined there.
  • The brewer’s instalment plan and its implied rate from F04 (annuities) help but are not essential.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

At the end of 2026 Lantern was financed mostly by Ana: equity of $42,387 against $30,000 of loans (the Coastline note and the van note). By the end of 2027 its equity had grown to $62,818, but it also owed $65,250 of interest-bearing debt and $173,379 of lease liabilities for the two cafés. Coastline’s credit officer has a second question after liquidity: how much of Lantern now belongs, in effect, to its lenders and landlords, and how comfortably does it earn their interest?

Solvency is the ability to meet all obligations over the long run, not just those due this year. A business is solvent when its assets are worth more than everything it owes and when its earnings can carry the cost of its debt. Leverage (in the UK, gearing) is the use of borrowed money to finance the business. Leverage is not bad in itself: interest is fixed, so when the business earns more than the debt costs, the owners keep the difference. But fixed payments must be made in bad years too, so leverage magnifies losses as well as gains, and every lender asks how much there is.

Solvency ratios come in two families. Leverage ratios compare stocks on the balance sheet: how much of the assets is financed by liabilities or debt rather than by equity. Coverage ratios compare flows: how many times the year’s earnings cover the interest, or how many years of earnings the debt represents. A lender wants both, because a business can have little debt and weak earnings, or a lot of debt and strong earnings.

Every leverage measure rose and every coverage measure fell. Liabilities went from 54.8% to 80.8% of total assets, and for every dollar of equity Lantern now has $4.22 of liabilities rather than $1.21. Interest coverage fell from 31.43 to 11.39 times. Lantern held more cash than debt at the end of 2026 (negative net debt); a year later its debt exceeded its cash by $58,719.

Two different stories sit inside those numbers. Interest-bearing debt a little more than doubled, from $30,000 to $65,250, and debt to equity rose modestly, from 0.71 to 1.04. Total liabilities rose more than fivefold, from $51,289 to $265,065, and almost two-thirds of the 2027 total is one item: the cafés’ lease liabilities. Whether leases are debt is therefore the most important judgement in Lantern’s solvency analysis, and this lesson takes it slowly.

Key idea: Say which “debt” you mean

Analysts use “debt” for at least three things: total liabilities, interest-bearing debt, and interest-bearing debt plus lease liabilities. Lantern’s 2027 “debt to equity” is 4.22, 1.04 or 3.80 depending on the choice. None is wrong; mixing them is. This course uses interest-bearing debt unless it says otherwise, and calls the first ratio liabilities to equity.

In depth

Leverage ratios and how they connect

The leverage ratios all compare the two ways a business finances its assets: by owing (liabilities) or by owning (equity). Because total assets = total liabilities + equity, they carry much the same information and can be converted into each other:

Lantern 2027: 1+4.22=5.221 + 4.22 = 5.221+4.22=5.22, and 1÷(1−0.808)=5.221 \div (1 - 0.808) = 5.221÷(1−0.808)=5.22. The equity multiplier says Lantern controls $5.22 of assets for each dollar of equity; it reappears in the DuPont analysis of return on equity in module 3. The debt-to-capital ratio uses interest-bearing debt only: of the long-term funding provided by lenders and owners together, how much came from lenders? Lantern’s rose from 41.4% to 50.9%.

Which liabilities matter? Trade payables, wages, taxes and unearned revenue are owed, but they arise from operating and they carry no interest; a lender worries most about claims that rank alongside its own and charge interest. Lantern’s 2027 liabilities split three ways: $65,250 of interest-bearing debt (24.6%), $173,379 of lease liabilities (65.4%) and $26,436 of operating liabilities (10.0%).

Vendor finance is debt

Lantern bought its cold-brew brewer on the supplier’s plan: 24 monthly payments of $1,050, $25,200 in all, for a machine whose cash price is $24,000. It recorded the brewer and an instalment note at $24,000, and the $1,200 difference is interest, spread over the plan at its implied interest rate (F04 found it cheaper than Coastline’s 7.2% loan). The supplier is a lender in all but name, and the plan is interest-bearing debt with a repayment schedule. Vendor financing of this kind must be counted with the bank loans; had Lantern recorded it with its accounts payable, its debt ratios would look lower than its real borrowing.

Coverage: can earnings carry the debt?

Interest coverage (times interest earned) divides EBIT by interest expense. EBIT is used because interest is paid out of earnings before tax; for Lantern, which has no income outside its operations, EBIT is operating income. Lantern’s fell from 37,716÷1,200=31.4337{,}716 \div 1{,}200 = 31.4337,716÷1,200=31.43 to 32,787÷2,879=11.3932{,}787 \div 2{,}879 = 11.3932,787÷2,879=11.39. EBITDA coverage adds back depreciation and amortisation, which use no cash in the year, and fell less steeply, from 35.43 to 18.27. Because both earnings and interest are flows over the same period, coverage ratios are not distorted by 2026’s ten months.

Two cautions. First, 2026’s EBIT was earned before Ana took any salary; the $48,000 she was paid in 2027 alone is larger than 2027’s EBIT, so 2026’s 31.43 flatters the business. Second, coverage counts interest, not principal. $51,091 of Lantern’s debt principal falls due in 2028 (the $24,000 line may be renewed, but the bank need not renew it), more than the $42,231 its 2027 EBITDA would leave after interest and tax. A lender looking at repayments as well uses a debt-service measure, comparing cash flow with interest plus scheduled principal.

Net debt to EBITDA asks how many years of EBITDA would repay the debt, net of cash. Net debt is debt minus cash; Lantern’s was −$12,940 at the end of 2026 (more cash than debt) and $58,719 at the end of 2027, or 1.12 times EBITDA. Because it divides a balance by a flow, a part-year needs annualising: 2026’s ten-month EBITDA of $42,516 is $51,019 a year, giving −0.25 rather than −0.30. Lenders often set a maximum for this ratio in loan agreements, and define both sides carefully.

Leases as debt

An operating lease commits the lessee to fixed payments for years, like a loan used to buy the asset. Since ASC 842 (effective for public companies for periods beginning after 15 December 2018, and for other entities three years later), lessees record that commitment: a right-of-use asset and a lease liability at the present value of the payments. Lantern’s cafés added $202,255 of each on 1 March 2027, discounted at 7.2%; by the year end the liability was $173,379. The liability is on the balance sheet, so it is already in the debt ratio and liabilities to equity. Whether it also belongs in “debt” is the analyst’s choice: ASC 842 itself characterises operating lease liabilities as operating liabilities rather than debt, so adding them to debt is an adjustment the analyst makes, and states, to see the business’s fixed commitments in full.

Standards: Operating leases in the statements under ASC 842 and IFRS 16

Under US GAAP an operating lease’s cost is a single lease expense within income from continuing operations (Lantern’s Operating Lease Cost, $4,000 a month, $40,000 for ten months). The interest on the liability is inside that cost, so it is not in interest expense, EBIT is after it, and the payments are operating cash outflows. IFRS 16, effective for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2019, has a single lessee model: apart from leases of 12 months or less and of low-value assets, every lease is accounted for like a purchase on credit, with depreciation of the right-of-use asset and interest on the liability. The same café would therefore show higher EBIT and EBITDA, and more interest, under IFRS than under US GAAP. Compare across the two frameworks only after adjusting.

If the lease liability is counted as debt, the income statement must be adjusted to match, or the ratios mix two bases. Treat the lease as a loan throughout: split the $40,000 lease cost into its interest part, $11,124 (the accretion on the liability), and its amortisation part, $28,876. Move the interest below EBIT into interest expense, and add the amortisation to depreciation and amortisation.

The adjustment changes the picture more than any other in Lantern’s analysis. Interest coverage falls from 11.39 to 3.14: once the cafés’ rent is treated as financing, nearly a third of Lantern’s earnings before financing costs go to its lenders and landlords. Net debt to EBITDA rises from 1.12 to 2.51. The unadjusted figures are not wrong, but they leave out a five-year commitment that is as fixed as a loan’s.

One detail of the adjustment matters for comparisons. It keeps ASC 842’s amortisation figure ($28,876), which early in a lease is below straight-line depreciation of the right-of-use asset, because the liability’s interest is highest at the start. Under IFRS 16 the same café would depreciate the asset straight-line, 202,255÷60×10=33,709202{,}255 \div 60 \times 10 = 33{,}709202,255÷60×10=33,709 for ten months, so its EBIT would be about $39,078 and its interest coverage 2.79 rather than 3.14. EBITDA, $92,587, is the same either way, because both depreciation and interest are added back.

When equity is negative

Equity is a residual: assets minus liabilities. When a company pays its owners more, over the years, than it earns, or makes large losses, equity can fall below zero, and the company reports a shareholders’ deficit. Starbucks is the best-known example among large companies.

Starbucks’ operating income includes 301.2 of income from equity investees. Source: Form 10-K for the fiscal year ended 29 September 2024.

A debt-to-equity ratio of −2.09 does not mean Starbucks has negative leverage; it means the ratio has stopped working. As equity approaches zero, debt ÷ equity and the equity multiplier rise without limit, and debt to capital climbs past 100% (Starbucks’ is 191.6%); when equity crosses zero the first two flip sign and any comparison breaks. Return on equity fails the same way. With negative equity, use measures that don’t divide by it: debt ratio, debt to EBITDA and coverage. By those, Starbucks covers its interest 9.62 times and its net debt is 1.72 times EBITDA: a company with heavy obligations that its earnings carry comfortably.

Where the deficit came from matters too. Starbucks already had a shareholders’ deficit of $5,314.5 million at the start of fiscal 2022. Over fiscal 2022 to 2024 it paid $7,280.1 million in dividends and $6,264.1 million to repurchase its own shares, $13,544.2 million in all, against net earnings attributable to Starbucks of $11,167.0 million: $2,377.2 million more than it earned. Paying out more than it earned, financed partly by borrowing, is a choice about capital structure, not a sign of losses. A small business with negative equity from accumulated losses is a very different case, and the debt ratio alone won’t tell them apart: read the statement of changes in equity.

For comparison, Harbourline Roasters, the fictional roaster from F00, has a debt ratio of 54.5%, debt to equity of 0.70, interest coverage of 5.55 and net debt of 1.77 times EBITDA, close to Lantern’s 2026 leverage but with much weaker coverage.

Common mistakes

  • “Debt to equity and liabilities to equity are the same ratio.”

    They share a denominator, not a numerator. Lantern’s 2027 liabilities to equity is 4.22; its interest-bearing debt to equity is 1.04. Textbooks and data providers use both names loosely, so state your definition and use the same one for every company you compare.

  • “Operating leases aren’t debt because the payments are operating expenses.”

    The payments are as fixed as a loan’s, and since ASC 842 the obligation is on the balance sheet. Counting the liability as debt is a choice, but leaving it out treats Lantern as if it owned its cafés outright. Show the ratios both ways when leases are large.

  • “Add the leases to debt and keep EBITDA as reported.”

    That puts the leases in the numerator and their cost in the denominator too: Lantern’s net debt to EBITDA comes out at 4.41 instead of 2.51. If the lease is debt, its cost is financing: add the lease cost back to EBITDA, and move its interest part into interest expense for coverage.

  • Using net income for interest coverage

    Net income is after interest and tax, so it understates what is available to pay interest: 22,431÷2,879=7.7922{,}431 \div 2{,}879 = 7.7922,431÷2,879=7.79 instead of 11.39 for Lantern. Use EBIT, the earnings before the interest you are testing.

  • “Negative debt to equity means low leverage.”

    Starbucks’ −2.09 comes from negative equity, a shareholders’ deficit, not from negative debt. When equity is negative or tiny, debt to equity, the equity multiplier and return on equity are not meaningful; use the debt ratio, debt to EBITDA and coverage instead.

  • “High interest coverage means the debt is safe.”

    Coverage ignores principal. Lantern covers its 2027 interest 11.39 times but $51,091 of its debt principal falls due in 2028, and its lease payments as well. Look at the repayment schedule and the cash flow that will meet it.

In practice

Coastline’s credit review, continued. The credit officer records Lantern’s leverage both ways and writes one sentence on each. Excluding leases, Lantern is moderately geared (debt to equity 1.04, net debt 1.12 times EBITDA) and covers its interest more than eleven times. Including leases, it is heavily geared (3.80, and 2.51 times lease-adjusted EBITDA) with coverage of about three times. She notes that most of the interest-bearing debt is short-term (the line of credit, the Coastline note and the brewer plan’s 2028 payments) and that the business will need to refinance part of it in 2028, which links solvency back to the liquidity review.

Ana’s capital structure. Ana has financed growth almost entirely with borrowing and leases: no new shares in 2027, and a dividend of $2,000. That kept her ownership at 100% but left equity thin. Hollis Avery’s planned investment in 2028 would lower every leverage ratio at once; the question for Ana is the price of that equity against the interest and the covenants of more debt. F05 develops the trade-off; this lesson gives her the measures to see it.

Reading a large company. An analyst reviewing Starbucks for a bond fund ignores its debt to equity, which is meaningless, and its return on equity, which is too. She looks at coverage (9.62 times), net debt to EBITDA (1.72, before leases) and the size of the lease liabilities, $10,234.7 million, about two-thirds as large as the company’s financial debt. Then she reads the notes for the maturity of both, because a company that pays out more than it earns relies on refinancing each maturity as it falls due.

Summary

  • Solvency is the ability to meet all obligations over the long run; leverage is financing with borrowed money, which magnifies both gains and losses.
  • Leverage ratios (debt ratio, liabilities to equity, debt to equity, debt to capital, equity multiplier) compare balances and convert into one another: equity multiplier = 1 + liabilities ÷ equity.
  • Coverage ratios compare flows: EBIT or EBITDA against interest expense; net debt to EBITDA gives the years of earnings the debt represents. Annualise part-year EBITDA when dividing a balance by it.
  • Lantern 2026 → 2027: debt ratio 54.8% → 80.8%; debt to equity 0.71 → 1.04; interest coverage 31.43 → 11.39; net debt −$12,940 → $58,719 (1.12 times EBITDA).
  • Vendor finance, such as Lantern’s brewer plan at an implied 4.73%, is interest-bearing debt and belongs with the bank loans.
  • Operating lease liabilities are on the balance sheet under ASC 842; counting them as debt is a judgement that must be applied consistently: add the lease cost back to EBITDA and its interest to interest expense. Lantern: debt to equity 3.80, coverage 3.14, net debt to EBITDA 2.51.
  • With negative equity (Starbucks: a deficit of $7,441.6 million), debt to equity and ROE are meaningless; use the debt ratio, debt to EBITDA and coverage.

Total liabilities divided by total assets: the share of the assets financed by what the business owes rather than by its owners.
Equity multiplier
Total assets divided by shareholders’ equity: the dollars of assets controlled for each dollar of equity. It equals 1 plus liabilities to equity.
Debt-to-capital ratio
Interest-bearing debt divided by the sum of interest-bearing debt and equity: the share of the long-term funding provided by lenders.
Interest coverage ratio
Earnings before interest and taxes divided by interest expense: how many times the period’s earnings cover its interest. Also called times interest earned.
Vendor financing
Credit given by the seller of an asset, such as an instalment plan for equipment, so that the buyer pays over time; it is a loan from the seller and is counted as debt.
Right-of-use asset
A lessee’s right to use a leased asset for the lease term, recognised as an asset at the start of the lease, usually at the amount of the lease liability.
Lease liability
A lessee’s obligation to make the lease payments, measured at their present value at a discount rate such as the lessee’s incremental borrowing rate, and reduced as payments are made.
Shareholders’ deficit
Negative shareholders’ equity: total liabilities exceed total assets, usually because of accumulated losses or because distributions to owners have exceeded earnings.
