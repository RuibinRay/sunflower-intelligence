Distress scores

Combine liquidity, accumulated profit, earning power and leverage into one score with Altman’s Z-score models, place a business in its zone, and judge when the score can and cannot be trusted.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate Altman’s Z, Z′ and Z″ scores from a balance sheet and income statement, and place a business in the distress, grey or safe zone.
  • Explain what each ratio in the score measures and which ones moved Lantern from 6.47 in 2026 to 0.24 in 2027.
  • Choose the version of the model that fits a business (public or private, manufacturer or not) and show how the choice changes the zone.
  • Evaluate the limits of the scores: their samples, operating leases on the balance sheet, part-year periods and negative book equity.
  • Distinguish a distress score from a forecast or a probability of default, and name other approaches to credit risk.

Review first

  • Liquidity — Working capital, the first ratio in every Z-score, and why Lantern’s fell below zero in 2027.
  • Solvency and leverage — Equity against liabilities, and operating leases as debt under ASC 842.
  • Cash flow and earnings quality — The quality of the EBIT and retained earnings that the score uses.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Coastline Bank (fictional, like Lantern) screens every small-business borrower with a distress score before a loan officer reads the file. For Lantern the screen gives 6.47 on its 2026 statements, well inside the safe zone, and 0.24 on its 2027 statements, inside the distress zone. The loan officer has to decide what that drop means, and whether the model was built for a business like Lantern at all.

Each ratio you have met in this course answers one question: can the business pay its bills this year, how much does it owe, how hard do its assets work, how profitable is it? A lender needs one answer to a different question: is this business likely to fail? In 1968 Edward Altman looked for a combination of ratios that would separate firms that went bankrupt from those that didn’t. His result, the Altman Z-score, is still the most widely taught distress score.

Altman took 66 US manufacturing companies, 33 that had filed for bankruptcy between 1946 and 1965 and 33 that had not, and used multiple discriminant analysis: a statistical method that finds the weights on several variables that best separate two groups. The weighted sum is a single score. Firms that later failed tended to have low scores; healthy firms high ones. Between them lay a range where the model made mistakes, which Altman called the zone of ignorance, now usually called the grey zone.

The five ratios

Every version of the score is built from some or all of the same five ratios. Each captures a different way a business can be weak.

  • X1X_1X1​ = working capital ÷ total assets. Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities: the short-term cushion. A firm in trouble usually sees it shrink as losses eat its current assets and it borrows short term.
  • X2X_2X2​ = retained earnings ÷ total assets. Retained earnings are the profits a business has kept since it began. The ratio measures cumulative profitability, and it is low for young firms, which fail more often.
  • X3X_3X3​ = EBIT ÷ total assets. EBIT (earnings before interest and tax) over assets is the earning power of the assets, whatever the financing. It carries the largest weight in the original model.
  • X4X_4X4​ = equity ÷ total liabilities. How far the value of the business could fall before what it owes exceeds what it is worth. The original model uses the market value of equity; the private-firm versions use book equity.
  • X5X_5X5​ = sales ÷ total assets. Asset turnover: how much revenue the assets generate. It varies so much between industries that the non-manufacturer model leaves it out.

Read the terms rather than only the total. Working capital, the biggest single change, went from a cushion worth 2.11 points to a deficit costing 1.11: at the end of 2027 current liabilities exceeded current assets by $55,433, because the cash went into the cafés, the line of credit was drawn, the Coastline note fell due within the year and the cafés’ lease payments due in 2028 became current liabilities. Earning power fell from 2.71 to 0.67 points as EBIT dropped and total assets more than tripled. Equity against liabilities fell from 0.87 to 0.25 points, mostly because $173,379 of lease liabilities arrived on the balance sheet.

Key idea: A score is a screen, not a verdict

The zones describe where firms in Altman’s samples fell, not what will happen to Lantern. A score of 0.24 says Lantern’s 2027 balance sheet looks like those of firms that failed. The loan officer’s job is to find out why, and the reasons here (a fit-out paid in cash, leases now on the balance sheet, $30,840 of revenue due in March 2028) are exactly what the score cannot see.

In depth

Where the models came from

The original Z was fitted to manufacturers whose shares were traded, which is why it uses the market value of equity in X4X_4X4​. Altman’s 1968 article wrote the first four ratios as percentages, so its published weights read 0.012, 0.014, 0.033 and 0.006 (and 0.999 on sales); his 2000 paper restates them as 1.2, 1.4, 3.3 and 0.6 for ratios written as decimals. Use the second set with decimals: mixing them is a classic error.

Most firms that borrow from banks have no share price. Altman re-estimated the model for private firms with book equity in X4X_4X4​: that is Z′, whose weights and zones differ because book and market values differ. He then fitted Z″ without X5X_5X5​, to reduce the industry effect that asset turnover brings: a retailer or café turns its assets over far faster than a steel mill, which would make it look safer than it is. Z″ was developed for non-manufacturers and for companies in emerging markets; in that use Altman added a constant of 3.25, so that a score of zero corresponds to a bond in default, and calibrated the scores to US bond ratings.

What the sources say about the zones

Standards: Checking the cut-offs

Textbooks and websites quote Altman’s zones with small differences, so it is worth knowing what he wrote. His 2000 working paper describes the original model’s cut-off of 2.675 and recommends the lower bound of the zone of ignorance, 1.81, as a more realistic cut-off; his later presentations show the original model’s zones as below 1.8 (distress), 1.8 to 2.99 (grey) and above 2.99 (safe). For Z′ the 2000 paper gives a grey area of 1.23 to 2.90 (one of its tables prints 1.21). For Z″ Altman’s 2017 presentation, which writes the model with its +3.25 constant and titles it simply Z″ (his 2000 paper calls the scores on this scale EM scores), gives below 4.35 (distress) and above 5.85 (safe); taking off the constant gives the 1.10 and 2.60 used for the plain Z″ in this course.

The constant matters when you read someone else’s analysis. Lantern’s 2027 Z″ is 0.24 without the constant and 0.24+3.25=3.490.24 + 3.25 = 3.490.24+3.25=3.49 with it; both are in the distress zone of their own scale. Comparing 3.49 with the plain zones (1.10 and 2.60) would wrongly put Lantern in the safe zone. Altman (2000) also gives the average score with the constant for each US bond rating in 1994: 8.15 for AAA, 5.85 for BBB, 3.75 for B−, 3.20 for CCC+ and 0 for D. On that scale Lantern’s 2026 score of 9.72 is above the AAA average and its 2027 score of 3.49 lies between the CCC+ and B− averages. Those averages describe large rated companies in the mid-1990s, so treat them as a rough translation, not a rating.

Choosing the model changes the zone

The same business can land in different zones under different versions. Lantern’s 2027 Z′, which keeps sales ÷ total assets, is 2.16, in its grey zone: sales of $577,860 on $327,883 of assets add 1.76 points. That is the industry effect Z″ removes. For a café business, Z″ is the right model, and its answer is distress.

Harbourline Roasters, the fictional listed roaster from F00, shows the other side. A roaster turns green beans into roasted coffee, so it is a manufacturer, and it has a share price, so the original Z applies. F00 gives Harbourline’s total equity ($300 million) but not its split; for this lesson assume (a fictional assumption) that $180 million of it is retained earnings. With its market capitalisation of $765 million against liabilities of $360 million, Harbourline’s Z is 3.04, just inside the safe zone (above 2.99). That call rests on the assumption: between none and all of the $300 million being retained earnings, Z ranges from 2.66 to 3.30, and with less than about $156 million of retained earnings it would be grey. On book equity its Z′ is 1.90, in the grey zone, and its Z″ is 2.98, safe. The market thinks Harbourline’s equity is worth two and a half times its book value, and the original model gives that confidence weight.

Limits of the scores

  • The samples. The original Z was fitted to 66 US manufacturers with between $1 million and $25 million of assets, half of which failed between 1946 and 1965; later versions to other samples. A two-year-old private café business is far from any of them, and accounting, financing and industries have changed since.
  • Leases on the balance sheet. Since ASC 842 (from 2019 for public companies; from fiscal years beginning after 15 December 2021 for private ones) and IFRS 16, lessees record right-of-use assets and lease liabilities. The current part of the lease liability cuts working capital, the asset enlarges every denominator and the liability lowers equity ÷ liabilities. The models were fitted before that change.
  • Part-year and first-year periods. Lantern’s 2026 EBIT covers ten months. Annualised, its 2026 Z″ would be about 7.01 rather than 6.47 (same zone). Young firms also have small retained earnings, which X2X_2X2​ penalises by design.
  • Book equity that isn’t a cushion. Some strong companies have negative book equity after years of buybacks and dividends. Their X2X_2X2​ and X4X_4X4​ go negative and the score says distress, which may say more about the balance sheet than about the risk.
  • Quality of the inputs. The score takes EBIT and retained earnings at face value. Profit inflated by aggressive accounting raises the score, as the previous lesson showed it can.
  • Not a forecast. The zones mark where Altman’s samples were classified with or without error; they are not probabilities. A score near a boundary is a reason to look closer, not a coin toss.

Leases show the second limit at its starkest. Remove the right-of-use asset and the lease liabilities from Lantern’s 2027 balance sheet, as under the rules before ASC 842 (the straight-line lease cost equals what the rent would have been, so EBIT and equity don’t change), and its Z″ rises from 0.24 to 2.27: grey, not distress. The cafés’ economics are the same; only the accounting moved.

Starbucks illustrates the fourth. At 29 September 2024 it reported a retained deficit of $7,343.8 million and total shareholders’ deficit of $7,441.6 million, after years of dividends and share repurchases ($2,585.0 million and $1,266.7 million in fiscal 2024 alone). Its Z″ from the FY2024 balance sheet is −0.27, deep in the distress zone, although in the same year its operations produced $6,095.6 million of cash and its operating income covered its interest expense 5,408.8÷562.0=9.625{,}408.8 \div 562.0 = 9.625,408.8÷562.0=9.62 times. A model built on book equity misreads a company whose deficit comes from returning cash to shareholders.

Other approaches

Altman’s scores are the best known of many credit-scoring models. Ohlson’s O-score (1980) uses logistic regression on similar ratios and gives a probability of failure rather than a zone. Market-based models infer default risk from the share price and its volatility. Credit rating agencies combine financial ratios with judgement about the industry, management and the terms of the debt. Banks build their own scorecards from their loan histories, and Altman’s later work extends the approach to small and medium-sized firms. All of them start from the same evidence: liquidity, profitability, leverage and the quality of the figures.

Common mistakes

  • “The original weights are 0.012, 0.014, 0.033, 0.006 and 0.999.”

    Those are the 1968 article’s weights for ratios written as percentages (15 for 15%). With ratios as decimals use 1.2, 1.4, 3.3, 0.6 and 1.0. Mixing them produces a false distress signal.

  • “Use the original Z for any company.”

    Z was fitted to public manufacturers with a share price. For a private firm use Z′ (book equity); for a non-manufacturer such as a café, a retailer or a service business use Z″, which drops sales ÷ total assets. Lantern’s Z′ (2.16, grey) and Z″ (0.24, distress) differ because of that one term.

  • “Lantern’s EM score of 3.49 is above 2.60, so it is safe.”

    The constant of 3.25 shifts the whole scale. Compare a score with the zones of its own version: 3.49 against 4.35 and 5.85, or 0.24 against 1.10 and 2.60. Both say distress.

  • “A score in the distress zone means the firm will go bankrupt.”

    The zones describe how firms in Altman’s samples were classified, not a probability for any one firm. The score is a screen that tells you where to look: for Lantern, at the timing of the law firm’s payment and the leases.

  • “Comparing a firm’s Z″ before and after 2019 shows how its risk changed.”

    Operating leases came onto US balance sheets under ASC 842 (from 2019 for public companies, later for private ones). A lease-heavy firm’s score can fall sharply with no change in its business: Lantern’s 2027 Z″ is 2.27 without its leases and 0.24 with them. Put both years on the same basis first.

In practice

The loan officer’s file. Coastline’s policy sends any borrower with Z″ below 1.10 to the credit committee instead of approving a renewal automatically. The loan officer’s note on Lantern does not argue with the score; it explains it. Of the drop from 6.47 to 0.24, the largest part comes from working capital, and the note traces it: cash spent on the cafés’ fit-out and machines, the line of credit, the Coastline note now due within a year and $37,010 of lease payments due in 2028. Recomputed without the leases the score is 2.27. The note recommends renewing the line at its current limit, not increasing it, on condition that Lantern reports monthly until the law firm’s payment arrives, and recomputing the score on the 2028 statements.

A supplier extending credit. Kilnworth Roasting, which supplies Lantern’s beans on 30-day terms, runs a simpler check on its trade customers: a Z″ from their latest accounts, the trend over two years and how promptly they pay. A customer whose score has fallen into the distress zone and who has started paying late is moved to shorter terms. Lantern still pays on time, so Kilnworth keeps its terms but asks for the 2028 half-year figures.

A portfolio screen. A fund that buys corporate bonds computes Z-scores for every issuer each quarter, using the version that fits each one, and reviews any issuer whose score has crossed into the grey or distress zone. The analysts know the model’s blind spots, so a low score triggers questions rather than a sale: is the cause leases, buybacks, a one-off loss, or a business that is genuinely weakening?

Summary

  • Altman’s Z-scores weight several ratios, found by multiple discriminant analysis, into one score that separates firms that failed from those that didn’t.
  • The ratios: working capital, retained earnings, EBIT and equity (market or book), each over total assets or total liabilities, and in Z and Z′ sales ÷ total assets.
  • Zones: Z below 1.81 distress, above 2.99 safe; Z′ 1.23 and 2.90; Z″ 1.10 and 2.60 (4.35 and 5.85 with its +3.25 constant).
  • Lantern’s Z″ fell from 6.47 (safe) in 2026 to 0.24 (distress) in 2027, driven by negative working capital, lower earning power and lease liabilities.
  • Choose the version for the firm: Z for public manufacturers, Z′ for private manufacturers, Z″ for non-manufacturers and private firms; the choice can change the zone.
  • Limits: samples of 1946–65 US manufacturers with $1–25 million of assets, operating leases now on balance sheets (Lantern 2.27 without them), part-year periods, negative book equity (Starbucks −0.27) and the quality of the inputs.
  • A score is a screen that tells you where to look, not a forecast or a probability of default.

Terms introduced

Altman Z-score
A distress score developed by Edward Altman that weights several financial ratios (working capital, retained earnings, EBIT and equity, each relative to assets or liabilities, and in some versions sales) into one number, compared with zones that separate firms resembling those that failed from those that didn’t.
Multiple discriminant analysis
A statistical method that finds the weights on several variables which best separate observations into predefined groups, such as firms that went bankrupt and firms that did not.
Grey zone
The range of a distress score between the distress and safe cut-offs, where the model misclassified firms in its samples and cannot call the outcome; Altman called it the zone of ignorance.
