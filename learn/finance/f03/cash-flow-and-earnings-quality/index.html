Cash flow and earnings quality

Test how much of a profit has arrived as cash: compare cash from operations with net income, measure accruals and free cash flow, find the estimates inside earnings, and recognise the warning signs of aggressive accounting.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate the ratio of cash from operations to net income, total accruals, the accruals ratio and free cash flow, stating the balances used.
  • Explain, line by line from the indirect method, why Lantern’s 2027 profit of $22,431 produced only $8,341 of operating cash.
  • Assess how much a profit depends on estimates, such as an unbilled contract asset measured with an inflation forecast.
  • Distinguish growth that consumes cash from accounting that inflates profit, using Amazon’s and Enron’s filings.
  • Apply a list of red flags to a set of statements and decide what further evidence an analyst should ask for.

Review first

  • The indirect method — Every measure here starts from the reconciliation of net income to cash from operations.
  • Revenue recognition — Lantern’s contract asset is revenue recognised over time under ASC 606, before it can be billed.
  • Efficiency and working capital — Working capital that grows with the business is the main reason profit and operating cash differ.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern’s 2027 income statement shows net income of $22,431. Over the same year its cash fell from $42,940 to $6,531, and by December it had drawn $24,000 on Coastline’s line of credit. Coastline’s credit analyst, reviewing the line, writes one question at the top of her notes: how much of this profit is cash?

Profit and cash differ because financial statements are prepared on the accrual basis: revenue is recorded when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, whenever the cash moves. That is the right way to measure performance, but it means net income contains amounts that have not yet become cash, and some that never will if an estimate turns out wrong. Earnings quality is the analyst’s word for how far reported profit can be trusted as a guide to the cash the business generates and will go on generating.

Three questions make the idea concrete. Has the profit arrived as cash, or is it sitting in receivables, contract assets and inventory? Will it recur, or does it include one-off gains? And how much of it rests on estimates, such as bad debts, useful lives or a forecast of inflation, that management chose? None of these makes a profit wrong. Each tells you how much weight it can bear.

Cash from operations against net income

The first test is the simplest: divide cash from operations (CFO) by net income. A ratio near or above 1 means the profit has come in as cash; a ratio well below 1 means much of it is still tied up somewhere. The gap between them, net income minus CFO, is called total accruals in this sense: the part of profit not yet matched by operating cash.

The two years point opposite ways. In 2027 only $0.37 of each dollar of profit arrived as cash, and the accruals swung from −$12,553 to +$14,090.

The working-capital lines pull the other way, by $33,890 in total, and one line dominates them: the increase of $30,840 in the contract asset. That is the law firm’s catering. The revenue is real under ASC 606, and it is in net income, but not a cent of it is in cash.

Key idea: Take out the unbilled revenue and the profit disappears

Lantern’s income before tax was $29,908. Without the $30,840 of unbilled law-firm revenue it would have been 29,908−30,840=−93229{,}908 - 30{,}840 = -93229,908−30,840=−932, a loss; after tax at 25% the loss is $699. Everything else Lantern did in 2027 (two new cafés, the shop, workshops and its other catering) roughly broke even. That doesn’t make the profit false. It means 2027’s profit is a claim on one client’s payment in March 2028, and its quality depends on that client and on the estimate of the fee.

Free cash flow

Free cash flow (FCF) takes the analysis one step further. Operating cash has to pay for the equipment, fit-outs and other long-lived assets that keep the business running and growing, called capital expenditure (capex). What is left is free for lenders and owners: to repay debt, pay dividends or build up cash. The usual definition, and Amazon’s published one, is CFO minus capital expenditure.

Lantern’s 2027 free cash flow was 8,341−54,000=−45,6598{,}341 - 54{,}000 = -45{,}6598,341−54,000=−45,659. The $54,000 was the cafés’ fit-out ($36,000) and machines ($18,000), all paid in cash. The shortfall was met by running down cash by $36,409 and by net financing of $9,250: $24,000 drawn on the line of credit, less $12,750 of loan repayments and a $2,000 dividend. Check: −45,659+9,250=−36,409-45{,}659 + 9{,}250 = -36{,}409−45,659+9,250=−36,409.

Negative free cash flow is not, by itself, a bad sign. A business that is investing to grow spends cash before the new assets earn it; the cafés were open for ten months of 2027 and their first full year is 2028. The question an analyst asks is whether the investment is a one-off step or a habit, and how it was paid for. Lantern’s was paid for with a cash buffer that is now gone and a line of credit that is 80% drawn, which is why the quality of 2027’s profit matters so much to Coastline.

In depth

The measures and their conventions

Four measures cover most of what an analyst needs. Each is defined exactly as in the course’s ratio engine, so the lessons, the exercises and the ratios widget agree.

  • CFO ÷ net income. How many dollars of operating cash each dollar of profit produced. Meaningless when net income is near zero or negative: then compare the dollar amounts.
  • Total accruals = net income − CFO. Positive when profit runs ahead of cash, negative when cash runs ahead.
  • Accruals ratio = (net income − CFO) ÷ average total assets. Scaling by assets lets you compare years and companies of different sizes. For 2027 the average is of the opening and closing balance sheets; for 2026 there is no opening balance sheet (Lantern started on 1 March 2026 with nothing), so 2026 uses closing total assets.
  • Free cash flow = CFO − capital expenditure, and the FCF margin = FCF ÷ revenue. Some analysts also subtract dividends, or only the capex needed to maintain the existing assets; OpenStax’s accounting text shows both versions. State the definition you use.

Lantern’s accruals ratio was −12,553÷93,676=−13.4%-12{,}553 \div 93{,}676 = -13.4\%−12,553÷93,676=−13.4% in 2026 (closing total assets) and 14,090÷210,779.5=6.7%14{,}090 \div 210{,}779.5 = 6.7\%14,090÷210,779.5=6.7% in 2027, where 210,779.5 is the average of $93,676 and $327,883. The 2027 figure is flattered by the size of the balance sheet: $173,379 of it is the cafés’ right-of-use asset, which ASC 842 brought onto the balance sheet in March. Large positive accruals, relative to assets, have long been studied as a sign that profits will be lower in later years; treat the ratio as a prompt to look for the reason, not as a verdict.

A fifth measure looks at investment: capex ÷ depreciation. A business that spends about as much as its depreciation is maintaining its assets; one that spends much more is growing them. Lantern’s 2027 ratio was 54,000÷19,800=2.7354{,}000 \div 19{,}800 = 2.7354,000÷19,800=2.73, a growth year. A ratio well below 1 year after year can mean assets are wearing out without replacement, which flatters free cash flow for a while.

Estimates inside earnings

Every profit contains estimates. The analyst’s job is to find the large ones and ask how much they could move. Lantern’s 2027 statements have four.

  • The contract asset. The law firm’s fee is $40,000 a year in March 2027 dollars, raised by the consumer price index. Lantern estimated 2.8% inflation, giving 40,000×1.028=41,12040{,}000 \times 1.028 = 41{,}12040,000×1.028=41,120 for the first contract year and 41,120×9/12=30,84041{,}120 \times 9/12 = 30{,}84041,120×9/12=30,840 for April–December. Each percentage point of inflation moves 2027 revenue by 40,000×0.01×9/12=30040{,}000 \times 0.01 \times 9/12 = 30040,000×0.01×9/12=300 dollars. The estimate is small; the timing and the single client are the real exposure.
  • The allowance for doubtful accounts. Set from an aging of catering receivables; a higher loss rate on the over-60-day band would cut profit at once.
  • Useful lives. The fit-out is depreciated over the five-year leases and the machines over five years. Longer lives would mean lower depreciation and higher profit, with no change in cash.
  • The lease measurement. The right-of-use asset and lease liability ($202,255 at the start) use Coastline’s 7.2% as the discount rate. The straight-line lease cost of $40,000 doesn’t depend on it, but the balance sheet does.

Notice what the list shows: estimates change profit, never cash. That is why comparing profit with cash is the most reliable single test of earnings quality. Cash from operations can be shifted between years too, by delaying payments to suppliers or selling receivables, but it is much harder to create from nothing.

Leases and comparing operating cash flows

Under US GAAP (ASC 842) the payments on an operating lease are operating cash outflows: Lantern’s $40,000 of café rent is inside its CFO. Under IFRS 16 there is no operating lease category for lessees, and the principal part of each lease payment is a financing outflow. On IFRS, Lantern’s operating cash flow would be higher by at least the principal repaid, 40,000−11,124=28,87640{,}000 - 11{,}124 = 28{,}87640,000−11,124=28,876 dollars, and by the $11,124 of interest as well if its policy put interest paid in financing. Free cash flow would rise by the same amount, though nothing about the cafés’ economics changed.

Standards: US GAAP and IFRS: lease payments in the cash-flow statement

US GAAP: operating lease payments are operating activities; for a finance lease, the principal is financing and the interest is classified like other interest paid (operating). IFRS 16: the principal portion of lease payments is financing; the interest portion follows the company’s IAS 7 policy for interest paid; short-term, low-value and variable lease payments are operating. Before comparing the CFO or free cash flow of a US and an IFRS company that lease heavily, put them on the same basis.

Reinvestment over reported profit: Amazon, 2012–2014

The opposite pattern to Lantern’s is a business whose reported profit looks poor while its operations produce a great deal of cash. Amazon’s filings for 2012–2014 are a well-known example.

In 2014 Amazon reported a net loss of $241 million and operating cash of $6,842 million. Two things explain most of the gap. Depreciation and amortisation of $4,746 million was charged against profit without using cash in the year; it reflected years of heavy investment in warehouses and computing. And Amazon’s working capital was negative: at the end of 2014 it owed suppliers $16,459 million, more than its inventories ($8,299 million) and receivables ($5,612 million) together. Customers paid before Amazon paid its suppliers, so growth released cash instead of absorbing it. Free cash flow stayed positive while profit was close to zero, because Amazon chose to reinvest rather than report earnings.

The lesson cuts both ways. Low profit with strong cash flow can be a sign of conservative or investment-heavy accounting, not weakness. But Amazon’s model depends on suppliers’ terms; a business with Lantern’s structure, where a large client pays a year after the first event, sits on the other side of that trade.

Aggressive accounting: Enron’s restatement

On 8 November 2001 Enron filed a Form 8-K saying it would restate its financial statements for 1997 to 2001. Entities it had kept off its balance sheet should have been consolidated: Chewco and JEDI from November 1997, and a subsidiary of LJM1, a related-party partnership, from 1999. The filing’s table shows what consolidation did: net income fell by $96 million (1997), $113 million (1998), $250 million (1999) and $132 million (2000), and debt rose by $711, $561, $685 and $628 million in those years. For 2000, reported net income of $979 million became $847 million.

The Board’s special investigative committee reported in February 2002 (the Powers Report). Its summary found that many of the most significant transactions were designed to produce favourable financial-statement results rather than real economic objectives or a transfer of risk, and that transactions with the related-party entities led Enron to report earnings from the third quarter of 2000 to the third quarter of 2001 that were almost $1 billion higher than should have been reported.

Many of the most significant transactions apparently were designed to accomplish favorable financial statement results, not to achieve bona fide economic objectives or to transfer risk.

Report of Investigation by the Special Investigative Committee of the Board of Directors of Enron Corp. (Powers Report), 1 February 2002, p. 4.

What could an analyst have seen beforehand? Not the restated figures. The 2000 10-K showed revenues up from $40,112 million to $100,789 million in a year, total assets nearly doubled from $33,381 million to $65,503 million and debt up from $8,152 million to $10,229 million. Rapid growth is not a red flag in itself; growth financed by debt, with earnings that depend on transactions with related parties, is the pattern the list below is built to catch.

Red flags

No single sign proves anything. Several together are a reason to read the notes closely and to ask management questions before relying on the profit.

  • Receivables or contract assets growing faster than sales. Revenue is being recognised before customers pay, or customers are paying more slowly.
  • Operating cash persistently below net income, year after year, without an investment or growth story that explains it.
  • Costs capitalised that peers expense, such as software development, customer acquisition or start-up costs. Capitalising moves the cash outflow into investing and the expense into later years.
  • One-off gains inside operating income: sales of property, insurance settlements, the release of provisions.
  • Related-party transactions and entities kept off the balance sheet, especially when the related party is an officer of the company.
  • Changes in estimates or policies, such as longer useful lives, lower bad-debt rates or a new revenue policy, that raise profit in the year they are made.
  • Dividends or buybacks paid while free cash flow is negative, financed by debt.
  • Non-GAAP “adjusted” earnings that drift further above GAAP net income each year.

Lantern trips two of them in 2027: a contract asset that grew from nothing to $30,840, and a $2,000 dividend paid in a year of negative free cash flow. Neither is improper. Both belong in Coastline’s file. Analysts who want a more formal screen sometimes use statistical models such as Beneish’s M-score, which combines several of these signals into one score; like Altman’s Z-scores in the next lesson, it flags firms for review rather than proving anything.

Common mistakes

  • “Lantern made a profit, so it generated cash.”

    Profit is measured on the accrual basis; cash is not. In 2027 Lantern earned $22,431 and its operations produced $8,341, because $30,840 of revenue was unbilled. Always read the cash-flow statement next to the income statement.

  • “Negative free cash flow means the business is failing.”

    Free cash flow is negative whenever investment exceeds operating cash, which is normal in a year of expansion. Ask what the capex bought, whether it will produce cash, and how it was financed. Lantern’s −$45,659 is the cafés; the concern is that it used up the cash buffer and most of the credit line.

  • “Depreciation is added back, so more depreciation means more cash.”

    Depreciation is a noncash expense; the add-back only undoes it. Changing useful lives changes profit and accruals, never operating cash (apart from tax). That is exactly why comparing profit with cash exposes estimates.

  • “A ratio of CFO to net income of 3.5 is excellent.”

    Not when net income is tiny or negative: the ratio explodes or changes sign. Amazon’s 2014 ratio would be negative (CFO $6,842 million against a $241 million loss). Compare the amounts in dollars and use the accruals ratio, which divides by assets instead.

  • “Lease-heavy companies under IFRS and US GAAP have comparable operating cash flows.”

    Under IFRS 16 the principal part of lease payments is financing; under ASC 842 operating lease payments are operating. An IFRS reporter with Lantern’s cafés would show at least $28,876 more operating cash and free cash flow. Adjust before you compare.

In practice

The credit file. Coastline’s analyst rewrites her note on Lantern after reading the reconciliation. The decline in operating cash is explained: $30,840 is due from a law firm on 31 March 2028, and the cafés’ fit-out and machines are one-off investments already paid for. What worries her is not the quality of the accounting but its timing. Until the law firm pays, Lantern has $6,531 of cash, $6,000 of undrawn credit and a $10,000 note due on 1 March 2028, a month before the money arrives. She asks Ana for the law firm’s payment record on its first invoices, a monthly cash forecast to April 2028, and confirmation that no dividend will be paid until the line is reduced.

The owner’s view. For Ana the same analysis changes a decision. The cafés’ 2027 profit looks thin after their lease cost, wages and depreciation, but their cash is better than their profit: the till takings arrive daily and depreciation is a large noncash charge. Her free cash flow problem was the one-off fit-out. Before she opens a third café she plans to wait for a full year of the first two, to see whether their operating cash covers the next fit-out without the credit line.

An equity analyst’s screen. An analyst covering listed retailers runs the red-flag list on each company every quarter: receivables and contract assets against revenue, CFO against net income over three years, capitalised costs, gains in operating income and the gap between adjusted and GAAP earnings. Companies that trip several flags get a closer reading of the notes and the MD&A, and questions on the next earnings call. A company like Amazon in 2014, with a reported loss and rising free cash flow, passes the cash tests easily; one whose earnings grow while its cash shrinks does not.

Summary

  • Earnings quality asks whether profit has arrived as cash, whether it will recur and how much it rests on estimates.
  • Compare cash from operations with net income: Lantern’s ratio fell from 1.46 (2026) to 0.37 (2027), and accruals swung from −$12,553 to $14,090.
  • The indirect method shows why: in 2027 the $30,840 law-firm contract asset, unbilled until 31 March 2028, absorbed almost all of the profit; without it Lantern made a loss of $699.
  • Free cash flow = CFO − capital expenditure. Lantern’s −$45,659 reflects the cafés’ fit-out and machines, paid for from cash reserves and the line of credit.
  • Estimates (useful lives, allowances, inflation forecasts) move profit but not cash; lease classification moves cash between activities (IFRS 16 versus ASC 842), so adjust before comparing.
  • Amazon (2012–2014) shows low profit with strong cash flow from depreciation and negative working capital; Enron’s 2001 restatement shows how off-balance-sheet, related-party structures inflated earnings.
  • Red flags: receivables growing faster than sales, CFO persistently below net income, capitalised costs, one-off gains, related parties, changed estimates, payouts funded by debt.

Terms introduced

Earnings quality
How far reported profit can be relied on as a guide to the cash a business generates and will go on generating: whether it has arrived as cash, whether it will recur and how much it depends on management’s estimates.
Free cash flow
Cash from operating activities minus capital expenditure: the operating cash left for lenders and owners after paying for long-lived assets. A non-GAAP measure whose exact definition varies between companies.
Capital expenditure
Cash spent on property, plant, equipment and similar long-lived assets (capex), reported in investing activities.
Accruals ratio
Net income minus cash from operating activities, divided by average total assets: the share of the asset base by which profit ran ahead of (positive) or behind (negative) operating cash.
Restatement
A revision of previously issued financial statements to correct an error, so the earlier figures can no longer be relied on.
