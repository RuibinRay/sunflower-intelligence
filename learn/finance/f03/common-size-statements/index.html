Common-size statements

Express every line of the income statement as a share of revenue and every line of the balance sheet as a share of total assets, read the margins that result, and compare a small business with one thousands of times its size without being misled by how each one classifies its costs.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Prepare a common-size income statement (as a percentage of net revenue) and a common-size balance sheet (as a percentage of total assets).
  • Calculate and interpret the gross, operating, EBITDA and net margins.
  • Explain how the classification of a cost (cost of sales or operating expense) and the accounting for leases move margins, and restate two businesses on the same basis.
  • Compare a small business with a much larger one using common-size figures, and state what common-size analysis cannot show.

Review first

  • Horizontal analysis — You compared Lantern’s two years line by line; this lesson compares the lines within each year.
  • The multi-step income statement — The multi-step income statement’s subtotals (gross profit, operating income) are the margins’ numerators.
  • The balance sheet and equity — Total assets, the base of the common-size balance sheet, and the classification of its lines come from there.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Coastline Bank’s credit analyst has Lantern’s comparative statements and the horizontal analysis. She knows revenue nearly tripled and operating income fell. Her next question is about structure: out of each dollar Lantern took in, how much went on coffee and food, how much on staff and rent, and how much was left? And does that look like a coffee business that can carry debt? Answering it means putting 2026 ($193,320 of revenue in ten months) and 2027 ($577,860) on the same scale, and later putting Lantern beside companies far larger than itself.

Vertical analysis expresses each line of a statement as a percentage of a base figure in the same statement and the same period. A statement with every line shown that way is a common-size statement. On the income statement the base is net revenue (net sales), so each line reads as cents per dollar of revenue. On the balance sheet the base is total assets, which equals total liabilities and equity, so each line reads as a share of everything the business controls, or of how it is financed.

Dividing by a total removes size. Lantern’s 2026 and 2027 income statements differ by a factor of three, and Starbucks’ revenue is tens of thousands of times Lantern’s, yet on a common-size statement all three add to 100%. Vertical and horizontal analysis are partners: horizontal analysis says how fast a line grew; vertical analysis says whether it grew faster than the business around it. In 2027, 38.8% of each dollar of Lantern’s revenue went on cost of sales, 55.5% on operating expenses, and 5.7% was left as operating income; net income was 3.9%.

Key idea: One margin up, the others down

Lantern’s gross margin rose from 54.0% to 61.2% while its operating margin fell from 19.5% to 5.7%. Wages expense, which now includes the café staff and Ana’s salary, rose from 9.5% to 27.9% of revenue, and the cafés’ lease cost added 6.9%. The cafés sell coffee with a low cost of goods but need staff and premises, and Lantern records those below gross profit.

In depth

Building a common-size statement

Divide every line by the base and keep the signs: expenses are shares of revenue, so cost of goods sold of 28.2% means 28.2 cents of each revenue dollar. Use net revenue, after returns and discounts, as the base, so that the subtotals read directly as margins. Use the same base for every line in a column, and show the change between years in percentage points. Because a common-size line is a ratio of two amounts from the same period, it needs no annualising: Lantern’s ten-month 2026 can be compared with 2027 directly, though seasonal months with an unusual mix of sales still colour a part-year.

The standard bases are conventions, and an analyst may choose a narrower one when it matches the question. A cost that belongs to one activity is better divided by that activity’s revenue: Lantern’s café staff as a share of café sales, not of all revenue, tells Ana whether the cafés are staffed sensibly. The same applies to a segment of a large company, or to a balance sheet line set against current assets rather than total assets. Say which base you used; a percentage without its base cannot be checked or compared.

The four margins

Four subtotals of the multi-step income statement, divided by net revenue, give the margins analysts quote most. The gross margin (gross profit ÷ net revenue) shows what is left after the direct cost of what was sold; it reflects pricing and the cost of goods. The operating margin (operating income ÷ net revenue) shows what is left after running the business; since Lantern has no other income, its operating income is its EBIT. The EBITDA margin adds depreciation and amortisation back, so it is not affected by how long ago assets were bought or how they are depreciated. The net margin (net income ÷ net revenue) is what the owners keep after interest and tax.

Read the margins as a cascade. Lantern kept 61.2% of revenue after the direct cost of sales in 2027, but operating expenses took 55.5%, leaving 5.7%; interest took 0.5% and tax 1.3%, leaving a net margin of 3.9%. A margin that falls between two subtotals locates the problem: in 2027 the damage was done between gross profit and operating income.

Classification moves margins

A margin depends on which lines sit above it. Lantern records the wages of its event baristas ($41,600 in 2027) in cost of services, because they are hired for each catering job; the café staff ($92,000) are in wages expense, an operating expense, because they work fixed shifts whatever the day’s takings. Both choices are defensible. Had Lantern put the café staff in cost of sales, its 2027 gross margin would have been 45.3% instead of 61.2%, below 2026’s 54.0%, and the story “gross margin improved” would have disappeared. The operating margin would not change at all: moving a cost between two lines above operating income changes the subtotal between them, not the operating income.

Companies choose their own expense captions, and two businesses in the same industry can put the same cost on different lines. Starbucks does not present cost of sales or gross profit at all. Its statement of earnings lists product and distribution costs (30.9% of total net revenues in fiscal 2024), store operating expenses (42.3%), other operating expenses, depreciation and amortization, and general and administrative expenses as separate operating expenses. Subtracting only product and distribution costs from revenue would give 69.1%, a figure Starbucks does not report and that is not comparable with a retailer’s gross margin that includes store costs. When two companies classify differently, compare the margins below the point where their classifications converge (operating or EBITDA margin), or rebuild both on one definition from the notes.

Standards: Leases and EBITDA: US GAAP and IFRS

Under US GAAP (ASC 842) an operating lease produces a single, straight-line lease cost in operating expenses, so Lantern’s $40,000 of café lease cost sits inside EBITDA. IFRS 16, effective since 2019, has no operating-lease category for lessees: the same leases would be charged as depreciation of the right-of-use asset and interest on the lease liability, and the IASB’s effects analysis notes that EBITDA under IFRS 16 excludes all lease expenses. Restated that way, Lantern’s 2027 EBITDA margin would be about 16.0% rather than 9.1%. Leases of 12 months or less and leases of low-value assets are exempt under IFRS 16: their payments are still expensed, typically on a straight-line basis, so they stay among operating costs under both frameworks. Compare EBITDA margins across the two frameworks only after adjusting for leases.

The common-size balance sheet

On the balance sheet the base is total assets. The common-size view shows how the business has deployed its resources and how it has financed them, independent of size. Lantern’s balance sheet changed character in 2027: cash fell from 45.8% of total assets to 2.0%, the cafés’ right-of-use assets became 52.9% of the total, and equity, which financed 45.2% of the assets at the end of 2026, financed only 19.2% a year later.

A falling share is not always a falling amount. Lantern’s property and equipment grew from $37,200 to $95,400, yet its share of total assets fell from 39.7% to 29.1%, because the right-of-use assets grew faster still. Read the common-size percentages beside the dollar amounts and the horizontal changes, never instead of them.

Comparing with a much larger business

Common-size statements make comparisons across scale possible. Harbourline Roasters, F00’s fictional listed roaster, has revenue of $640 million, about 1,108 times Lantern’s; Starbucks’ fiscal 2024 revenue of $36,176.2 million is about 62,604 times. On the margins table, Lantern’s 2026 margins beat both, before Ana was paid a salary, while its 2027 operating margin of 5.7% is below Harbourline’s 9.5% and well below Starbucks’ 15.0%.

The balance sheets differ more. Harbourline’s equity finances 45.5% of its assets and its debt 31.8%; Lantern’s equity finances 19.2%, with lease liabilities at 52.9% and other debt at 19.9%. Starbucks’ total liabilities are 123.7% of its total assets, so its shareholders’ equity is a deficit of 23.7% of assets; its operating lease liabilities are 32.7% and its right-of-use assets 29.6%. A negative equity line is a warning that some ratios built on equity will mean nothing, a point the solvency and returns lessons take up.

What common-size statements cannot show

  • Scale. A 3.9% net margin on $577,860 is $22,431: enough for one owner-manager, not for a business that must also repay $65,250 of debt. Dollars still matter, and so does size: a large chain buys coffee and leases premises on terms a two-café business cannot get.
  • Cash and timing. Margins are accrual measures. Lantern’s 2027 revenue includes the law firm’s contract revenue that will not be billed until March 2028; the cash-flow lessons ask how much of the margin arrived as cash.
  • Growth. Each percentage is one year’s structure. A share can fall while the amount rises, as Lantern’s property and equipment did; pair vertical with horizontal analysis.
  • Whether a percentage is good. A 27.9% wage share means little without a benchmark for comparable cafés and caterers, which the benchmarking lesson discusses.
  • Causes. A percentage that changes because its base changed (revenue fell, or a large asset was added) can look like a change in the line itself.

Common mistakes

  • “Juniper’s 68% gross margin beats Copperleaf’s 45%.”

    Gross margins depend on what each business puts in cost of sales. Restate both on one definition, or compare a margin below the point where the classifications meet, such as the operating margin. Starbucks presents no gross profit at all.

  • “Lantern’s 2026 margins must be annualised first.”

    A margin divides one flow by another for the same months, so the period length cancels out. Annualise flows for horizontal comparisons, not for margins. (Seasonality can still make a part-year’s mix unusual.)

  • “Property’s share fell, so Lantern has less equipment.”

    A common-size share can fall because the total grew faster. Lantern’s property and equipment rose from $37,200 to $95,400 while its share fell from 39.7% to 29.1%. Read the dollars and the horizontal change beside the percentage.

  • “A common-size balance sheet uses revenue as its base.”

    The balance sheet’s base is total assets (equal to total liabilities and equity), both measured at the same date. Revenue is a flow over a period and is the base for the income statement only.

  • “EBITDA margins are comparable everywhere.”

    EBITDA is not defined by US GAAP, and lease accounting differs: under ASC 842 operating lease cost stays inside EBITDA, while under IFRS 16 it is replaced by depreciation and interest, which EBITDA excludes. Adjust before comparing a US GAAP reporter with an IFRS one.

In practice

The lender’s structure check. Coastline’s analyst keeps a page of common-size statements for every small-business borrower in food and drink. Lantern’s 2027 page shows wages at 27.9% of revenue and occupancy (kitchen rent and café leases) at 9.4%, against 9.5% and 6.2% in 2026. Those two lines explain most of the fall in the operating margin. Her credit memo states the margins, names the lines that moved and asks Lantern for the cafés’ monthly wage and lease shares, because a café business whose wages stay near a quarter of revenue in its second year needs higher prices or fewer staff hours to service more debt.

The owner’s targets. Ana uses the same percentages to set targets. If café staff can be scheduled to cost no more than a set share of café sales, she can check it every month from the till reports and the payroll without waiting for the year-end statements. She also learns to explain her margins in the bank’s terms: the gross margin rose because the cafés’ coffee costs little to make, but their staff and premises sit below gross profit, which is why she shows the operating margin first.

The investor’s screen. An equity analyst comparing Starbucks with other restaurant companies would not compare gross margins, because Starbucks presents none. She would compare operating margins and EBITDA margins, adjusting for leases where a peer reports under IFRS, and use the common-size balance sheet to spot what is unusual: a shareholders’ deficit and lease liabilities of about a third of total assets.

Summary

  • Vertical analysis divides each line by a base in the same statement: net revenue for the income statement, total assets for the balance sheet. The result, a common-size statement, removes scale.
  • The gross, operating, EBITDA and net margins are the income statement’s subtotals as a share of revenue; where a margin falls between two subtotals shows where the problem is. Lantern’s gross margin rose from 54.0% to 61.2% while its operating margin fell from 19.5% to 5.7%.
  • Margins need no annualising: both parts cover the same months.
  • Classification moves margins: Lantern’s café staff in operating expenses, its event baristas in cost of services, and Starbucks’ separate product, store and administrative lines. Compare like definitions, or the operating margin.
  • Lease accounting moves EBITDA: under ASC 842 operating lease cost is inside EBITDA, under IFRS 16 it is not.
  • Common-size statements make a two-café business comparable with Harbourline or Starbucks, but they hide scale, cash, growth and causes: read them with the dollars and the horizontal changes.

Terms introduced

Vertical analysis
Expressing each line of a financial statement as a percentage of a base in the same statement and period: net revenue for the income statement, total assets for the balance sheet.
Common-size statement
A financial statement in which every line is shown as a percentage of a base figure (net revenue or total assets), so that businesses of different sizes, or one business in different years, can be compared.
Operating margin
Operating income divided by net revenue: the share of each dollar of revenue left after the costs of running the business, before interest and income tax.
EBITDA margin
EBITDA (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation) divided by net revenue. A non-GAAP measure, affected by how leases are accounted for.
Net margin
Net income divided by net revenue: the share of each dollar of revenue the owners keep after all expenses, interest and tax. Also called the net profit margin.
