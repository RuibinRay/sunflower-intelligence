Liquidity

Measure whether a business can pay what falls due in the next year: working capital and the current, quick, cash and operating cash flow ratios, what belongs in each and why, and how to explain a fall like Lantern’s from 2.15 to 0.52.

About 70 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate working capital and the current, quick, cash and operating cash flow ratios from a classified balance sheet and a statement of cash flows.
  • Classify current assets and liabilities by how close they are to cash, including a contract asset, prepayments, unearned revenue, current lease liabilities and a drawn line of credit.
  • Explain why cafés and restaurants can run current ratios below 1 without being in difficulty, using Starbucks’ balance sheet.
  • Analyse a change in working capital with the balance-sheet identity, and explain what drove Lantern’s fall from 2.15 to 0.52.
  • Identify window dressing and assess what a minimum current ratio covenant would require of a borrower.

Review first

  • Capital and liquidity — Working capital, the current ratio and the quick ratio were introduced there; this lesson builds on them.
  • The balance sheet and equity — The ratios read a classified balance sheet: current and noncurrent items, and the current portion of long-term debt.
  • Revenue recognition — The contract asset and unearned revenue (a contract liability) come from there.
  • Common-size statements — You will compare Lantern with Starbucks, a business hundreds of thousands of times larger.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

In January 2028 Coastline Bank’s credit officer reads Lantern’s 2027 statements, as the bank does every year for its borrowers. One line stops her. At 31 December 2026 Lantern had $2.15 of current assets for every dollar it owed within the year; at 31 December 2027 it had $0.52. Ana’s first reaction is that the business has never been busier, and she is right: revenue roughly tripled. Both facts are true at once, and this lesson shows how.

Liquidity, for a business, is its ability to pay its obligations as they fall due. Liquidity analysis asks a narrow question: will the cash that arrives over the coming months, together with the cash already held, cover the bills, wages, rent and loan payments due over the same months? It is a different question from profitability (does the business earn more than it spends over a year?) and from solvency (is it worth more than everything it owes, in the long run?). A profitable, solvent business can still fail for lack of cash on the wrong day.

F00 introduced the first two measures: working capital, current assets minus current liabilities, and the current ratio, current assets divided by current liabilities. Both rest on the balance sheet’s one-year line: a current asset is expected to turn into cash, or be used up, within a year (or the operating cycle, if longer); a current liability is due within a year. This lesson adds three stricter measures, shows what belongs in each, and uses them to explain Lantern’s year.

Key idea: Liquidity is about timing and composition

A current ratio compares two totals at one date. Two businesses with the same ratio can be in very different positions: one holds cash and owes suppliers on 30-day terms; the other holds slow-moving stock and owes a bank loan due next week. Always look at what the current assets are, when they will turn into cash, and when each liability falls due.

In depth

A ladder of measures

The liquidity ratios form a ladder. Each step leaves out the current assets that are furthest from cash, so each answers a harsher version of the same question.

  • Current ratio: all current assets against all current liabilities. It assumes every current asset will become cash in time to meet the liabilities.
  • Quick ratio (acid test): only cash, short-term investments and receivables, the assets that are already money or a customer’s promise to pay on a fixed date. Inventory (it must be sold first), prepayments (they will never come back as cash) and a contract asset (it is not yet billable) are left out.
  • Cash ratio: cash and short-term investments only. It asks what could be paid tomorrow if nothing more came in.
  • Operating cash flow ratio: cash from operations for the period against current liabilities at its end. It replaces a stock of assets with a flow: how much of what is owed would one period’s operations pay off?

None of these has a single right value. OpenStax’s Principles of Finance calls a 2 : 1 current ratio “actually quite high for most companies and most industries”, and notes that many firms operate successfully with a cash ratio below 1, since idle cash earns little. What matters is the comparison: with the same business in earlier years, with similar businesses, and with what the business must pay soon.

What goes in, and why

The balance sheet decides what is current; the analyst decides how close to cash each current item really is. Lantern’s 2027 balance sheet has examples of almost every kind.

The line of credit ($24,000 drawn) is shown as current. Standards: Leases in current liabilities

Under ASC 842, which public companies in the US have applied for periods beginning after 15 December 2018 and other entities for periods beginning after 15 December 2021, a lessee recognises a right-of-use asset and a lease liability for an operating lease. The liability is split between current and noncurrent like a loan, but the right-of-use asset is noncurrent. Every new lease therefore lowers working capital and the current ratio. Before ASC 842 the cafés’ rent would have appeared only as an expense; the payments would still have been due. IFRS 16 has required the same for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2019. Leases as debt are taken up in the next lesson, Solvency and leverage.

Why Lantern’s ratios fell

The quickest way to explain a change in working capital uses the accounting equation. Split each side into current and noncurrent parts: current assets + noncurrent assets = current liabilities + noncurrent liabilities + equity. Rearranged:

Working capital=Equity+Noncurrent liabilities−Noncurrent assets\text{Working capital} = \text{Equity} + \text{Noncurrent liabilities} - \text{Noncurrent assets}Working capital=Equity+Noncurrent liabilities−Noncurrent assets

Working capital is the part of the long-term funding (equity and long-term debt) that is not tied up in long-term assets. It rises when the business keeps profits or borrows long-term, and falls when it buys long-lived assets or when long-term debt moves into the coming year. Lantern’s working capital fell by $85,620 in 2027, and the identity splits the fall into four causes.

The biggest cause is the cafés. Lantern paid $36,000 for the fit-out and $18,000 for the machines in cash: long-lived assets bought with money that had been working capital. The statement of cash flows tells the same story from the other side. Operations brought in $8,341; buying the fit-out and machines took $54,000; repaying the van note and the brewer plan took $12,750 and the dividend $2,000. The $24,000 drawn on the line of credit filled part of the gap, and cash fell by $36,409. In effect Lantern paid for five-year assets with cash and with a loan due within a year, which is why its working capital went below zero.

The next two causes are not new spending but the passage of time. The leases put $37,010 of 2028 payments into current liabilities. The Coastline note, borrowed in 2026 for two years, is now due on 1 March 2028, and the brewer plan’s 2028 instalments are current. Look at Lantern’s current liabilities by kind and the point is plain: the trade items (payables, wages, interest, unearned revenue and tax) total $26,436, almost exactly the whole of 2026’s current liabilities ($26,289). All of the increase is debt ($51,091) and leases ($37,010).

The contract asset hides a further weakness. Without it the current ratio would be 0.25, not 0.52. It will turn into cash, $41,120 if the CPI estimate holds, on 31 March 2028, a month after the Coastline note’s $10,000 falls due. Liquidity is a calendar question as much as a ratio question, and the next cash-flow lessons return to what the contract asset means for the quality of 2027’s profit.

What the balance sheet does not show

  • Undrawn facilities. Lantern can still draw $6,000 on its $30,000 line of credit. Cash a business can borrow at short notice is liquidity that no ratio counts; companies describe their facilities in the notes and in management’s discussion.
  • Timing within the year. The ratios compare totals, not dates. Wages due on 5 January and a note due on 1 March count the same.
  • Seasonality. A balance sheet is a photograph of one day. Lantern’s year ends just after its December gift peak, when stock has been sold and cash collected; another date would give other ratios.
  • The owner. A small company’s owner can often lend to it or invest more. That is support, not liquidity of the business itself, and a lender will not count on it unless it is written down.

Why cafés and restaurants run low current ratios

A business that is paid at the till holds few receivables, and one that sells fresh food and drink holds little stock. Its suppliers, meanwhile, give it normal trade credit. Its current assets are therefore small compared with what it owes, and a current ratio below 1 can be perfectly healthy if cash keeps arriving every day. Starbucks is the clearest example.

Two of Starbucks’ largest current liabilities make the ratio look worse than the position is. The stored-value card liability is money customers have already paid onto their cards; it is settled with coffee, not cash. The current operating lease liability is rent on the stores that produce the cash. Against both stand $6,095.6 million of operating cash flow in a year, enough to pay two-thirds of all current liabilities. Lantern’s 2027 operating cash flow ratio, 0.07, is the real difference: its cafés now give it a café’s balance sheet without, yet, a café chain’s cash flow.

For comparison, Harbourline Roasters, the fictional roaster from F00, has a current ratio of 1.50 and a quick ratio of 0.83. A roaster holds green and roasted coffee and sells to shops on credit, so its current assets are larger. Comparing Lantern with a peer means choosing one with the same business model, and Lantern is now a mixture: part café, part online shop, part caterer.

Comparing a ten-month year with a twelve-month one

The current, quick and cash ratios compare two balances at one date, so the length of the period before that date doesn’t enter them: 2026’s ratios compare directly with 2027’s. The operating cash flow ratio does mix a flow with a balance. Lantern’s 2026 cash from operations, $39,940, covers ten months; annualised (× 12 ÷ 10) it would be $47,928 and the ratio 1.82 rather than 1.52. Annualising assumes the missing two months look like the average ones, and Lantern’s first ten months included its December peak, so treat the 1.82 as an upper figure.

Window dressing

Window dressing means arranging transactions around the year end so that the balance sheet looks better than the business’s normal position. The common moves: paying suppliers just before the year end, delaying purchases, chasing collections into December, or borrowing on 30 December and repaying on 2 January. Whether a move helps depends on whether the ratio is above or below 1. Paying a liability with cash takes the same amount off both totals: above 1 that raises the ratio, below 1 it lowers it.

  • At the end of 2026 (ratio 2.15), paying $6,000 of payables on 31 December would have raised the current ratio to 2.49 without making Lantern any more able to pay its bills.
  • At the end of 2027 (ratio 0.52), the same payment would lower it to 0.49. Drawing the last $6,000 of the line of credit and holding the cash would instead raise it to 0.54, by adding the same amount to both totals.

An analyst guards against window dressing by comparing the year end with quarterly figures where they exist, by reading the cash-flow statement for unusual December movements, and by checking whether short-term borrowing at the year end matches the interest paid during the year.

Covenants in outline

Lenders write liquidity tests into loan agreements. A covenants clause might require a minimum current ratio, tested on the borrower’s quarterly or annual statements; failing it is a default that lets the lender demand repayment, raise the rate or impose conditions, although lenders often agree a waiver instead. The agreement defines the ratio, and the definition matters: whether operating lease liabilities, the current portion of the lender’s own loan or the drawn line of credit count as current liabilities can decide whether a borrower passes. Common mistakes

  • “A current ratio below 1 means the business can’t pay its bills.”

    It means current liabilities exceed current assets on one date. Starbucks has run below 1 (0.75 at its 2024 year end) while generating $6.1 billion of operating cash a year, because its sales arrive as cash every day and part of its current liabilities is settled in coffee. Judge a low ratio with the cash flow that refills it.

  • “The higher the current ratio, the better.”

    Beyond what the business needs, idle cash and slow stock earn little, and a high ratio can be manufactured at the year end. Lantern’s 2.15 at the end of 2026 included cash it spent on the cafés’ fit-out two months later. Compare with similar businesses and with the business’s own needs.

  • “The contract asset is as good as a receivable.”

    A receivable is an unconditional right to be paid: only time stands between the business and the cash. A contract asset depends on something else, such as further performance or a billing date. Lantern’s $30,840 can’t be invoiced until 31 March 2028 and rests on a CPI estimate, so it stays out of the quick ratio.

  • “Unearned revenue will drain cash like a payable.”

    The cash has already arrived; the obligation is met by delivering the event, workshop or coffee. It costs cash only through the cost of delivering and any refunds. It still counts in current liabilities, so the standard ratios treat it like a payable; say so when it is large, as Starbucks’ stored-value card liability is.

  • “Leave the lease liabilities out: rent is an operating cost.”

    Since ASC 842, the part of an operating lease liability due within a year is a current liability, and the payments are as contractual as a loan’s. Leaving them out is a deliberate adjustment, not a correction; if you make it (some loan agreements do), say so and make it consistently for every business you compare.

  • Turning the ratio upside down

    Current liabilities divided by current assets gives 1.94 for Lantern in 2027 and invites the conclusion that liquidity improved. The current ratio is assets over liabilities: how many dollars of current assets stand behind each dollar due.

In practice

The credit officer’s review. Coastline’s credit officer starts from the ratios but writes her review around dates. Lantern holds $6,531 of cash and $6,000 of undrawn credit line. Against that, in the first quarter of 2028 it must pay its year-end payables, wages and taxes ($16,557 of trade items other than unearned revenue and interest), three months of café rent ($12,000), the brewer plan’s instalments and, on 1 March, the $10,000 Coastline note. Its main inflows are daily café, shop and workshop takings and, on 31 March, the law firm’s fee. Her question is whether the takings cover the gap until 31 March; the ratios told her to ask it.

Ana’s options. Ana can improve liquidity in four ways, and each has a cost. She can bring in long-term money (Hollis Avery’s equity or a term loan) so that long-lived assets are no longer financed from working capital. She can convert the line of credit into a term loan, which moves it out of current liabilities but adds a repayment schedule. She can ask the law firm to pay quarterly rather than annually in arrears, which would shrink the contract asset and bring cash forward. Or she can slow spending: the 2028 Cold Brew launch, more market research and dividends can all wait. The efficiency lesson measures how much cash each of these releases.

A supplier’s view. Kilnworth Roasting sells Lantern its beans on 30-day terms. Its credit controller sees the same balance sheet and asks a simpler question: will Lantern keep paying on time? The quick ratio of 0.15 would worry her if she looked at it alone; Lantern’s record of paying within terms and the steady cash from the cafés will matter more. Liquidity ratios start conversations; the payment history and the cash calendar finish them.

Summary

  • Liquidity is the ability to pay obligations as they fall due; it is different from profitability and from solvency.
  • The ladder of measures: current ratio (all current assets), quick ratio (cash, short-term investments and net receivables), cash ratio (cash and short-term investments) and the operating cash flow ratio (a period’s cash from operations against year-end current liabilities).
  • Classify with care: a contract asset, inventory and prepayments are current but not quick; unearned revenue is settled by delivering; current lease liabilities and current portions of debt are contractual cash payments.
  • Lantern’s current ratio fell from 2.15 to 0.52 because it paid for the cafés’ fit-out and machines in cash, took on leases whose 2028 payments are current, saw the Coastline note and the brewer’s instalments fall due within the year, and borrowed $24,000 short-term; its trade liabilities hardly changed.
  • Working capital equals equity plus noncurrent liabilities minus noncurrent assets, which explains any change by cause.
  • A ratio below 1 is normal for businesses paid at the till with steady cash flow, such as Starbucks (0.75 in FY2024, with an operating cash flow ratio of 0.67); Lantern’s 0.07 operating cash flow ratio is the real concern.
  • Window dressing can move a ratio either way: paying liabilities with cash raises a ratio above 1 and lowers one below 1.
  • Covenants turn liquidity into a legal test; the definition of the ratio is negotiable and decisive.

Terms introduced

Cash ratio
Cash and short-term investments divided by current liabilities: what could be paid at once if nothing more came in.
Operating cash flow ratio
Net cash from operating activities for a period divided by current liabilities at its end: how much of what is owed within the year one period’s operations would pay.
Window dressing
Arranging transactions around a reporting date, such as paying suppliers early or borrowing for a few days, so that the statements look better than the business’s normal position.
Line of credit
An agreement under which a lender stands ready to lend up to a set limit, which the borrower draws on and repays as it needs; interest is charged only on the amount drawn.
