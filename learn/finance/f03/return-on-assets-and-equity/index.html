Return on assets and equity

Measure how much profit a business earns on the resources it uses and on the owners’ capital, on averages stated openly, and judge when a high return on equity reflects good business and when it reflects borrowing, an unpaid owner or tiny equity.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Calculate return on assets and return on equity on average balances, and state which basis you used.
  • Explain why a first period, a part year or an unpaid owner-manager distorts returns, and adjust for each.
  • Explain how leverage links the two ratios, and calculate the effect of debt on return on equity in good and bad years.
  • Decide when return on equity is not meaningful (tiny or negative equity) and choose a better measure.
  • Calculate return on invested capital in outline and explain how it differs from return on equity.

Review first

  • The balance sheet and equity — Returns divide profit by assets and equity, so you need to know what is in each and how equity moves from year to year.
  • Common-size statements — Return on assets is driven by the margins you computed there.
  • Solvency and leverage — The link between return on assets and return on equity is the leverage measured there.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern’s 2027 statements are finished. Revenue tripled to $577,860, yet net income fell from $27,387 to $22,431. A friend of Ana’s who works in a bank looks at the figures and tells her not to worry: “your return on equity is over 40%; most listed companies would kill for that.” Coastline Bank’s analyst, reading the same statements, writes in her file that Lantern’s returns “rest on a thin base of equity and a great deal of borrowing”. Both are reading the same two numbers. This lesson is about what those numbers mean.

A profit figure on its own can’t tell you whether a business did well. $22,431 is a fine result for a business that needs $50,000 of resources and a poor one for a business that needs $5 million. Return ratios divide profit by what was used to earn it. The two you meet most often divide Net income by total assets and by Equity: the first asks how productive all the business’s resources were, the second how much the owners earned on their capital.

Return on assets (ROA) is net income divided by average total assets. Every asset is in the denominator, whoever paid for it: the cafés’ machines bought with cash, the brewer bought on the supplier’s plan, the right-of-use assets for the leased cafés. ROA answers the manager’s question: for each dollar of resources the business controlled during the year, how many cents of profit did it produce?

Return on equity (ROE) is net income divided by average equity, the owners’ book capital (Book value of equity). It answers the owner’s question: for each dollar Ana has in the business, at book value, how many cents did the business earn for her? Net income is already after interest, so it is what belongs to the owners; equity is what they have put in or left in. When a company has preferred stock, the numerator is net income less preferred dividends and the denominator is common equity only, so that both belong to the common shareholders.

Why averages

Net income is a flow, earned over the whole year. Assets and equity are stocks, measured at a date. Dividing a year’s flow by one date’s stock pairs a year’s profit with resources the business had only at the end (or only at the start). When the balance sheet changes a lot during the year, that matters. Lantern’s total assets rose from $93,676 to $327,883 in 2027, mostly on 1 March, when the cafés’ leases put $202,255 of right-of-use assets on the balance sheet. The course therefore divides by the average of the opening and closing balances, the convention of the course’s ratio engine and of OpenStax’s Principles of Finance.

The gap between them widened because each dollar of Ana’s equity carried more assets in 2027 than in 2026: the rest was paid for by lenders, the landlords of the cafés and suppliers. That is Leverage, and it is the reason the bank analyst and Ana’s friend read the same 42.6% so differently.

A high ROE can come from productive assets or from financing each dollar of equity with several dollars of other people’s money. In depth

The denominator must be stated

Analysts use three denominators: the opening balance, the closing balance and the average. Each has a defence. The opening balance is what the owners had committed when the year began, and investment made during the year rarely earns a full year’s profit at once; Damodaran (2007) uses it for that reason. The closing balance is the only one available for a business’s first period. The average treats the year’s resources as building up evenly. None is wrong, but they can give very different answers, and a ratio quoted without its basis can’t be compared with anything.

The spread is widest for ROA, because Lantern’s assets more than tripled. On opening balances the cafés’ assets aren’t in the denominator at all, although they produced much of the year’s revenue; on closing balances they count for twelve months although they were used for ten. The average sits between. For a business whose balance sheet barely changes, the three bases agree closely, which is why the choice is often ignored; for a growing or shrinking business it is the first thing to check.

First periods: Lantern’s 2026

Lantern began on 1 March 2026 with nothing; Ana’s $20,000 bought its stock that day. There is no opening balance sheet, so the course uses closing balances for 2026: ROA 27,387÷93,676=29.2%27{,}387 \div 93{,}676 = 29.2\%27,387÷93,676=29.2% and ROE 27,387÷42,387=64.6%27{,}387 \div 42{,}387 = 64.6\%27,387÷42,387=64.6%. Two other reasonable choices give quite different answers. Averaging the $20,000 Ana put in on 1 March with the closing $42,387 gives an ROE of 87.8%. Annualising ten months of net income (27,387×12÷10=32,86427{,}387 \times 12 \div 10 = 32{,}86427,387×12÷10=32,864) gives 77.5% on closing equity; but the ten months included the fourth-quarter gift peak and no January or February, so annualising probably overstates a normal year.

A first period also has a deeper problem. The closing equity of $42,387 already includes most of the year’s profit, retained in the business, so the denominator grows with the numerator; a business that earned nothing would end its first year with only its contributed capital. Whatever you choose, say what you did and compare like with like: Lantern’s 2027 ROE on closing equity is 35.7%, the figure to set beside 2026’s 64.6%. Even that pair is not fully like-for-like: 64.6% is earned in ten months (77.5% annualised) and 35.7% in twelve.

An unpaid owner

In 2026 Ana drew no salary: she was paid only through $5,000 of dividends, which are not an expense. From 2027 she is paid $48,000 a year, an operating expense that reduces net income. The two years’ profits therefore measure different things. 2026’s profit is what was left for the owner before paying her for her work; 2027’s is after paying her a market wage.

To compare them, charge 2026 with what Ana’s work would have cost: ten months of the $48,000 salary, $40,000. Income before tax becomes 36,516−40,000=−3,48436{,}516 - 40{,}000 = -3{,}48436,516−40,000=−3,484. At Lantern’s 25% tax rate the salary would have cut income tax by $10,000, from $9,129 to a credit of $871 (in practice, a loss to carry forward), so net income becomes −3,484×0.75=−2,613-3{,}484 \times 0.75 = -2{,}613−3,484×0.75=−2,613. Held against the reported closing balances, ROE is −6.2% and ROA −2.8%. Most of 2026’s spectacular return was Ana’s unpaid labour. Buyers and lenders looking at owner-managed businesses commonly make this adjustment, often called normalising the owner’s pay; for a company whose managers are paid market salaries it isn’t needed.

Caution: Adjust the profit, not just the ratio

Charging a salary changes income before tax, then income tax, then net income. Subtracting the whole $40,000 from net income ignores the tax it saves; subtracting nothing compares a business with a free manager with one that pays its manager. Keep the balance sheet as reported unless you also know how the cash and retained earnings would have differed.

For Lantern it rose from 2.21 at the end of 2026 to 4.01 on 2027’s averages. Equity grew by $20,431 in 2027 (net income $22,431 less a $2,000 dividend); total liabilities grew by $213,776, from $51,289 to $265,065: the lease liabilities of $173,379, the line of credit, the brewer plan and the wages and customer deposits owed by a larger business. Leverage adds 18.1 points to Lantern’s ROE, and it does so because the business earns far more on its capital than its debt costs. (Treating the lease liabilities as debt would change both ROIC and the spread; F05 takes that up.)

Higher ROE from leverage is not free. Interest and lease payments are fixed, so the owners’ result swings more widely and the business can run out of cash in a bad year. That is why the bank analyst’s note mentions Lantern’s current ratio of 0.52 at the end of 2027 (the liquidity lesson) and why the distress-scores lesson returns to these balance sheets. ROE measures the reward; the leverage and liquidity ratios measure the risk taken to get it.

When equity is tiny or negative

ROE divides by equity, so small equity gives huge answers and negative equity gives nonsense. A business with $5,000 of equity and $50,000 of profit has an ROE of 1,000%, which says more about the denominator than about the business. If equity is negative, a profitable company shows a negative ROE and a loss-making one a positive ROE, two negatives making a positive. In either case report ROE as “not meaningful” and use ROA or ROIC instead; Damodaran (2007) gives the same advice.

Starbucks is a real example. At 29 September 2024 its balance sheet shows total assets of $31,339.3 million and total liabilities of $38,780.9 million, so shareholders’ equity is a deficit: −$7,448.9 million attributable to Starbucks. The deficit was built up by returning cash to shareholders: in fiscal 2022, for example, Starbucks repurchased $4,013.0 million of its stock and paid $2,263.3 million of dividends, together far more than its net earnings of $3,283.4 million (including noncontrolling interests, as its cash-flow statement starts). In fiscal 2024 dividends ($2,585.0 million) and repurchases ($1,266.7 million) again exceeded net earnings ($3,762.3 million), yet the deficit narrowed slightly from −$7,994.8 million a year earlier, because other items added to equity. Net earnings attributable to Starbucks were $3,760.9 million, so a mechanical ROE on average equity is 3,760.9÷(−7,721.85)=−48.7%3{,}760.9 \div (-7{,}721.85) = -48.7\%3,760.9÷(−7,721.85)=−48.7% for a company that made a large profit. Its ROA, 3,760.9÷30,392.4=12.4%3{,}760.9 \div 30{,}392.4 = 12.4\%3,760.9÷30,392.4=12.4%, still means something.

Note: Negative equity is not insolvency

Starbucks’ deficit comes from returning cash to shareholders, not from losses: its net earnings were positive in each of fiscal 2022, 2023 and 2024. Book equity is an accounting residual: it records what owners contributed and left in, measured by accounting rules, not what the business is worth. The solvency lesson explains why debt to equity is meaningless here for the same reason.

Return on invested capital, in outline

ROE mixes how well the business operates with how it is financed. ROIC separates them by putting operating profit after tax, often called NOPAT, over the capital that lenders and owners provide, whatever its mix. For Lantern in 2027, before any adjustment for leases: NOPAT 32,787×0.75=24,59032{,}787 \times 0.75 = 24{,}59032,787×0.75=24,590; invested capital (interest-bearing debt plus equity) averages (72,387+128,068)÷2=100,227.5(72{,}387 + 128{,}068) \div 2 = 100{,}227.5(72,387+128,068)÷2=100,227.5; ROIC is 24.5%. Analysts differ on the details: whether to subtract cash, whether to treat lease liabilities as debt (which also means moving the interest inside the lease cost out of operating profit), which tax rate to use. F05 develops ROIC and compares it with the Cost of capital; here it is enough to know that a business creates value for its owners when ROIC exceeds what its capital costs.

Variants you will meet

  • ROA on operating profit. Net income is after interest, so ROA computed on it falls when a business borrows, even if its assets are as productive as before. Some analysts use EBIT after tax over average total assets instead: Lantern’s 2027 figure is 24,590÷210,779.5=11.7%24{,}590 \div 210{,}779.5 = 11.7\%24,590÷210,779.5=11.7% against 10.6% on net income. The course uses net income, as the ratio engine does; say which you use.
  • Return on average common equity. With preferred stock, net income less preferred dividends over common equity, as above.
  • Annualised quarterly returns. A quarter’s net income times four over average equity; seasonal businesses distort it, as Lantern’s ten months show.
  • Market-based returns. ROE uses book equity. A shareholder who paid more than book value earns less on the price paid: F00’s market-to-book ratio links the two.

Common mistakes

  • “A higher ROE means a better-run business.”

    ROE rises with leverage as well as with performance, and it explodes when equity is small. Lantern’s ROA fell from 29.2% to 10.6% while its ROE fell only to 42.6%, because assets per dollar of equity nearly doubled. Read ROE beside ROA (or ROIC) and the leverage that links them.

  • “Dividing by closing balances is fine.”

    For a business whose balance sheet changed a lot, closing balances count new assets for the whole year and understate the return. Lantern’s 2027 ROA is 6.8% on closing assets and 10.6% on average assets. Use averages where an opening balance sheet exists, and always state the basis.

  • “Lantern’s 2026 ROE of 64.6% shows how profitable the first year was.”

    It is a ten-month profit on closing equity that already contains most of that profit, earned with an owner who drew no salary. Charge ten months of her $48,000 salary and 2026 makes a loss. Normalise the owner’s pay before comparing owner-managed businesses with each other or with companies that pay managers.

  • “Starbucks’ ROE is −48.7%, so it lost money.”

    Starbucks earned net income of $3,760.9 million in fiscal 2024; the minus sign comes from its negative equity. When average equity is negative or tiny, report ROE as not meaningful and use ROA or ROIC.

  • “Subtract the owner’s salary from net income.”

    A salary is deductible, so it reduces tax as well. Charge it before tax and recompute the tax: for Lantern, 2026 net income falls by $30,000 (40,000×0.7540{,}000 \times 0.7540,000×0.75), not by $40,000.

In practice

The lender’s view. Coastline Bank’s analyst cares less about Lantern’s ROE than about whether the business earns enough on its assets to service what it owes. She records ROA on average assets, ROIC before and after treating the leases as debt, and the leverage that turns them into ROE, and she notes that Lantern’s 2027 return is earned on a balance sheet where liabilities are 81% of total assets (265,065÷327,883265{,}065 \div 327{,}883265,065÷327,883). Her conclusion is that the returns are adequate but the margin of safety is thin: a fall in café sales would hit owners first and lenders soon after, because the lease and loan payments don’t shrink with sales.

The owner’s view. Ana uses ROE to ask whether the money she has left in Lantern earns more than it would elsewhere. In 2027, after paying Ana her own salary, Lantern earned 42.6% on her average book equity: far above the few percent a savings account pays, but for far more risk. When Lantern sells new shares to an outside investor in 2028 (F00’s Hollis Avery), the investor’s return will depend on the price she pays, not on book equity: an investor who pays twice book value for a share of the profits earns half the ROE on the money put in.

The manager’s view. Inside the business, ROA (or a return on the assets each unit uses) compares the cafés, the catering kitchen and the online shop. Each café carries its fit-out, its machines and its right-of-use asset; the online shop carries little more than inventory. A café that earns the same profit as the shop on five times the assets is using capital less well, and a decision to open a third café should be judged on the return the new assets will earn, not on the extra revenue.

Reading any company’s returns. Check the basis (average, opening or closing), the numerator (net income, net income to common, or operating profit after tax), whether the period is a full year, and whether the equity is large enough for ROE to mean anything. Then ask what drives the return: margin, the intensity with which assets are used, or leverage. That last question is the subject of the next lesson.

Summary

  • Return on assets is net income over average total assets; return on equity is net income (less preferred dividends) over average common equity. Lantern: ROA 29.2% (2026, closing) and 10.6% (2027, average); ROE 64.6% and 42.6%.
  • Profit is a flow and assets and equity are stocks, so divide by the average of opening and closing balances. Opening, average and closing bases can differ widely for a growing business: always state the basis.
  • First periods have no opening balance sheet and part years distort flows; compare like with like (Lantern 2027 on closing equity: 35.7%).
  • Owner-managers who take no salary inflate profit. Charge a market salary before tax: Lantern’s 2026 becomes a small loss.
  • ROE = ROA × assets per dollar of equity. Leverage raises ROE above ROIC only while ROIC exceeds the after-tax cost of debt, and it widens the swings both ways.
  • When equity is tiny or negative (Starbucks at 29 September 2024), ROE is not meaningful: use ROA or ROIC.
  • ROIC = EBIT × (1 − t) over average debt plus equity measures operations independently of the financing mix; F05 develops it.

Terms introduced

Return on assets
Net income for a period divided by average total assets (closing total assets when there is no opening balance sheet): how much profit each dollar of resources produced. Abbreviated ROA.
Return on equity
Net income less any preferred dividends for a period divided by average common shareholders’ equity: how much the business earned on each dollar of the owners’ book capital. Abbreviated ROE; not meaningful when equity is negative or tiny.
Return on invested capital
Operating profit after tax (EBIT × (1 − tax rate)) divided by the capital provided by lenders and owners (interest-bearing debt plus equity, usually averaged): a return on the business’s operations that does not depend on how they are financed. Abbreviated ROIC; definitions vary in their treatment of cash and leases.
