A credit review of Brightwater Bike Repair

Fictional case

December 2027. Mateo Ruiz has asked Harbour Credit Union for a second loan: $25,000 over five years to stock e-bikes and fit out an e-bike service bay. You are the credit union’s analyst. You have Brightwater’s first six months (July–December 2026) and its first full year (2027), the statements you prepared in F02. In 2027 Brightwater moved to a bigger workshop, rented month to month, opened a small shop and started selling bikes and accessories. Mateo works in the business and pays himself only through dividends. Everything about Brightwater and the credit union is fictional.

Work through the stages: what changed, how liquid and efficient Brightwater is, how much debt it can carry, what it earns on its capital, how good its earnings are, and then your recommendation. Use the July–December 2026 figures with care: six months, and a business that has since changed.

What you will produce

  • Growth and common-size margins for the two periods, adjusted for the six-month first period, with the main cause of the change in margin.
  • Liquidity and efficiency ratios against the credit union’s benchmark, with the receivables and inventory days explained.
  • Solvency now and pro forma with the new loan, tested against each covenant, and the headroom on the tightest one.
  • Return on equity broken down by the DuPont method, compared with the benchmark, with what drives the difference.
  • Cash-flow quality (cash from operations against net income, free cash flow, dividends) and Altman’s Z″.
  • A recommendation memo to the credit committee: lend or not, on what terms and conditions, and what to monitor.
