Benchmarking and annual reports

Judge a company’s ratios against the right peers, read an annual report for what the ratios can’t show (the MD&A, the auditor’s report and the notes on revenue, leases and debt), and write an analysis that ends in a conclusion someone can act on.

About 65 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Choose a peer group and justify it by business model, size, ownership and accounting basis.
  • Calculate a peer median and quartiles and place a company within them, stating the method used.
  • Distinguish the differences between two companies that come from scale, from business model and from accounting.
  • Locate in a Form 10-K the items an analyst needs (business, risk factors, MD&A, the financial statements, the notes and the auditor’s report) and explain what each adds.
  • Read a company’s notes on revenue, leases and debt and use them to correct or extend a ratio analysis.
  • Write an analysis memo that states a conclusion, supports it with evidence and names the risks and conditions.

Review first

  • Common-size statements — Comparing a small business with a much larger one starts from common-size statements and margins.
  • Distress scores — The peer comparison includes each business’s Z″.
  • US GAAP and IFRS — Peers that report under different frameworks need adjusting before they are compared.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern’s 2027 current ratio is 0.52 and its operating margin 5.7%. Is that bad? Coastline’s loan officer can’t say from the numbers alone. A café business with daily card takings can run with fewer current assets than a manufacturer, and a business in its expansion year earns less on its assets than it will later. She needs two things: a standard to compare against, and the information behind the numbers that only the annual report and its notes contain.

A ratio on its own is a fact without a meaning. It acquires one when it is compared: with the same company’s earlier years (the trend, which earlier lessons covered), with a target such as a loan covenant, or with similar businesses. A benchmark is the standard used for that comparison, and the businesses chosen to supply it are the peer group.

Choosing peers

Good peers do the same thing in the same way. Four tests help.

  • Business model. Same products, customers and way of earning revenue. Café chains that own their stores differ from those that license their brand to other operators; a roaster that sells beans wholesale differs from one that runs cafés.
  • Size and stage. A two-year-old business with three revenue lines is not comparable with a multinational on most measures, and a business in an expansion year looks different from one at steady state.
  • Ownership and financing. Private firms often pay their owners partly through dividends rather than salaries (Lantern did in 2026), and listed firms can use market prices that private ones lack.
  • Accounting basis. US GAAP and IFRS treat leases, interest paid and some other items differently; fiscal years end on different dates.

No peer passes all four perfectly, so analysts use several layers: a close peer set for the main comparison, and larger or listed companies for context. Published industry statistics are a third source, but use them only when you know where they come from and how they were calculated. For this lesson Coastline’s loan officer uses the bank’s own file of nine independent coffee businesses with cafés, which, like Lantern, is fictional.

Medians and quartiles

Peer figures are summarised by their median and quartiles rather than their average. The median is the middle value when the figures are sorted: half the peers are at or below it. The first quartile (Q1) has a quarter of the peers below it and the third (Q3) three-quarters. A few extreme peers, such as one with a large cash pile, pull an average a long way but barely move a median. Spreadsheets compute quartiles by more than one rule; this lesson uses Excel’s =QUARTILE.INC(range,1), which places Q1 at position (n−1)×0.25(n - 1) \times 0.25(n−1)×0.25 in the sorted list, counting from zero. With nine peers that is exactly the third value.

The table changes the reading of two of the three numbers. A current ratio of 0.52 is low for a café business, but the peer file shows that café businesses commonly run below 1: the median is 1.05 and Q1 is 0.85. Lantern is still the lowest of all ten, so its liquidity is weak even by its industry’s standards. Its operating margin of 5.7% sits between Q1 and the median: ordinary for a peer, not alarming. And its Z″ of 0.24 is below all but one peer, while the peer median of 2.4 is itself in Altman’s grey zone: lease-heavy café businesses score low on the model, which is worth knowing before reading too much into Lantern’s zone.

A much larger company: Starbucks

Students often compare a small business with the best-known company in its industry. It can be instructive, as long as you separate three kinds of difference: scale, business model and accounting.

Scale. Starbucks’ revenue is about 62,600 times Lantern’s. Ratios remove size from the arithmetic, but not from the economics: Starbucks buys coffee, rents property and borrows on terms Lantern cannot get. Business model. Of Starbucks’ 40,199 stores at its year end, 19,181 were licensed: other companies run them, bearing the operating costs and investment, and Starbucks earns revenue from licensed stores (mostly product and equipment sales to licensees, plus royalties and licence fees; 12.5% of revenue) with little capital. Its customers also prepay on stored-value cards, a liability ($1,781.2 million, with current deferred revenue) that funds working capital. Accounting and financing. Starbucks has returned so much cash to shareholders that its equity is negative, so return on equity and debt-to-equity have no meaning for it. Of the five rows, only the operating margin and the lease share compare like with like, and even the margin reflects licensing.

In depth

Reading an annual report

A US public company’s annual report to the SEC is the Form 10-K. Its structure is fixed, so you can find the same information in every one. The SEC’s investor guide to the form describes each item; the ones an analyst reads first are these.

Item 1, Business
What the company does: products, segments, customers, markets and, for a retailer, how many stores it runs and how (Starbucks’ company-operated and licensed stores, for example).
Item 1A, Risk Factors
The most significant risks, in management’s words. Read it for what the numbers can’t show: dependence on a supplier, a customer or a lender; pending regulation.
Item 7, Management’s Discussion and Analysis (MD&A)
Management explains the year’s results, liquidity and capital resources, known trends and uncertainties, and its critical accounting estimates. It is where a company explains a fall in margin or a rise in debt.
Item 7A, Market Risk
Exposure to interest rates, exchange rates and commodity prices (coffee, for a roaster).
Item 8, Financial Statements
The audited statements, the notes and the auditor’s report.
Item 9A, Controls and Procedures
Management’s assessment of internal control over financial reporting; a material weakness here is a warning.

Item 6 once held five years of selected financial data; the SEC removed the requirement in 2020, and filings now show it as “[Reserved]”, as Starbucks’ fiscal 2024 10-K does. For trends you now read the statements of several years.

The auditor’s report

The auditor’s report sits in Item 8, before the statements. Most reports give an unqualified (clean) opinion: the statements present fairly, in all material respects, the company’s position and results under US GAAP. The SEC’s guide advises investors to look carefully at any other opinion. Three things deserve a reader’s attention: a qualified or adverse opinion or a disclaimer; a paragraph on substantial doubt about the company’s ability to continue as a going concern (the going concern assumption from F01); and, for most public companies, critical audit matters, the areas the auditor found most challenging, which often point to the biggest estimates. Lantern is private and unaudited; a lender of Coastline’s size might ask for a review or an audit as the loan grows.

Non-GAAP measures

Companies often add their own measures to the GAAP figures: adjusted earnings, EBITDA, free cash flow. A non-GAAP financial measure is allowed in SEC filings with conditions, among them that the company presents the most directly comparable GAAP measure with equal or greater prominence and reconciles the two. Amazon’s free cash flow in its 2014 10-K is an example: defined in a footnote, computed from two lines of its cash-flow statement. Read the reconciliation, watch how the definition changes over time, and compute your own measures from the GAAP figures.

The notes that matter most

The SEC’s beginners’ guide says the notes to the financial statements are packed with information. For a credit or equity analysis, five notes repay the most attention.

  • Revenue recognition: what the company sells, when it recognises revenue, contract assets and liabilities, and the judgements involved (ASC 606).
  • Leases: the right-of-use assets and lease liabilities, the discount rate, the remaining term and a maturity table of undiscounted payments (ASC 842).
  • Debt: each borrowing, its rate, its security, its covenants and when it falls due.
  • Commitments and contingencies: purchase obligations, guarantees and lawsuits that are not, or not yet, liabilities.
  • Subsequent events: what happened between the balance-sheet date and the date the statements were issued, such as new borrowing or a lost customer.

The ratios said Lantern was illiquid; the notes say exactly when the pressure peaks and what would relieve it.

Writing the analysis

An analysis is finished when it reaches a conclusion that its reader can act on. A memo to a lender or an owner usually has five parts, and the order matters: the reader should meet the conclusion first.

  1. Conclusion and recommendation, in two or three sentences: what you advise and on what conditions.
  2. The evidence: the few ratios and trends that support it, each with its benchmark (prior year, peers, covenant).
  3. What the numbers don’t show: from the notes and the MD&A, the timing of cash, the estimates, the commitments.
  4. Risks: what would make the conclusion wrong, and how likely it is.
  5. Conditions and follow-up: covenants, information to be reported, when to review.

State your conventions (averages or closing balances, leases in or out of debt, which Z-score), round sensibly, and don’t list every ratio you computed. A memo that ends “the company has strengths and weaknesses” has not finished the job.

Common mistakes

  • “Starbucks is the obvious benchmark for a coffee business.”

    It is the obvious context, not a benchmark. Its scale, its licensed stores and its negative equity make most of its ratios incomparable with Lantern’s. Benchmark against businesses with the same model and size, and use large companies to understand the industry.

  • “Use the average of the peers.”

    One unusual peer can drag an average a long way. The median and quartiles describe where a typical peer sits and how widely peers differ; say which quartile method you used, because spreadsheets offer more than one.

  • “The ratios are the analysis; the notes are detail.”

    The notes often change the conclusion. Lantern’s current ratio says it is illiquid; the lease, revenue and debt notes say when the pressure peaks (1 March 2028) and when it eases (31 March 2028).

  • “A clean audit opinion means the company is healthy.”

    An unqualified opinion says the statements are fairly presented, not that the business is sound. A fairly presented set of statements can show a business in distress; read the going-concern paragraph and the critical audit matters, then do the analysis.

  • “Adjusted earnings are the company’s real profit.”

    Non-GAAP measures are management’s choices. Start from the GAAP figures, read the reconciliation and check whether the adjustments recur year after year.

In practice

A bank’s peer files. Lenders that specialise in an industry build peer files from the statements their borrowers provide, as Coastline does for café businesses. Each borrower’s ratios are compared with the file’s quartiles every year, and a borrower that falls into the bottom quartile on several measures is reviewed. The file is the bank’s own data, which is why it is more useful than a general statistic, and why the bank keeps it confidential.

An investor reading a 10-K. An analyst who owns shares in a coffee roaster reads its 10-K in a fixed order: Item 1 for the business model, Item 1A for new risk factors (comparing them with last year’s), the MD&A for management’s explanation of margins and liquidity, the auditor’s report and its critical audit matters, then the notes on revenue, leases, debt and commitments, and only then the ratios. Reading in this order means she knows what the numbers ought to show before she computes them.

An owner’s own benchmark. Ana asks Coastline whether it can share the peer file’s quartiles, without the names. With them she sets her 2028 targets: an operating margin at the peer median, a current ratio above the peer Q1 by the end of 2028, and no new café until Lantern’s Z″ is back above the peer median. The same comparison that a lender uses to judge risk gives an owner a plan.

Summary

  • A ratio means something only against a benchmark: the company’s own trend, a target such as a covenant, or a peer group.
  • Choose peers by business model, size and stage, ownership and accounting basis; use larger companies for context, not targets.
  • Summarise peers by the median and quartiles, and say which quartile method you used (QUARTILE.INC places Q1 at (n − 1) × 0.25).
  • Lantern 2027 is below every peer on the current ratio, between Q1 and the median on operating margin, and near the bottom on Z″; the peers’ own Z″ median (2.4) is in the grey zone.
  • Separate scale, business model and accounting when comparing with a much larger company: Starbucks’ licensed stores, stored-value cards and negative equity make most of its ratios incomparable.
  • In a 10-K read Items 1, 1A, 7 (MD&A), 7A, 8 (statements, notes and the auditor’s report) and 9A; treat non-GAAP measures with care.
  • The notes on revenue, leases, debt, commitments and subsequent events supply the timing and the estimates that ratios can’t; a memo should start from a conclusion and support it with benchmarked evidence.

Terms introduced

Benchmark
A standard against which a ratio or result is judged, such as the company’s own earlier years, a target or covenant, or the median of a peer group.
Peer group
The set of comparable businesses, chosen for a similar business model, size, ownership and accounting basis, whose figures provide a benchmark.
Form 10-K
The annual report a US public company files with the SEC, with a fixed structure of items including the business description, risk factors, management’s discussion and analysis, and the audited financial statements and notes.
Management’s discussion and analysis
The section of an annual report (Item 7 of Form 10-K) in which management explains the results, liquidity, capital resources, known trends and uncertainties, and critical accounting estimates. Often shortened to MD&A.
Auditor’s report
The independent auditor’s opinion on whether the financial statements are presented fairly, in all material respects, under the applicable framework, with any going-concern paragraph and, for most public companies, critical audit matters.
