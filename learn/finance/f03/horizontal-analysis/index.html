F03 · Module 1, lesson 1 · Lesson 1 of 10

Horizontal analysis

Put two or more years of statements side by side, measure what changed in dollars, percentages, index numbers and compound growth rates, and decide which changes are real, which are produced by the calendar or by a new kind of business, and which matter.

About 75 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Identify the questions an owner, a lender and an investor bring to a set of statements, and the figures each looks at first.
  • Calculate dollar and percentage changes, base-year index numbers and compound annual growth rates from comparative statements.
  • Adjust a comparison for a part-year period by annualising flows, and explain when annualising misleads.
  • Recognise changes in a business that break comparability (new activities, new costs, new accounting) and restate a comparison on a like-for-like basis.
  • Assess which changes are material and write a short conclusion a lender or owner can act on.

Review first

  • The multi-step income statement — You prepared Lantern’s multi-step income statement; this lesson compares two of them line by line.
  • The balance sheet and equity — The classified balance sheet is the second statement you compare, and its totals are the bases you divide by.
  • Capital and liquidity — F00 introduced working capital and the current ratio; this course starts by watching them change.
  • Percentages and compound growth: (end ÷ start)^(1/years) − 1, from F04’s first lesson.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern’s accountant has closed the books for 2027, the year Ana Lim opened the Harbourside and Millbrook cafés. Net revenue rose from $193,320 to $577,860, yet net income fell from $27,387 to $22,431 and cash from $42,940 to $6,531. Ana wants to know whether the business is doing better or worse. Coastline Bank, which has lent Lantern money and given it a line of credit, wants to know whether it will be repaid. Both start in the same place: the two years side by side.

Financial statement analysis turns the statements you learned to prepare in F02 into judgements: what changed and why, how healthy the business is, and what someone should do about it. The statements themselves don’t make those judgements. A net income of $22,431 means little until you know what it was last year, what it would have been without a one-off cost, and what similar businesses earn.

Different readers bring different questions. An owner asks whether the business is earning enough on the money invested, whether a new venture is paying its way and whether there is cash to pay a dividend or a salary. A lender asks whether interest and principal will be paid on time, from operating cash rather than from new borrowing, and what is left if things go wrong. An investor deciding whether to buy shares asks how fast profits are growing, how much risk comes with them and what price is fair. Suppliers, employees and tax authorities ask narrower versions of the same questions.

Analysts have three basic tools. Horizontal analysis, this lesson, compares the same line across periods. Vertical analysis, the next lesson, compares each line with a total in the same period. Ratio analysis, modules 2 to 4, relates lines from different parts of the statements (current assets to current liabilities, profit to assets). The three answer different questions and are always used together.

Comparative statements and the two measures of change

Comparative financial statements show the current period and one or more earlier periods in adjacent columns, with the same lines in the same order. Public companies present them as a matter of course: Starbucks’ statement of earnings in its 2024 annual report shows fiscal 2024, 2023 and 2022 side by side. For each line you can then measure two things. The dollar change is the current amount minus the earlier one; it tells you the size of the change. The percentage change divides the dollar change by the earlier amount, the base; it tells you the speed of the change, and lets you compare lines of very different sizes.

Lantern’s net revenue rose by $384,540, or 198.9%: it nearly tripled. Its operating income fell by $4,929, or 13.1%. The first number says how much more business Lantern did; the second says that none of it reached profit. The table shows every line of the two income statements.

Key idea: Read the dollars and the percentages together

A percentage on a small base can be huge and unimportant; a small percentage on a large base can matter a great deal. Insurance rose 120%, but only by $1,800. Wages rose 777.2% and by $143,000: that line needs explaining. A line that starts at zero, like café sales, has no percentage change at all.

In depth

Computing changes: the rules

The base is always the earlier period. When the base is negative (a loss, or a negative cash flow), the plain formula gives the wrong sign: a loss of $40,000 that becomes a profit of $10,000 has improved, but (10,000 − (−40,000)) ÷ (−40,000) is −125%. Some analysts divide by the absolute value of the base, which gives +125%; this course’s ratio engine does that. Many prefer to write n/m and describe the change in dollars, because a percentage change from a loss to a profit has no natural meaning. When the base is zero, as for Lantern’s contract asset or café sales, no percentage exists: say what the new item is and how large it is.

Ratios and margins change in percentage points, not percent. Lantern’s net margin (net income as a share of revenue, the subject of the next lesson) fell from 14.2% to 3.9%: a fall of 10.3 percentage points, which is a 72.6% fall in the margin itself. Write “points” when you subtract two percentages, and keep percent for the relative change.

Index numbers: following a line over several years

With three or more years, percentage changes from year to year become hard to read. A trend percentage (or base-year index) divides every year’s amount by the amount in a chosen base year and multiplies by 100, so the base year is 100 and every other year reads as a percentage of it. Lines that grow at different speeds then separate on one scale. Choose a base year that was ordinary: an index based on a year of unusually low profit makes every later year look strong.

Lantern has only two periods, so the illustration is a real company. Starbucks reported total net revenues of $32,250.3 million for fiscal 2022, $35,975.6 million for 2023 and $36,176.2 million for 2024, and operating income of $4,617.8 million, $5,870.8 million and $5,408.8 million.

The index shows what year-on-year changes hide. Revenue rose 11.6% in fiscal 2023 and then almost stopped (0.6%). Operating income rose faster than revenue in 2023 and then fell back to 117.1, so the 2023 margin gain was partly lost. Part of the 2023 peak is not operating performance: fiscal 2023’s operating income includes a $91.3 million gain on the sale of assets, which flatters that year’s index. Meanwhile the number of stores grew from 38,038 to 40,199 (5.7%) during fiscal 2024. That count includes licensed stores, which bring Starbucks only licensed-store revenue, so it is not a like-for-like base for total revenue; still, revenue grew far more slowly than the store estate, which tells an analyst to look at sales per store before celebrating the new openings.

Growth rates and the compound annual growth rate

Over several years, the single growth figure analysts quote is the compound annual growth rate (CAGR) you met in F04: the constant yearly rate that takes the first year’s amount to the last year’s. Starbucks’ revenue grew at a CAGR of 5.9% from fiscal 2022 to fiscal 2024: (36,176.2 ÷ 32,250.3)^(1/2) − 1. The number of years is the number of gaps between the first and last year (two), not the number of years in the table (three).

A CAGR describes only the two ends. Starbucks’ operating income has a CAGR of 8.2% over the same two years, which sounds like steady growth, but it rose 27.1% and then fell 7.9%. Always look at the path as well; a simple average of the yearly growth rates is not the same thing either, and overstates the compound rate whenever growth varies.

Part-year periods: Lantern’s ten-month first year

Lantern’s 2026 runs from 1 March to 31 December. Comparing ten months of revenue with twelve flatters 2027: part of the 198.9% growth is two extra months. The usual correction is to annualise the part-year flows, multiplying them by 12 ÷ 10. Annualised 2026 revenue is $231,984, and growth on that base is 149.1% instead of 198.9%. Only flows (revenue, expenses, cash flows) are annualised. Stocks (balance sheet amounts at a date) are never annualised: Lantern’s cash at 31 December 2026 is $42,940, whatever the length of the year before it.

Annualising assumes that the missing months are typical of the months you have. For Lantern they are not. The ten months of 2026 include the whole fourth-quarter gift season: about 60% of 2026’s shop order value came in October to December (B01’s monthly estimates, orders × average order value). The shop also relaunched on 1 July (B01): order value was $9,096.15 in March–June against an estimated $101,928.40 in July–December, so 2026’s ten months are anything but typical. The missing months, January and February, are quieter than an average month. Against the same months of 2027, unadjusted growth therefore overstates the change (two extra months) and annualised growth understates it (the 12 ÷ 10 scaling treats the gift peak as an ordinary month): the two figures bracket the truth rather than measure it.

Note: The better fix: same months

If monthly figures are available, compare March to December 2027 with March to December 2026. Like-for-like months remove both the length problem and the seasonal problem. Lantern’s managers can do this from their own records; an outside lender with only the annual statements cannot, and must annualise and say so.

When the business itself changed

Horizontal analysis assumes the same business in both columns. Lantern’s 2027 breaks that assumption three ways. First, a new activity: the cafés produced $236,000 of revenue that had no 2026 counterpart, with their own staff, lease costs and equipment. Second, new costs for the old business: Ana was paid only through dividends in 2026 and drew a salary of $48,000 in 2027, and Lantern spent $21,000 on market research. Had Ana been paid at that rate for the ten months of 2026 ($40,000), 2026’s operating income of $37,716 would have been −$2,284: a loss, and its net income a loss of $2,613 after a tax benefit at 25% (in practice, a loss to carry forward). The fall in operating income is partly a change in how the owner is paid, not in how the business performed.

Third, new accounts and new accounting. The cafés’ leases put a $173,379 right-of-use asset and an equal lease liability on the balance sheet under ASC 842, so total assets grew 250.0%, but only 64.9% without the right-of-use asset. The law firm’s catering contract added a contract asset of $30,840 (revenue earned but not yet billable; a later lesson asks how far to trust it). A line of credit, an instalment note for the cold-brew brewer and operating lease cost appear for the first time. Other events that break comparability in larger companies include acquisitions and disposals, changes in accounting policy and new standards. A new standard may or may not be applied to the earlier years shown, depending on the transition method it allows, so read the note on accounting changes before comparing across the year of adoption.

The response is to separate the comparable from the new: compare the activities that existed in both years, show the new ones on their own, and adjust for one-off or reclassified costs where you can measure them. The applied worked example below does this for Lantern.

Materiality: which changes deserve attention

A full comparative statement has dozens of changes, and an analysis that comments on all of them hides the few that matter. Materiality, from F01, is the test: would the change influence the reader’s decision? Analysts often screen with two thresholds at once, a percentage and a dollar amount, and flag only lines that change by more than both. The thresholds are a working convention chosen for the business and the reader, not a rule. With 10% and $5,000, Lantern’s insurance (120%, $1,800), interest (139.9%, $1,679) and bad debts (−5.4%) drop out, as do rent, fuel, miscellaneous costs, sales returns and advertising (up exactly $5,000, not more). Wages, cost of goods sold, cost of services, utilities, depreciation, shipping, card fees, the new lease cost and research stay in, among others, as do cash, current liabilities and the lease balances.

Size is not the only test. A small change can be material by its nature: interest more than doubled because Lantern began borrowing on a line of credit, which matters to Coastline far more than the $1,679 suggests. And a line that barely moved can matter because it should have moved: accounts payable rose 0.8% while purchases grew with a business three times the size, a question for the efficiency lesson.

Caution: Growth is not health

Revenue up 198.9%, cash down 84.8%, current liabilities up 335.7%: horizontal analysis shows the direction of travel, but not whether Lantern can pay its bills. The liquidity, solvency and cash-flow lessons answer that; for now, note that the fastest-growing lines are on the wrong side for a lender.

Common mistakes

  • “Revenue tripled, so the business is three times as good.”

    Growth in revenue says nothing about profit, cash or risk. Lantern’s revenue grew 198.9% while operating income fell 13.1% and cash fell 84.8%. Read every important line, and read the cash and the liabilities as well as the income statement.

  • “Ten months against twelve is close enough.”

    Two extra months are 20% more time. Annualise the part-year flows (× 12 ÷ 10), or better compare the same months, and say which you did. Never annualise balance sheet amounts: they are stocks at a date.

  • “A percentage change can always be calculated.”

    From a base of zero (a new line such as Lantern’s café sales) there is no percentage change, and from a negative base the plain formula gives the wrong sign. Write n/m and describe the change in dollars.

  • “The margin rose from 5% to 7%, so it rose 2%.”

    Subtracting two percentages gives percentage points: the margin rose 2 points, which is a 40% increase in the margin. Use points for differences between percentages and percent for relative changes.

  • “The CAGR shows how the business grew each year.”

    A CAGR uses only the first and last amounts. Starbucks’ operating income had a two-year CAGR of 8.2% while it rose 27.1% and then fell 7.9%. Show the path (an index or the yearly changes) beside the CAGR, and count the years as gaps, not columns.

In practice

The lender’s first page. Coastline Bank’s credit analyst opens Lantern’s file with a one-page comparative summary: the income statement and the condensed balance sheet with dollar and percentage changes, a column of 2026 flows annualised and a line explaining that 2026 ran for ten months. She marks every line that passes the bank’s screening thresholds, then writes one sentence for each: what changed, why, and whether it helps or hurts repayment. Revenue growth helps; the fall in cash to $6,531, the new $24,000 line of credit and the 335.7% rise in current liabilities hurt. The cafés and the leases explain most of the balance sheet’s growth, so she asks Lantern for the cafés’ results by month, the only way to see whether their sales are still rising.

The owner’s questions. Ana reads the same statements with different eyes. The bridge in the applied example tells her that her salary and the research programme, not a failing business, account for the lower profit. Her next questions come from the same table: are the cafés covering their share of the shared costs yet, and will 2028’s results, the first full year of cafés with no research programme, show the operating income she expected? She asks her accountant to keep monthly figures by activity from now on, so next year’s comparison can be like for like.

Reading a public company. An equity analyst reading Starbucks’ 10-K starts the same way, with the three years of the statement of earnings side by side and the store counts in Item 1. The index numbers show revenue almost flat in fiscal 2024 while stores grew 5.7%, so the analyst reads management’s discussion of comparable store sales for the explanation before forming a view. Horizontal analysis rarely answers the question; it tells the analyst which question to ask next.

Summary

  • Owners ask whether the business earns enough, lenders whether they will be repaid, and investors how fast profits will grow at what risk; each starts from the comparative statements.
  • Horizontal analysis measures each line’s dollar change (size) and percentage change on the earlier base (speed); read them together, and write n/m when the base is zero or negative.
  • Trend percentages put several years on one scale (base year = 100); the CAGR, (end ÷ start)^(1/years) − 1, summarises growth between two ends but hides the path.
  • Part-year flows are annualised (× 12 ÷ months) or, better, compared month for month; stocks are never annualised. Seasonal businesses make annualised figures misleading: Lantern’s 2026 includes the whole gift season.
  • New activities, new costs and new accounting break comparability: separate the comparable from the new, as Lantern’s cafés, Ana’s salary and the leases require.
  • Materiality decides which changes to discuss: a change can matter by its size in dollars and percent together, or by its nature.

Terms introduced

Horizontal analysis
Comparing the same line of the financial statements across two or more periods, as a dollar change and a percentage change on the earlier period. Also called trend analysis.
Comparative financial statements
Financial statements that show the current period and one or more earlier periods in adjacent columns, with the same lines in the same order.
Trend percentage
A line’s amount in a year divided by its amount in a chosen base year, times 100, so the base year reads 100. Also called a base-year index.
Annualise
To scale a flow for a part-year period to a full year, by multiplying it by 12 and dividing by the number of months it covers. Balance sheet amounts are never annualised.
Comparability
The quality of financial information that lets users identify similarities and differences between periods or between businesses; it is lost when the business, its accounting policies or the length of the period change.
