US GAAP and IFRS

Compare the two frameworks where they differ for the statements in this course (inventory, property, development costs, leases, cash-flow classification and, from 2027, the structure of the income statement), measure what each difference does to the figures, and see why Lantern reports under US GAAP.

About 85 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Describe who sets US GAAP and IFRS, the SEC’s role, and how far the two frameworks converged.
  • Classify each difference between the frameworks by what it changes: profit, assets and equity only, or presentation only.
  • Calculate how a difference between the frameworks changes a company’s profit, assets or operating cash flow, and restate a profit figure from one framework to the other.
  • Explain what IFRS 18 changes in the income statement and what ASU 2024-03 requires of US public business entities, with their effective dates.
  • Identify the frameworks a US private company may use, and explain why Lantern uses US GAAP.

The idea

Fictional case

In January 2027 Ana meets a possible investor who also owns shares in Aldgate Coffee plc, a fictional UK-listed roaster that reports under IFRS. “Your numbers and Aldgate’s won’t be comparable,” he tells her. Ana wants to know whether he is right: which of Lantern’s figures would change under IFRS, and which rules could make Aldgate’s statements look different from Lantern’s even if the two businesses were identical?

Two frameworks dominate financial reporting. US GAAP, written by the FASB and organised in its Accounting Standards Codification, is what the SEC requires of US public companies and what Lantern uses. IFRS Accounting Standards, written by the IASB, are required in more than 140 jurisdictions. F01 introduced the boards, and earlier lessons in this course met most of the differences one at a time; this lesson puts them side by side and asks what each does to the figures.

Most of what you have learned works the same way under both. Both use accrual accounting and double entry, and both produce the same four statements. Revenue, the largest figure on most income statements, follows the same five-step model, because the IASB issued IFRS 15 in May 2014 together with the FASB’s Topic 606. The differences sit in particular choices: how inventory is costed and whether a write-down can be reversed, whether property can be carried at fair value and how it is depreciated, whether development spending becomes an asset, how leases are accounted for, where interest and dividends go in the statement of cash flows and, from 2027, how the income statement itself is structured.

A difference matters when it changes a figure a reader uses, and the differences change different figures. Some change profit: LIFO, write-down reversals, development costs and component depreciation. One changes assets and equity without passing through profit when it happens: an upward revaluation. Some change only where an item is shown: interest and dividends in the statement of cash flows, and the new income statement categories; leases do some of each. Knowing which kind you face tells you what to adjust before comparing two companies.

Key idea: Recognition, measurement, presentation

Ask three questions of any difference. Is the item recognised at all, or when (development costs)? At what amount is it measured (LIFO, write-down reversals, revaluation, components)? Where is it presented (cash-flow classification, income statement categories)? Differences of the first two kinds change profit, assets or equity; differences of the third kind change only subtotals.

In depth

Who sets the rules, and how far they converged

The SEC has legal authority over the financial reporting of US public companies and recognises the FASB as their standard setter; it requires domestic public companies to use US GAAP. Since a 2007 rule it has accepted statements prepared under IFRS as issued by the IASB from foreign private issuers (foreign companies whose securities trade in the United States) without a reconciliation to US GAAP. The SEC sets no framework for private companies such as Lantern; who decides for them is covered at the end of this lesson.

On 18 September 2002, at a joint meeting in Norwalk, Connecticut, the FASB and the IASB pledged their best efforts to make their existing standards fully compatible as soon as practicable, and to coordinate their future work so that compatibility would last. The Norwalk Agreement started a decade of joint projects aimed at convergence. The most visible result for this course is revenue: IFRS 15 and Topic 606 were issued together in May 2014. Many differences survived, including every one in this lesson, and in 2024 the two boards issued separate standards on the income statement, IFRS 18 and ASU 2024-03, that differ in scope and depth.

Inventory, property and development costs, in brief

Earlier lessons in this course taught each of these rules. Here they are side by side, with what each does to the figures.

  • Inventory cost formulas. US GAAP (ASC 330) allows LIFO; IAS 2 doesn’t, and the US LIFO conformity rule means a company that wants LIFO’s tax saving must report under LIFO too. For Lantern’s House Blend in the fourth quarter of 2026, periodic LIFO would have meant $360 less gross profit than FIFO ($22,920 of cost of goods sold against $22,560) and, at 25%, $90 less tax.
  • Write-downs that recover. Both frameworks write inventory down to net realisable value; US GAAP has done so since ASU 2015-11 for every cost method except LIFO and the retail inventory method. Only IAS 2 reverses a write-down when value recovers, up to the amount written off (paragraph 33); under ASC 330-10-35-14 the written-down amount becomes the new cost. IFRS profit is therefore higher in the year of recovery, US GAAP profit when the goods are sold.
  • Revaluation. IAS 16 offers a revaluation model, class by class: increases go to other comprehensive income and a revaluation surplus in equity, and later depreciation is based on the revalued amount. US GAAP allows the cost model only. The simple example below follows a revaluation and a later fall in value.
  • Components. IAS 16 requires component depreciation for significant parts with different useful lives (paragraph 43); US GAAP permits it but doesn’t require it. Had Lantern’s $12,000 of equipment been an $8,400 machine lasting five years and a $3,600 grinder lasting three, ten months’ depreciation would have been (8,400÷60+3,600÷36)×10=2,400(8{,}400 \div 60 + 3{,}600 \div 36) \times 10 = 2{,}400(8,400÷60+3,600÷36)×10=2,400 rather than $2,000.
  • Development costs. ASC 730 expenses research and development as incurred; software to be sold is capitalised under ASC 985-20 once technological feasibility is established. IAS 38 expenses research but capitalises development once six criteria are met (paragraph 57): technical feasibility, the intention and the ability to complete and use or sell the asset, probable future economic benefits, adequate resources, and reliable measurement of its cost. Spending expensed before then can’t be capitalised later (paragraph 71).

Cash-flow classification and leases

Two differences change where amounts appear more than their totals. Under ASC 230 interest paid, interest received and dividends received are operating cash flows; IAS 7 as amended by IFRS 18 makes interest and dividends paid financing, and interest and dividends received investing, for a company without a specified main business activity (the statement of cash flows lesson has the full table). Lantern’s only such item is the $450 of interest paid, so its operating cash flow would be $40,390 instead of $39,940, financing $24,550 instead of $25,000, and the increase in cash unchanged at $42,940. The amendments also make operating profit, not profit for the year, the starting point of the indirect method.

Leases are the larger difference for most companies. Both standards put most leases longer than 12 months on the balance sheet, but IFRS 16 has a single lessee model, with depreciation and interest in profit or loss and principal repayments in financing, while ASC 842 keeps operating leases, whose cost is one straight-line expense and whose payments are operating cash flows. For the same lease an IFRS reporter therefore usually shows higher operating profit and higher operating cash flow. Lantern pays $1,200 a month for its shared kitchen (F01’s entry m13); assuming either side can end the arrangement at a month’s notice, it is a short-term lease of 12 months or less, which both standards let a lessee keep off the balance sheet, so the rent is an expense under either.

The income statement: IFRS 18 and ASU 2024-03

IFRS 18 Presentation and Disclosure in Financial Statements, issued by the IASB in April 2024, replaces IAS 1 for annual reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027, with earlier application permitted. It is applied retrospectively, so the previous year’s comparative figures are restated. Its three main changes are two to the statement of profit or loss and one to the notes.

  • Categories. Income and expenses are classified in five categories: operating, investing, financing, income taxes and discontinued operations. Operating is the default category, holding everything not classified elsewhere, including unusual items. Investing holds returns from assets that earn a return largely independently of the company’s other resources, such as dividends from shares in other companies and rental income. Financing holds income and expenses on liabilities such as bank loans and bonds, and interest on other liabilities such as leases and pensions.
  • Required subtotals. A company without a specified main business activity presents operating profit and profit before financing and income taxes, so these subtotals mean the same thing in every such company’s income statement. Companies whose main business is lending or investing classify some of these items differently.
  • Management-defined performance measures, in a note. A subtotal of income and expenses that a company uses in public communications outside its financial statements, such as an “adjusted operating profit”, is a management-defined performance measure and must be explained in a single note: how it is calculated and why it is useful, a reconciliation to the most directly comparable subtotal that IFRS requires, with the tax and non-controlling-interest effect of each reconciling item, and a statement that it may not be comparable with similar measures used by other companies.

IFRS 18 also adds principles for grouping information, aggregating and disaggregating items. A company that presents any operating expenses by function (cost of sales, selling, administration) must disclose in a note the amounts of five specified expenses by nature included in each line: depreciation, amortisation, employee benefits, impairment losses and inventory write-downs. The IASB described its proposals as changes to presentation and disclosure, not to recognition and measurement: a company’s profit for the year is the same, but the way it is built up and explained changes.

US GAAP has no equivalent of the IFRS 18 categories or required subtotals. It sets few rules for the layout of a private company’s income statement; SEC registrants follow the line items of Regulation S-X Rule 5-03. Measures such as adjusted EBITDA stay outside the financial statements, as non-GAAP financial measures: a public company that uses one must reconcile it to the most directly comparable GAAP measure (Regulation G) and, in SEC filings and earnings releases, present that GAAP measure with equal or greater prominence (Item 10(e) of Regulation S-K).

The FASB’s 2024 standard on the income statement is narrower. ASU 2024-03, issued in November 2024 (Subtopic 220-40, Expense Disaggregation Disclosures), applies only to public business entities and changes nothing on the face of the income statement. In a tabular note, a company discloses for each relevant expense caption the amounts of purchases of inventory, employee compensation, depreciation, intangible asset amortisation and depletion that it contains, describes the rest qualitatively, and discloses its total selling expenses and, in annual periods, how it defines them. It is effective for annual reporting periods beginning after 15 December 2026 and for interim periods within annual reporting periods beginning after 15 December 2027, as ASU 2025-01 (January 2025) clarified, with early adoption permitted.

What Lantern uses, and why

No regulator tells a US private company which framework to use. The IFRS Foundation’s profile of the United States says a private company can select the framework that fits the purpose of its statements, unless a contract specifies one: US GAAP, full IFRS, or the IFRS for SMEs Accounting Standard, a separate, simpler IASB standard that the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants (AICPA) recognises. Owner-managed businesses that don’t need GAAP statements may also use a special-purpose framework: the cash or modified cash basis, the tax basis, or the AICPA’s Financial Reporting Framework for Small- and Medium-Sized Entities, released in June 2013.

Lantern uses US GAAP for the reason F01 gave: its bank and any future investor expect GAAP statements, and can compare them with those of other US businesses. As a private company it may also elect accounting alternatives developed by the Private Company Council. Lantern is not a public business entity, so ASU 2024-03 doesn’t apply to it, and IFRS 18 applies only to companies that report under IFRS.

Standards: Lantern under IFRS in 2026

If Lantern had prepared its 2026 statements under IFRS, with IFRS 18 applied early, its net income, total assets and increase in cash would be the same. It uses FIFO, has spent nothing on development, owns no property it might revalue, rents its kitchen month by month (a short-term lease under either standard), and its Holiday Blend write-down has not recovered; its equipment would need splitting only if it had significant parts with different lives. Two things would change: the $450 of interest paid would move to financing, and the income statement would show operating profit and profit before financing and income taxes, both $37,716, with the $1,200 of interest expense in the financing category.

Common mistakes

  • “A revaluation gain is profit.”

    Under IAS 16 a revaluation increase goes to other comprehensive income and builds up a revaluation surplus in equity; it reaches profit only to the extent that it reverses an earlier revaluation loss recognised in profit or loss. It does raise later depreciation, which lowers future profit.

  • “A fall in a revalued asset’s value always goes to profit.”

    Under IAS 16 the decrease is first taken against any revaluation surplus on the same asset, through other comprehensive income; only the excess is a loss in profit. Fairhaven’s $740,000 decrease cost its profit only $140,000.

  • “IFRS capitalises research and development.”

    IAS 38 never capitalises research. Development costs are capitalised only from the point at which all six criteria are demonstrated; earlier development spending stays in expense and can’t be capitalised later. US GAAP expenses both, apart from software to be sold.

  • “IFRS 18 changes how much profit a company makes.”

    IFRS 18 is about presentation and disclosure. It reorganises the income statement into categories, requires two subtotals and adds notes; Lantern’s profit for the period would still be $27,387. What changes is what the subtotals mean and where items such as interest are shown.

  • “ASU 2024-03 is the US version of IFRS 18.”

    ASU 2024-03 adds a note disaggregating expense captions for public business entities. It requires no new categories or subtotals on the face of the income statement and has nothing on management-defined performance measures, which in the United States stay outside the statements under the SEC’s non-GAAP rules.

  • “Leases are off the balance sheet under US GAAP.”

    Under ASC 842 operating leases longer than 12 months are on the balance sheet too, as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability. The difference from IFRS 16 is the expense pattern and the cash-flow classification: a single straight-line cost and operating cash flows under ASC 842, depreciation, interest and financing repayments under IFRS 16.

In practice

Return to the investor’s claim. Suppose he wants to compare Lantern’s results with Aldgate Coffee plc’s, and Aldgate reports under IFRS with IFRS 18 from 2027. A careful analyst doesn’t compare headline figures until the frameworks’ differences have been dealt with, and she works through them in the order of the comparison table, asking each time whether the difference changes profit, assets and equity, or only a subtotal.

Cash flows come first. Aldgate will classify interest paid as financing, so she moves Lantern’s $450 there too, or moves Aldgate’s interest back, so that both are measured the same way. If Aldgate leases its cafés for several years at a time, IFRS 16 also puts the principal part of its rent in financing, so she reads the lease note before comparing operating cash flows or operating profit.

Then profit. Aldgate can’t use LIFO and Lantern doesn’t, so no adjustment is needed there. If Aldgate has capitalised development costs, for a new cold-brew line say, its profit is higher this year than US GAAP would allow, and the intangible assets note shows how much was capitalised and how much amortised; if it reversed an inventory write-down, the inventory note says so. With those amounts she can restate Aldgate’s profit before tax on a US GAAP basis.

Then assets and equity. If Aldgate revalues its roastery, its equity includes a revaluation surplus and its depreciation is based on fair value, so any ratio built on assets or equity will differ from a cost-based company’s for the same business; F03 covers those ratios. Finally the subtotals: from 2027 Aldgate’s operating profit is defined by IFRS 18, and any “adjusted” profit its managers quote is reconciled in a note. Lantern’s multi-step income statement has an operating income line too, but US GAAP doesn’t define it, so the analyst checks what Lantern included: for Lantern, operating income of $37,716 contains everything except interest and tax, the same boundary IFRS 18 would draw.

The conclusion for Ana: the investor is right that the frameworks differ, but for a business like Lantern’s the differences are few, identifiable and measurable, and none of them changes its 2026 net income. The analyst’s work is to find them, adjust for them and say which she couldn’t adjust for, which is the same discipline a preparer applies when checking that statements articulate.

Summary

  • The FASB writes US GAAP, which the SEC requires of US public companies; the IASB writes IFRS, required in more than 140 jurisdictions. The 2002 Norwalk Agreement led to joint standards such as IFRS 15 and Topic 606 (May 2014), but many differences remain.
  • Differences change profit (LIFO, write-down reversals, development costs, components), assets and equity without profit (an upward revaluation), or only presentation (cash-flow classification, IFRS 18 subtotals); leases do some of each.
  • Under IAS 16 a revaluation increase goes to other comprehensive income, and a later decrease is taken first against that surplus and only then to profit; US GAAP stays at cost.
  • Development costs are expensed under ASC 730 but capitalised under IAS 38 once six criteria are met; total expense over a project’s life is the same, only its timing differs.
  • Cash flows: IFRS 18 amends IAS 7 so most companies show interest and dividends paid as financing and those received as investing, and IFRS 16 puts lease principal in financing. Lantern’s operating cash flow would be $40,390 instead of $39,940.
  • IFRS 18 (issued April 2024; annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027) adds five categories, two required subtotals and a note on management-defined performance measures; ASU 2024-03 (November 2024; public business entities, annual periods beginning after 15 December 2026) adds a note disaggregating expenses.
  • A US private company chooses its framework; Lantern uses US GAAP because its bank and investors expect it, and neither ASU 2024-03 nor IFRS 18 applies to it.

Terms introduced

Convergence
The effort by the FASB and the IASB, formalised in the 2002 Norwalk Agreement, to remove differences between US GAAP and IFRS and to coordinate their future standards.
Foreign private issuer
A company incorporated outside the United States that meets the SEC’s tests for foreign status (broadly, no more than half its voting shares held by US residents or, if more, management, assets and administration not mainly in the United States). Since 2007 the SEC has accepted its IFRS statements without reconciliation to US GAAP.
Other comprehensive income
Income and expenses that accounting standards require to be reported outside profit or loss and accumulated in a separate part of equity, such as revaluation increases on property under IAS 16.
Management-defined performance measure
Under IFRS 18, a subtotal of income and expenses that a company uses in public communications outside its financial statements to convey management’s view of its performance, and that IFRS does not itself specify. It must be explained and reconciled in a note.
Public business entity
A US GAAP term for a business that meets any of five criteria, such as filing financial statements with the SEC or having securities traded, listed or quoted on an exchange or an over-the-counter market. Some standards, such as ASU 2024-03, apply only to public business entities.
Private Company Council
The body set up in 2012 by the Financial Accounting Foundation, which oversees the FASB, as the FASB’s main advisory body on private-company matters. It develops accounting alternatives that private companies may elect, subject to the FASB’s endorsement.
