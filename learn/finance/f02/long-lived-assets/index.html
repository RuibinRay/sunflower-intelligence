Long-lived assets

Equipment, vehicles, buildings and patents help a business earn revenue for years, so their cost is spread over those years. Learn what goes into an asset’s cost, how to depreciate it by three methods, what to do when estimates change or the asset is sold, how intangible assets and goodwill are treated, and where US GAAP and IFRS part company.

About 100 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Determine the cost of a long-lived asset by deciding which expenditures to capitalise and which to expense.
  • Calculate depreciation by the straight-line, double-declining-balance and units-of-production methods, including part years under a stated convention.
  • Record depreciation, a change in an accounting estimate and the disposal of an asset with a gain or loss.
  • Calculate the amortisation of a patent, and explain how goodwill is treated by public and private companies under US GAAP and under IFRS.
  • Compare US GAAP and IFRS on revaluation, component depreciation and impairment.

  • What accounting is and why businesses need it — The historical cost principle and materiality decide what is capitalised.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern’s balance sheet at 31 December lists two assets that will still be working long after this year’s coffee has been drunk: the espresso machine and grinder bought on 3 March for $12,000, and the delivery van bought on 1 June for $30,000, paid with $10,000 in cash and a $20,000 note. Neither cost went straight to expense. Each is being spread over the years it helps earn revenue, $200 a month for the equipment and $400 a month for the van, so at the year end their book values are $10,000 and $27,200.

Assets that a business expects to use for more than a year are called long-lived assets. Those with a physical form, such as land, buildings, vehicles and equipment, are property, plant and equipment; those without one, such as patents, software licences and trademarks, are intangible assets. Because their benefits last for several periods, their cost is first recorded as an asset, which is what it means to capitalise a cost, and then allocated to expense over the periods that benefit. For tangible assets the allocation is depreciation; for intangible assets it is amortisation. Land is the exception: it doesn’t wear out, so it is not depreciated.

Every long-lived asset passes through the same four decisions. When it is acquired, the accountant decides which costs belong in it. While it is used, a method and estimates (useful life and residual value) decide how much of the cost each period bears. Each year the estimates are reviewed, and the asset is checked for impairment if something suggests it can no longer earn back its book value. And when it is sold or scrapped, it leaves the books, usually with a gain or a loss.

These choices matter because they move profit between years without moving any cash. Lantern’s depreciation for 2026 is $4,800, about 13% of its operating income of $37,716. A longer life, a higher residual value or a slower method would raise this year’s profit and lower later years’; the cash paid for the machine and the van left the bank when they were bought, and nothing about depreciation brings it back. That is why readers of financial statements look at depreciation policies closely, and why both US GAAP and IFRS require estimates to be reasonable and reviewed.

Key idea: What depreciation is, and isn’t

Depreciation allocates the cost of an asset, less its expected residual value, over the periods that use it. It is not an estimate of what the asset is worth today, and it doesn’t set money aside to replace it. A van with a book value of $27,200 might sell for more or less than that.

In depth

What goes into the cost of an asset

Under the historical cost principle an asset is recorded at everything it took to acquire it and get it ready for its intended use. IAS 16 spells this out: cost is the purchase price, including import duties and non-refundable purchase taxes, after trade discounts and rebates, plus the costs directly attributable to bringing the asset to the location and condition needed for it to operate as intended, such as site preparation, delivery and handling, installation and assembly, and testing (IAS 16, paragraphs 16–17). US GAAP (ASC 360) applies the same idea. Costs that would be incurred whether or not the asset was bought do not qualify: opening a new facility, advertising a new product, training staff, and administration and general overheads are expenses (IAS 16, paragraph 19).

  • Capitalise: purchase price less trade discounts; non-refundable sales tax and import duties; freight and insurance in transit; installation, assembly and testing; professional fees such as an architect’s or engineer’s; for land, legal fees, title costs and the cost of clearing it.
  • Expense: staff training; day-to-day servicing and repairs; repairs of damage caused in delivery or installation; advertising; general overheads; interest on money borrowed to buy a finished asset.

Land, land improvements and buildings are kept in separate accounts because they are treated differently. Land has an unlimited life and is not depreciated. Land improvements, such as a car park, fences and outdoor lighting, wear out and are depreciated. A building is depreciated over its own life. When a business buys land with an old building that it demolishes, the cost of demolition (less anything recovered from the materials) is part of the cost of the land, because it was needed to make the land ready for use.

Spending after an asset is in use follows the same test. Day-to-day servicing (oil changes, descaling the espresso machine, new tyres when they wear out) keeps the asset working as expected and is expensed (IAS 16, paragraph 12). Spending that extends the useful life, increases capacity or improves quality, such as a new engine or a second grinder head, is capitalised and depreciated over the remaining life. Many businesses set a capitalisation threshold, expensing small purchases such as a $40 kettle even though it will last for years; this applies the idea of materiality and is a policy, not a rule of the standards.

Three depreciation methods

Every method allocates the same total, the depreciable amount (cost minus residual value), over the same useful life; they differ only in timing. IAS 16 requires the depreciable amount to be allocated on a systematic basis (paragraph 50) by a method that reflects the pattern in which the asset’s benefits are consumed (paragraph 60), and names three: the straight-line method, the diminishing balance method and the units of production method (paragraph 62). It forbids methods based on the revenue an asset generates (paragraph 62A). US companies use the same three methods.

The straight-line method of F01 charges the same amount every full year. The double-declining-balance method, the commonest diminishing balance method, charges twice the straight-line rate on the asset’s book value at the start of each year: for a five-year asset, 2 × 20% = 40% (a 150% declining-balance method uses 1.5 times the straight-line rate, 30%). Residual value is not subtracted from the base, but depreciation stops when book value reaches it. The method front-loads the expense, which suits assets that lose most of their usefulness early, such as computers. The units-of-production method ties depreciation to use: divide the depreciable amount by the total expected output (miles, hours, units), and charge that rate for each unit used.

Note: Part years, and tax

An asset bought during the year needs a convention for its first and last years. Lantern’s policy, from F01, charges a full month in the month of purchase, so its van bears seven months in 2026 and five in 2031. Other businesses use half a year whatever the purchase date, or count months from the middle of the month; any convention is acceptable if applied consistently. US tax returns ignore all of this: most business property must be depreciated for tax by the Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System (MACRS) set out in IRS Publication 946, with its own recovery periods and accelerated methods. A company’s book and tax depreciation are therefore usually different.

When an estimate changes

Useful lives and residual values are estimates, and IAS 16 requires them to be reviewed at least at each financial year-end (paragraph 51). When new information changes an estimate, the change is a change in accounting estimate: it is applied from the period of the change onward, and earlier statements are not restated (ASC 250; IAS 8, paragraph 36). The new charge spreads the book value that remains, less the new residual value, over the remaining life. Suppose that on 1 January 2028 Ana decides to replace the espresso machine at the end of 2029 and expects to sell it then for $1,600. Its book value at 31 December 2027 is 12,000−2,000−2,400=7,60012{,}000 - 2{,}000 - 2{,}400 = 7{,}60012,000−2,000−2,400=7,600, so depreciation for 2028 and 2029 becomes (7,600−1,600)÷2=3,000(7{,}600 - 1{,}600) \div 2 = 3{,}000(7,600−1,600)÷2=3,000 a year instead of $2,400; the 2026 and 2027 figures stand, because they were the best estimates at the time.

A change of depreciation method is treated the same way, prospectively. US GAAP calls it a change in estimate effected by a change in accounting principle and allows it only if the new method is preferable; IAS 16 requires the method itself to be reviewed each year (paragraph 61). A method is not something to switch to improve a year’s profit.

Disposals: gains and losses

When an asset is sold, scrapped or traded in, three steps take it off the books. First, record depreciation up to the date of disposal, so the book value is current (by whole months under Lantern’s convention: an asset sold on 31 May bears May’s depreciation, one sold on 1 June does not). Second, remove the asset’s cost and its accumulated depreciation: credit the asset account, debit the contra account. Third, record what was received and the difference: proceeds above book value are a gain on disposal; proceeds below it, a loss. An asset scrapped for nothing produces a loss equal to its book value.

A gain on disposal is not revenue, because selling used equipment is not what the business exists to do (IAS 16 says so in paragraph 68). Under US GAAP the gain or loss belongs in income from continuing operations, and if the income statement shows an operating income subtotal, it is included in that subtotal (ASC 360-10-45-5). Many introductory textbooks show them under other income instead, but a US GAAP income statement that reports operating income must include them in it, as Lantern’s chart does. Either way they are kept apart from revenue and from the depreciation charge. A gain or loss is partly a correction of the depreciation charged earlier: had the estimates been perfect, the book value would have equalled the proceeds and there would have been neither.

Intangible assets

An intangible asset is recognised when it is bought, or acquired as part of a business, and can be identified separately: a patent, a copyright, a trademark, a licence, customer lists or software. A patent gives its owner the exclusive right to an invention; a US utility patent filed on or after 8 June 1995 runs for 20 years from the filing date. Intangibles with a finite useful life are amortised, usually by the straight-line method and with no residual value, over the shorter of their legal life and the period the business expects to benefit. Those with an indefinite life, such as a trademark the owner can renew indefinitely and intends to keep, are not amortised but are tested for impairment every year. Lantern’s chart records amortisation in a contra account, Accumulated Amortisation—Patents; some companies credit the patent account directly.

Intangibles a business creates itself are a different matter. US GAAP expenses research and development as it is incurred (ASC 730-10), so a patent developed in-house carries only its legal and registration costs. IAS 38 also expenses research but capitalises development costs once specified criteria are met. The GAAP and IFRS lesson compares the two.

Goodwill

Goodwill arises only when one business buys another. It is the amount by which the price paid exceeds the fair value of the identifiable assets acquired less the liabilities assumed, and it stands for things that can’t be recognised separately: reputation, an assembled workforce, expected savings from combining the two. Suppose Aster Hotel Group, a fictional chain, pays $2,600,000 for a guesthouse business whose identifiable assets are worth $2,450,000 and whose liabilities are $350,000. Goodwill is 2,600,000−(2,450,000−350,000)=500,0002{,}600{,}000 - (2{,}450{,}000 - 350{,}000) = 500{,}0002,600,000−(2,450,000−350,000)=500,000. A business can’t record goodwill it builds for itself, however valuable its name.

What happens next depends on who reports. A public company under US GAAP does not amortise goodwill; it tests it for impairment at least annually (ASC 350-20-35-28) and writes it down if it is impaired. A private company may elect an accounting alternative, introduced by ASU 2014-02 and extended to not-for-profits by ASU 2019-06, that amortises goodwill on a straight-line basis over ten years, or less if another life is more appropriate, and tests it for impairment only when an event suggests it may be impaired; Aster, if private, could amortise 500,000÷10=50,000500{,}000 \div 10 = 50{,}000500,000÷10=50,000 a year. Full IFRS does not amortise goodwill either (the separate IFRS for SMEs Accounting Standard does): IAS 36 tests it every year, and an impairment loss on goodwill is never reversed. The FASB considered requiring public companies to amortise goodwill but removed that project from its agenda in June 2022; in July 2026 it added a project on the level and frequency of goodwill impairment testing, so check the current rule before relying on this paragraph.

Impairment in outline

An asset is impaired when it can no longer earn back its carrying amount, perhaps because demand has fallen, the asset is damaged or technology has moved on. The two frameworks test this differently. US GAAP (ASC 360) uses two steps when events suggest a problem: if the undiscounted future cash flows from the asset are less than its carrying amount, the asset is written down to fair value, and an impairment loss on an asset held and used is never reversed. IAS 36 uses one step: the carrying amount is compared with the recoverable amount, the higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use (the present value of the future cash flows). If conditions improve, an impairment loss on an asset other than goodwill is reversed, up to the carrying amount the asset would have had without it.

Standards: IAS 16: revaluation and component depreciation

IAS 16 lets a company choose, class by class, between the cost model (cost less accumulated depreciation and impairment, the only model US GAAP permits for property, plant and equipment) and the revaluation model: the asset is carried at fair value at the date of revaluation less later depreciation and impairment, revaluations are made often enough that the carrying amount stays close to fair value, and the entire class is revalued together (paragraphs 29, 31, 34 and 36). An increase goes to other comprehensive income and builds up in equity as a revaluation surplus; a decrease goes to profit or loss, except to the extent it reverses an earlier surplus (paragraphs 39–40). IAS 16 also requires component depreciation: each part with a cost that is significant in relation to the total is depreciated separately (paragraph 43). Aster’s $2,400,000 hotel building might be a $1,800,000 structure lasting 40 years, a $300,000 roof lasting 20 and $300,000 of lifts and air-conditioning lasting 15, giving 45,000 + 15,000 + 20,000 = $80,000 a year, against $60,000 for the whole building over 40 years. Under US GAAP component depreciation is acceptable but not required, and composite depreciation of whole groups is common in industries such as utilities and railroads.

Common mistakes

  • “Training and the first service are part of the machine’s cost.”

    Only costs needed to get the asset to its location and ready to work are capitalised. Training improves the staff, and servicing keeps the machine running; both are expenses. Repairs of damage caused during installation are expenses too: they restore, they don’t create.

  • “Double-declining balance is twice the straight-line charge every year.”

    The rate is twice the straight-line rate, applied to the falling book value, so the charge shrinks each year. And the base is cost, not cost minus residual value; the residual value acts as a floor. Lantern’s van would have no depreciation at all in 2030 under this method.

  • “When the life changes, correct the earlier years.”

    A change in estimate is prospective: spread the remaining book value, less the new residual value, over the remaining life. Earlier years were right on the information then available and are not restated. Restating is for errors, not for new estimates.

  • “A gain on selling equipment is revenue.”

    Revenue comes from the business’s ordinary activities. A gain or loss on disposal is the difference between proceeds and book value; it is reported separately (inside operating income under US GAAP when that subtotal is shown) and never in net revenue.

  • “Forgetting to update depreciation before the sale.”

    If depreciation for the months before the sale is skipped, book value is overstated and the loss is overstated (or the gain understated) by the same amount. Saltire would report a loss of $6,500 instead of $1,250 without the $5,250 for 2026.

  • “Goodwill is never amortised.”

    That is the rule for public companies under US GAAP and under full IFRS. A US private company or not-for-profit may elect to amortise goodwill over ten years or less, and the IFRS for SMEs Accounting Standard amortises it too. Read a private company’s accounting policies before comparing its profit with a public company’s.

In practice

Behind the two lines of property and equipment on a balance sheet sits a fixed-asset register: one record for each asset, with its cost, date in service, method, life, residual value and location, from which each month’s depreciation is calculated automatically. Accountants reconcile it to the general ledger, add new purchases with their full cost (invoices, freight, installation), and remove assets that are sold or scrapped. Each year-end they review lives and residual values, which is where most judgement lies, and auditors test those judgements by comparing them with how long similar assets have actually lasted and what they have sold for.

Outside the accounting department the same numbers drive decisions. A capital budget decides which assets to buy, using the cash flows taught in F04 and F05, not depreciation. Lenders and analysts often add depreciation and amortisation back to operating income, giving EBITDA, because the charges are allocations of past spending; Lantern’s 2026 EBITDA is $42,516, its operating income of $37,716 plus $4,800 of depreciation. They then look at capital spending separately, because a business that stops replacing its equipment will flatter its profit for a while and pay for it later.

For Lantern the next decision is the espresso machine. Its book value will be $7,600 at the end of 2027, a number that says nothing about whether to replace it: that depends on what a new machine costs, what the old one would fetch and how much more coffee a faster machine would let Lantern sell. If Ana does decide on an earlier replacement, the accounts will follow her decision with a revised depreciation charge, not lead it. And if she ever buys another roaster’s business, the price above the fair value of its identifiable assets will appear as goodwill, which Lantern, as a private company, could choose to amortise over ten years.

Summary

  • Capitalise every cost needed to acquire an asset and get it ready for use (price net of discounts, non-refundable taxes, delivery, installation, testing); expense training, servicing and overheads.
  • Depreciation methods share the depreciable amount (cost − residual value) but differ in timing: straight line evenly, double-declining balance at 2 ÷ life on book value with residual value as a floor, units of production by use.
  • Lantern’s 2026 depreciation is $4,800: equipment $2,000 (ten months) and van $2,800 (seven months); property and equipment are $37,200 net.
  • A change in an estimate or method is prospective: (book value − new residual value) ÷ remaining life; earlier years are not restated.
  • On disposal, update depreciation, remove cost and accumulated depreciation, and record a gain or loss equal to proceeds − book value; it is not revenue.
  • Finite-lived intangibles such as patents are amortised over the shorter of legal and useful life; goodwill is not amortised by US public companies or under full IFRS, but US private companies may elect to amortise it over ten years or less.
  • IAS 16 allows revaluation to fair value and requires component depreciation; US GAAP permits only the cost model and doesn’t require components. US GAAP never reverses impairment losses on assets held and used; IAS 36 reverses them except on goodwill.

Terms introduced

Property, plant and equipment
Tangible assets a business uses to produce or supply goods and services, rent to others or administer itself, and expects to use for more than one year, such as land, buildings, vehicles and equipment. Also called fixed assets.
Intangible asset
An identifiable asset without physical substance, such as a patent, copyright, trademark, licence or software.
Capitalise
To record a cost as an asset, because it will bring benefits beyond the current period, rather than as an expense of the period.
Depreciable amount
Cost minus residual value: the total depreciation to be recognised over an asset’s useful life.
Double-declining-balance method
An accelerated depreciation method that charges twice the straight-line rate on the asset’s book value at the start of each year, never taking book value below the residual value.
Units-of-production method
A depreciation method that charges depreciation in proportion to use: (cost − residual value) ÷ total expected units, times the units used in the period.
Change in accounting estimate
A revision of an estimate, such as an asset’s useful life or residual value, because of new information. It is applied from the period of the change onward, without restating earlier periods.
Gain on disposal
The amount by which the proceeds from selling a long-lived asset exceed its book value at the date of sale; a shortfall is a loss on disposal. Neither is revenue.
Patent
An exclusive legal right to make, use and sell an invention. A US utility patent runs for 20 years from the date the application was filed.
Goodwill
The amount by which the price paid for a business exceeds the fair value of its identifiable assets less its liabilities. It is recognised only when a business is acquired.
Impairment
A loss recognised when an asset’s carrying amount can no longer be recovered from its use or sale, measured under US GAAP against fair value and under IFRS against recoverable amount.
Revaluation model
An IFRS option (IAS 16) to carry a whole class of property, plant and equipment at fair value at the revaluation date, less later depreciation and impairment, with increases taken to other comprehensive income. Not permitted under US GAAP.
Component depreciation
Depreciating separately each significant part of an asset that has a different useful life, such as a building’s structure, roof and lifts. Required by IAS 16; acceptable but not required under US GAAP.
