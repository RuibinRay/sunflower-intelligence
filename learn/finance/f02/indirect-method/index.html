The indirect method

Start from net income and reach cash from operating activities: add back noncash expenses, take out gains and losses, adjust for every change in operating working capital, then prepare a full statement from two balance sheets and reconcile it with the direct method.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain why each adjustment in the indirect method is added or subtracted, using the accounting equation.
  • Calculate net cash from operating activities by the indirect method, including noncash expenses, gains and losses, and changes in operating current assets and liabilities.
  • Prepare a complete statement of cash flows from comparative balance sheets, an income statement and notes, including a disposal at a gain and a current portion of long-term debt.
  • Reconcile the indirect method with the direct method, line by line.
  • Identify and correct common errors, such as treating depreciation as a source of cash or reversing the sign of a working-capital change.

Review first

  • The statement of cash flows — The three activities, the classification rules and the direct method come from there.
  • Long-lived assets — Depreciation and the gain or loss on a disposal are the main noncash adjustments.
  • The balance sheet and equity — The method reads the changes between two classified balance sheets, including the current portion of long-term debt.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens an online shop for beans, then launches a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern’s accountant has prepared the direct-method statement, but US GAAP also asks for a reconciliation of net income to net cash from operating activities, in a separate schedule when the direct method is used (ASC 230-10-45-30). Ana has a question of her own. The income statement says Lantern earned $27,387; the statement of cash flows says operations brought in $39,940. Which items explain the $12,553 between them?

The indirect method answers Ana’s question directly. It starts from net income and adjusts it, item by item, until it reaches net cash from operating activities, so each adjustment names one reason why profit and operating cash differ. Only the operating section changes: the investing and financing sections are the same whichever method is used. Most companies present their operating cash flows this way.

The adjustments come in three kinds.

  1. Noncash expenses. Depreciation, amortisation and similar charges reduce net income but use no cash in the period. Each is an add-back: it is added to net income.
  2. Gains and losses on investing and financing transactions. When equipment is sold, the whole cash proceeds are an investing inflow. The gain is also in net income, so it is subtracted; a loss is added back.
  3. Changes in operating working capital. Each operating current asset other than cash, and each operating current liability, changes during the year. Each change means that cash and net income moved by different amounts, and the adjustment corrects for it.

Why the signs are what they are

Students often try to memorise the signs. It is quicker to derive them from the accounting equation you met in F01. Split assets into cash and everything else, and move everything else to the other side:

Cash=Liabilities+Equity−Noncash assets\text{Cash} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity} - \text{Noncash assets}Cash=Liabilities+Equity−Noncash assets
ΔCash=ΔLiabilities+ΔEquity−ΔNoncash assets\Delta\text{Cash} = \Delta\text{Liabilities} + \Delta\text{Equity} - \Delta\text{Noncash assets}ΔCash=ΔLiabilities+ΔEquity−ΔNoncash assets

Read the second line as a rule. An increase in a noncash asset must have been paid for with cash unless a liability or equity grew to fund it, so it subtracts from cash. An increase in a liability means the business kept cash it would otherwise have paid out, or received cash it hasn’t yet earned, so it adds. Equity grows by net income, the starting point. The indirect method applies this identity to the operating accounts; the noncurrent assets go to investing, and debt, stock and dividends to financing.

Key idea: Depreciation is not a source of cash

Adding back depreciation doesn’t create cash; it removes an expense that used no cash this period. The cash for Lantern’s equipment and van left as investing outflows when they were bought. Had Lantern’s depreciation been $4,800 higher, net income would have been $4,800 lower and the add-back $4,800 higher, leaving operating cash flow unchanged (before any effect on income tax).

In depth

The method, step by step

  1. Start with net income from the income statement.
  2. Add back noncash expenses: depreciation, amortisation and impairment losses.
  3. Subtract gains and add back losses on selling long-term assets or settling debt; the cash from those transactions belongs to investing or financing.
  4. Adjust for the change in each operating current asset other than cash and each operating current liability: subtract increases in assets and decreases in liabilities; add decreases in assets and increases in liabilities.
  5. Leave out current items that belong to other activities: the current portion of long-term debt, short-term bank loans and dividends payable (financing), and short-term investments and loans made to others (investing).
  6. The total is net cash from operating activities. Prepare the investing and financing sections from the changes in the noncurrent accounts and the financing items, using the notes to find what the balances hide.

Accounts payable rose $9,210: $8,400 for beans, $520 for supplies and the $290 December utility bill.

Two kinds of current liability are left out on purpose. The $5,000 current portion of the van note arose by reclassifying long-term debt (adjustment e8): no cash moved, and repaying it will be a financing outflow. Lantern has no dividends payable, but a dividend declared and not yet paid would be a financing item too. Putting either in the reconciliation would mix financing into operating cash flow.

Standards: Presenting the reconciliation

US GAAP requires the reconciliation of net income to net cash from operating activities whichever method is used (ASC 230-10-45-29). With the direct method it goes in a separate schedule (45-30); with the indirect method it may be shown within the statement or in a separate schedule, with the reconciling items clearly identified (45-31 and 45-32). A company that uses the indirect method must also disclose the interest and income taxes it paid (ASC 230-10-50-2). Under IAS 7 the indirect method starts from profit or loss today; the IFRS 18 amendments, effective for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027, make the starting point operating profit or loss instead.

A later year: opening balances, a disposal and the current portion of debt

From the second year on, every adjustment is a change between two balance sheets. The investing and financing sections also need some reconstruction, because a balance sheet shows only the net effect of purchases, disposals, borrowing and repayments. The tool is a roll-forward of each account: opening balance plus additions minus reductions equals closing balance. Know three of the four, and you can find the fourth.

  • Equipment: opening cost + purchases − cost of assets sold = closing cost.
  • Accumulated depreciation: opening + depreciation expense − accumulated depreciation on assets sold = closing.
  • Debt: opening total (current portion + noncurrent) + new borrowing − repayments = closing total. Moving next year’s instalment from noncurrent to current changes neither total and involves no cash.
  • Retained earnings and dividends: opening + net income − dividends declared = closing; dividends paid = dividends declared − the increase in dividends payable.

Gains and losses complete the picture. When a business sells equipment, the cash proceeds equal its book value plus any gain, or minus any loss, and the whole amount is an investing inflow. The gain is also inside net income, so the indirect method subtracts it; otherwise it would be counted twice, once in operating and once in investing. A loss is added back for the same reason. The challenging example below works through a full year for a business with all of these features.

One more source of information matters: the notes. They tell you about transactions the balances blur, such as equipment bought partly with a note to the seller. Under ASC 230-10-45-13(c) that note is a financing transaction with no cash, disclosed as a noncash activity, and the principal paid on it later is a financing outflow. Without the note you would see only the net increase in equipment and in debt, and might report both as cash flows.

Checking a reconciliation

A statement of cash flows prepared from two balance sheets has a built-in test: every change in every balance sheet account must be used exactly once. Each change goes to one of four places: the operating section (through net income or a working-capital line), the investing section, the financing section, or the noncash disclosure. An account left out, or used twice, shows up as a closing cash figure that disagrees with the balance sheet.

Preparers often lay this out as a worksheet: list the balance sheet accounts with their opening and closing balances, explain each change, and tick it off. For Blackthorn, the $26,000 increase in equipment is explained by the $50,000 press less the $24,000 press sold; the $50,000 splits into $30,000 of investing cash and a $20,000 noncash note. The $7,000 increase in accumulated depreciation is $22,000 of depreciation (operating, added back) less the $15,000 removed on the sale. Debt rose $2,000 on the current line and $8,000 on the noncurrent line, and neither change is a cash flow by itself: total debt rose $10,000, made up of the $20,000 note (noncash) less the $10,000 repaid (financing).

A second test compares the two methods. If a business presents the direct method, its operating total must equal the reconciliation’s; if it presents the indirect method, deriving the main direct-method lines (cash from customers, cash paid to suppliers) is a quick check on the working-capital lines. Lantern’s accountant does both: $39,940 by each route.

Common mistakes

  • “Depreciation is a source of cash.”

    Depreciation is added back because it reduced net income without using cash this period; the add-back undoes the expense and creates no cash. The cash left when the asset was bought, as an investing outflow. Recording more depreciation can’t raise operating cash flow, apart from any effect on the tax bill.

  • “Receivables went up, so add the increase.”

    An increase in a current asset is subtracted: revenue was recorded but the cash hasn’t arrived. When a sign is in doubt, use the identity ΔCash=ΔLiabilities+ΔEquity−ΔNoncash assets\Delta\text{Cash} = \Delta\text{Liabilities} + \Delta\text{Equity} - \Delta\text{Noncash assets}ΔCash=ΔLiabilities+ΔEquity−ΔNoncash assets: assets other than cash enter with a minus sign, liabilities with a plus.

  • “The current portion of long-term debt is a current liability, so it belongs in the working-capital adjustments.”

    It is debt, and borrowing and repaying are financing; moving an instalment from noncurrent to current involves no cash at all. Dividends payable and short-term bank loans are financing items too. Lantern’s $5,000 current portion is left out of its reconciliation.

  • “Keep the gain in net income and show the proceeds in investing as well.”

    That counts the gain twice. Subtract the gain (or add back a loss) in the operating section and show the full proceeds in investing. Blackthorn subtracts its $2,500 gain and reports $11,500 of proceeds.

  • “Add back bad debt expense and the inventory write-down too.”

    Not when, as in Lantern’s reconciliation, receivables are taken net of the allowance and inventory after the write-down: both charges are already inside those changes, and adding them back counts them twice. If you start from gross receivables instead, the matching adjustment is the increase in the allowance (bad debt expense less write-offs, 1,404−820=5841{,}404 - 820 = 5841,404−820=584), not the whole expense; if inventory is measured before a write-down, add the write-down back.

  • “The changes are the closing balances.”

    Only when every opening balance is zero, as in Lantern’s first year. Blackthorn’s receivables rose from $19,800 to $24,600, so the adjustment is −$4,800, not −$24,600.

In practice

Explaining a fall in cash. Blackthorn’s owner asks why cash fell by $1,450 in 2027 when the business earned $23,250. The controller answers from the statement in the challenging example. Operations generated $38,050, more than net income because depreciation is a large noncash expense for a printer. The new press used $30,000 of cash, offset by $11,500 from selling the old one, the bank took $10,000 and dividends $11,000. The business is not losing money; it is investing and repaying debt slightly faster than operations replace the cash.

Forecasting next year. The same structure turns a profit forecast into a cash forecast. For 2028 the controller expects net income of about $26,000 and depreciation of $24,000, and expects receivables to grow by $5,000 with sales, so forecast operating cash flow is about 26,000+24,000−5,000=45,00026{,}000 + 24{,}000 - 5{,}000 = 45{,}00026,000+24,000−5,000=45,000. Against it stand the $12,000 of debt due in 2028 (the current portion), $15,000 of planned equipment and the dividends. Holding dividends at $12,000 leaves 45,000−12,000−15,000−12,000=6,00045{,}000 - 12{,}000 - 15{,}000 - 12{,}000 = 6{,}00045,000−12,000−15,000−12,000=6,000 to rebuild cash; raising them to $20,000 would mean another fall. The controller recommends keeping the dividend at $12,000 for another year.

Reading someone else’s reconciliation. Lenders and analysts read the indirect method for what it reveals about earnings. Working-capital adjustments that keep going the same way, such as receivables rising faster than sales year after year, suggest that profit is not turning into cash. A large operating cash flow that comes from stretching payables will reverse when suppliers insist on being paid, while one that comes from depreciation on a stable asset base is more likely to recur. The ratios that measure these patterns belong to F03; the reconciliation is where the evidence sits.

Summary

  • The indirect method starts from net income and adds or subtracts the items that make it differ from operating cash: noncash expenses, gains and losses on investing and financing transactions, and changes in operating working capital.
  • The signs follow from ΔCash = ΔLiabilities + ΔEquity − ΔNoncash assets: subtract increases in operating current assets, add increases in operating current liabilities, and the reverse for decreases.
  • Lantern’s first year: $27,387 of net income, $4,800 of depreciation and $7,753 of net working-capital changes give $39,940; opening balances were zero, so each change is the closing balance.
  • The direct and indirect methods are the same arithmetic grouped differently; both give Lantern $39,940, and US GAAP requires the reconciliation whichever is presented.
  • Leave financing and investing items out of the working-capital adjustments: the current portion of long-term debt, dividends payable, short-term loans and investments.
  • In a later year, roll each noncurrent account forward to find purchases, disposals, borrowing, repayments and dividends; subtract a gain, add back a loss, and report the full proceeds in investing.
  • Depreciation is not a source of cash, and profit is not cash: a growing business can be profitable and still run short of cash.

Terms introduced

Noncash expense
An expense that reduces net income without any cash payment in the period, such as depreciation, amortisation or an impairment loss. The indirect method adds it back.
Add-back
An amount added to net income in the indirect method because it reduced net income without reducing cash in the period, such as depreciation or a loss on the sale of equipment.
Operating working capital
The current assets other than cash and the current liabilities that arise from day-to-day operations: receivables, inventory, prepayments, accounts payable, accrued expenses, unearned revenue and income taxes payable. Current items that belong to investing or financing, such as short-term investments, the current portion of long-term debt and dividends payable, are excluded.
