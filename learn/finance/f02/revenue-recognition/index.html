Revenue recognition

Decide when revenue is earned and how much to record by working through the five steps of ASC 606 and IFRS 15: deposits and prepaid subscriptions, bundles, refunds, discounts and the question of gross or net. Then reconcile Lantern’s online-shop order values with the revenue in its books.

About 85 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Apply the five steps of ASC 606 and IFRS 15 to a contract, using the control criteria to decide whether revenue is recognised over time or at a point in time.
  • Calculate the share of a transaction price allocated to each performance obligation, in proportion to stand-alone selling prices.
  • Record contract liabilities (unearned revenue), receivables, refunds and sales discounts, and present contra-revenue accounts correctly.
  • Distinguish a principal from an agent and explain how the answer changes reported revenue.
  • Reconcile Lantern’s online-shop order values with the revenue, unearned revenue and cash in its books.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

January 2027. Lantern’s accountant is preparing the statements for its first financial year, the ten months from 1 March to 31 December 2026. Lantern earned money in three ways: it catered 76 events, ran 14 barista workshops and sold bags of beans in its online shop. B02 and B01 together put the shop’s order values at $111,024.55, and the bank records show $191,220 received from customers. The first line of the income statement needs a third figure: how much revenue Lantern earned in those ten months.

In F01 you met the revenue recognition principle: revenue is recognised when, or as, a business satisfies a performance obligation by transferring control of a good or service to a customer, at the amount it expects to be entitled to. In one month of catering the principle was easy to apply. Over a year, with deposits, prepaid subscriptions, unpaid invoices, refunds and bundled sales, it becomes the source of most of the judgement in an income statement. Revenue is the figure readers look at first, and every subtotal beneath it inherits any error in it.

US GAAP (Accounting Standards Codification Topic 606) and IFRS (IFRS 15) apply the principle through the same five steps, summarised in the figure. The first four settle the contract and the amount; only the fifth decides the timing.

Cash and revenue rarely move on the same day, and the gap between them leaves an asset or a liability on the balance sheet. If the customer pays first, the business owes the goods or service: a contract liability, which Lantern’s chart of accounts calls unearned revenue and many companies call deferred revenue. If the business performs first and the customer has not yet paid, it holds a receivable (or a contract asset, if its right to payment still depends on further performance). At 31 December Lantern had both: $6,100 of unearned revenue and $7,380 of accounts receivable.

Key idea: Four things that are not revenue events

Signing a contract, sending an invoice, receiving a deposit and receiving full payment are not, on their own, reasons to recognise revenue. Revenue follows the transfer of control: the event catered, the workshop held, the bag shipped. The other events decide only the amount, or where the money sits while it waits.

Each of Lantern’s streams fits the model differently. A catered event or a workshop is delivered on one day. A bag of beans is a good, recognised when it is shipped. A prepaid subscription is a promise of future bags, so its cash arrives before any revenue. The rest of the lesson works through the steps in turn, deals with refunds, discounts and the question of whose sale it is, and ends by reconciling the shop’s order values with the books.

In depth

Step 1: the contract

A contract is an agreement that creates enforceable rights and obligations; it can be written, spoken or implied by a business’s customary practice. IFRS 15 (paragraph 9) lists five conditions before the model applies, and ASC 606 has a parallel list:

  • the parties have approved the contract and are committed to performing;
  • each party’s rights to the goods or services can be identified;
  • the payment terms can be identified;
  • the contract has commercial substance (it is expected to change the business’s future cash flows);
  • it is probable that the business will collect the amount it will be entitled to.

Lantern’s contracts are simple: a catering booking confirmed by email at an agreed price, a workshop registration, an order placed at the shop’s checkout. A customer who later fails to pay, as Quayline Events did, doesn’t mean there was no contract. The loss is a bad debt, which the next module covers in Receivables and payables.

Step 2: the performance obligations

A performance obligation is a promise to transfer a distinct good or service. IFRS 15 (paragraph 27) treats a promise as distinct when two conditions hold: the customer can benefit from it on its own or with resources readily available to it, and the promise is separately identifiable from the other promises in the contract. A catered lunch includes food, coffee and the staff who serve them, but the client is buying one integrated service, so it is one obligation. A sound system sold with installation by the seller’s technician and a support plan is usually three obligations, because a customer could buy each separately and none significantly changes the others.

Step 3: the transaction price

The transaction price is the amount the business expects to be entitled to in exchange for the promised goods or services. It excludes amounts collected on behalf of third parties, such as some sales taxes (IFRS 15 paragraph 47). When the amount can change, because of discounts, rebates, refunds, credits, price concessions, incentives, bonuses or penalties, the business has variable consideration (ASC 606-10-32-6; IFRS 15 paragraph 51). It estimates the amount, but includes it only to the extent that it is probable that a significant reversal of the cumulative revenue recognised won’t occur later (ASC 606-10-32-11). Lantern’s prices are fixed: an agreed price for each event, $125 a workshop seat and a list price for each bag (its refunds for damaged bags, discussed below, are the exception).

Step 4: allocating the price

When a contract holds more than one performance obligation, the transaction price is divided in proportion to their stand-alone selling prices: what the business would charge for each promise sold separately (IFRS 15 paragraphs 74 and 77). A list price is evidence of a stand-alone selling price but isn’t automatically one. When no observable price exists, the business estimates it, for example by an adjusted market assessment or expected cost plus a margin; a residual approach is allowed only in limited circumstances (paragraph 79). A discount for buying the bundle is normally shared among all the obligations in proportion.

Step 5: satisfying the obligation

An obligation is satisfied when the customer obtains control of the good or service: the ability to direct its use and obtain substantially all of its remaining benefits (IFRS 15 paragraph 33). Revenue is recognised over time if any one of three criteria is met (ASC 606-10-25-27; IFRS 15 paragraph 35):

  1. the customer receives and consumes the benefits as the business performs (cleaning, a gym membership, a monitoring service);
  2. the business’s work creates or enhances an asset the customer controls as it is created (an extension built on the customer’s land);
  3. the work creates an asset with no alternative use to the business, and the business has an enforceable right to payment for the work done to date (a machine built to one customer’s design).

Otherwise the obligation is satisfied at a point in time, and the business looks for indicators that control has passed: a present right to payment, the customer’s legal title, physical possession, the significant risks and rewards of ownership, and the customer’s acceptance (ASC 606-10-25-30; IFRS 15 paragraph 38). No single indicator decides; they are weighed together.

F01 described a catered event as delivered at a point in time, a fair shorthand: strictly, it meets the first over-time criterion, because the client consumes the service as Lantern performs it. The performance lasts a few hours on one day, so either way all its revenue falls on the event date, and the same is true of a workshop.

A bag of beans is a good. Lantern refunds bags damaged in transit, so it keeps the risk of loss until the parcel arrives, and control strictly passes on delivery. Its accountant nevertheless recognises shop sales when orders ship: parcels arrive within days, so only orders in transit on 31 December are affected, and they were judged below materiality. With material sales in transit at a year end, revenue would wait for delivery.

Contract liabilities and receivables

A contract liability is a business’s obligation to transfer goods or services to a customer from whom it has received payment, or from whom payment is already due (IFRS 15 paragraph 106; ASC 606 uses the same term). A receivable is a right to payment that is unconditional: only the passage of time stands between the business and the cash (paragraph 108). If the business has performed but its right to payment still depends on something other than time, such as finishing another part of the contract, it holds a contract asset rather than a receivable (IFRS 15 paragraph 107; ASC 606-10-45-3). Lantern’s catering invoices of $59,920 created receivables when the events took place; by 31 December it had collected $51,720 and written off $820, leaving $7,380.

In early 2027 the $6,100 becomes revenue as Lantern caters the two events, runs the workshop and ships the subscription bags: each delivery debits Unearned Revenue and credits a revenue account. Had Lantern recorded the receipts as revenue in 2026, its 2026 revenue would be overstated by $6,100 and its balance sheet would hide obligations it still had to perform.

Note: Unearned revenue is not cash set aside

The $6,100 measures goods and services Lantern owes, not money in a separate account. The cash went into Lantern’s bank balance when it arrived; the liability tells a reader that part of that balance is already promised to customers, in coffee and service if Lantern performs, in refunds if it can’t.

Refunds, returns and allowances

When a customer returns goods or is given a price reduction, the business doesn’t erase the original sale. It debits a contra-revenue account, Sales Returns and Allowances, which has a normal debit balance and is deducted from gross revenue on the income statement. Keeping the reduction in its own account shows managers how much selling effort is undone by returns. Lantern refunded $1,025 to customers whose bags were damaged in transit. The damaged beans weren’t resold, so nothing went back into inventory:

When returned goods can be resold, a second entry puts them back into inventory at cost and reduces cost of goods sold, as the next lesson shows. A business that sells with a right of return and expects returns at the year end estimates them instead of waiting: it recognises revenue only for the goods it expects customers to keep, a refund liability for the amount it expects to pay back, and an asset, with a matching reduction of cost of sales, for its right to recover the goods (ASC 606-10-55-23; IFRS 15 paragraph B21). Lantern’s practice of refunding damaged bags is variable consideration in principle, but refunds were under 1% of shop sales and are paid within days, so those still to come at 31 December for late-December shipments were immaterial: its accountant records refunds when paid and recognises no refund liability. A business whose expected refunds at the year end are material must estimate them.

A sales discount rewards prompt payment. Terms of “2/10, n/30” offer 2% off if the customer pays within 10 days, with the full amount due in 30. Introductory texts, OpenStax among them, record the discount in a contra-revenue account, Sales Discounts, when the customer takes it. Under ASC 606 and IFRS 15 a prompt-payment discount is variable consideration, so a seller that expects most customers to take it estimates the discount when it recognises the sale. Lantern’s shop customers pay by card at checkout, so its Sales Discounts account has no balance in 2026; it would be used if Lantern offered credit terms to wholesale customers.

Gross or net: principal or agent

When another party is involved in supplying the customer, the business must decide whether it promised to provide the good or service itself, as a principal, or to arrange for the other party to provide it, as an agent. A principal recognises revenue at the gross amount the customer pays; an agent recognises only its fee or commission (IFRS 15 paragraphs B35B and B36). The test is whether the business controls the good or service before it reaches the customer. Three indicators help (paragraph B37): the business is primarily responsible for fulfilling the promise, it bears inventory risk, and it has discretion in setting the price.

Lantern buys roasted bags from Kilnworth Roasting, holds them in stock, sets their prices and answers customers’ complaints, so it is the principal and reports the full $111,025 as revenue. The card processor and the shop platform are Lantern’s suppliers, not partners in the sale: their $4,010 of fees is an operating expense, not a deduction from revenue. The answer matters for comparisons. A marketplace selling $1 million of other firms’ goods for a 10% commission reports $100,000 of revenue; a retailer selling the same goods as principal reports $1 million and the cost of the goods as well.

Standards: One model, two codifications

ASC 606 and IFRS 15 were developed jointly by the FASB and the IASB, and the five steps, the control criteria and the principal-versus-agent indicators are the same in both, which is why this lesson cites them side by side. F01’s accrual lesson gives their effective dates. Some detailed requirements differ, and courses after F02 deal with them; nothing in Lantern’s year depends on a difference.

Common mistakes

  • “Subscription payments are shop sales.”

    A prepaid subscription pays for bags not yet shipped, so it is a contract liability until each bag goes out. Lantern’s $2,960 of prepaid subscriptions is in Unearned Revenue, not in Online Sales Revenue; counting it as revenue would overstate 2026 sales.

  • “Refunds are an operating expense.”

    A refund undoes part of a sale, so it reduces revenue through a contra-revenue account. Lantern’s $1,025 appears between gross and net revenue. Treating it as an expense would leave net revenue $1,025 too high and distort the gross margin, which is calculated on net revenue.

  • “A bundle discount belongs to the item with the highest margin” (or “to the service”).

    The transaction price is allocated in proportion to stand-alone selling prices, so a bundle discount is shared by every obligation. Loading it onto one item moves revenue between periods, because the items are delivered at different times.

  • “Whoever collects the customer’s money reports the full price as revenue.”

    Collecting the cash doesn’t make a business the principal. The test is control of the good or service before transfer: who is responsible for delivering it, who bears inventory risk, who sets the price. An agent reports only its commission, even if it handles every payment.

  • “The analytics dashboard’s order values are revenue.”

    Order values measure orders placed. Revenue measures obligations satisfied: an order not yet shipped isn’t revenue, a refund reduces it and a prepayment isn’t revenue yet. Lantern’s figures happen to be close ($111,024.55 against $111,025 gross), but only a reconciliation shows why.

In practice

A subscription plan. Ana is considering an annual coffee subscription for 2027: twelve monthly bags for $180, paid at sign-up. Suppose 200 customers join on 1 April. The shop collects 200×180=36,000200 \times 180 = 36{,}000200×180=36,000 dollars that day, but each bag shipped earns only 180÷12=15180 \div 12 = 15180÷12=15 dollars of revenue, so by 31 December, after nine shipments, revenue is 200×9×15=27,000200 \times 9 \times 15 = 27{,}000200×9×15=27,000 and $9,000 is still owed in coffee. The plan improves Lantern’s cash immediately and its revenue only gradually, and the contract liability is the honest record of the gap between the two.

An auditor’s cut-off test. Revenue errors cluster at period ends, where a sale can slip into the wrong year. An auditor testing Lantern’s 2026 revenue would select shipments and events on either side of 31 December and check, against shipping records and event confirmations, that each landed in the right year. The auditor would also confirm that the two January events and the 9 January workshop sit in Unearned Revenue rather than in 2026 revenue, and that refunds paid in late December were deducted from 2026 revenue. The five steps tell the auditor what evidence to ask for: proof of the contract, the price and, above all, the date control passed.

Reading revenue across business models. An analyst comparing an online retailer with an online marketplace starts by asking how each reports. The retailer, as principal, reports the full price of what it sells; the marketplace, as agent, reports its commissions. A marketplace with smaller revenue may handle far more customer spending, and its gross margin will look much higher because it has almost no cost of goods sold. Comparing the two on revenue alone, or on gross margin alone, misleads; F03 develops the ratios that make such comparisons work.

Summary

  • ASC 606 and IFRS 15 recognise revenue through five steps: identify the contract, identify the performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate it by stand-alone selling prices, and recognise revenue when or as each obligation is satisfied.
  • An obligation is satisfied over time if the customer consumes the benefit as the business performs, controls the asset as it is built, or the asset has no alternative use and the business has a right to payment to date; otherwise at a point in time, judged by indicators such as title, possession and acceptance.
  • Cash received before performance is a contract liability (unearned revenue); performance before payment creates a receivable. Lantern ended 2026 with $6,100 of unearned revenue: $1,640 of event deposits, $1,500 of workshop fees and $2,960 of subscriptions.
  • Returns, allowances and sales discounts reduce revenue through contra-revenue accounts; expected returns are estimated as a refund liability. Lantern’s gross revenue of $194,345 less $1,025 of refunds gives net revenue of $193,320.
  • A principal reports the gross amount the customer pays; an agent reports its fee or commission. Lantern is the principal for its shop’s beans, so card and platform fees are expenses.
  • Analytics order values ($111,024.55) reconcile to the books: Online Sales Revenue $111,025, net online sales $110,000, and $2,960 of subscriptions still owed.

Terms introduced

Contract liability
A business’s obligation to transfer goods or services to a customer from whom it has received payment, or from whom payment is due. In the ledger it is usually called unearned or deferred revenue.
Contract asset
A business’s right to payment for goods or services it has already transferred, when that right depends on something other than the passage of time, such as its own further performance. When only time stands in the way, the right is a receivable instead.
Transaction price
The amount a business expects to be entitled to in exchange for the goods or services promised in a contract, excluding amounts collected for third parties such as some sales taxes.
Variable consideration
A part of the transaction price that can change, for example because of discounts, rebates, refunds, price concessions, bonuses or penalties. It is estimated and included only to the extent that a significant reversal of revenue is not expected.
Stand-alone selling price
The price at which a business would sell a promised good or service separately to a customer. Used to allocate a contract’s transaction price among its performance obligations.
Control
In revenue recognition, the customer’s ability to direct the use of a good or service and obtain substantially all of its remaining benefits. Revenue is recognised when control transfers.
Contra-revenue account
An account with a normal debit balance that reduces revenue on the income statement, such as Sales Returns and Allowances or Sales Discounts.
Sales Returns and Allowances
A contra-revenue account for refunds and credits given to customers who return goods or accept a price reduction for unsatisfactory goods.
Sales discount
A reduction of the amount owed that a seller offers for prompt payment, for example 2% off if the invoice is paid within 10 days (2/10, n/30). Recorded in a contra-revenue account.
Refund liability
A liability for the amount a business expects to refund to customers, for example for goods it expects to be returned. Revenue is not recognised for that amount.
Principal (in a sale)
In revenue recognition, a business that controls a good or service before it is transferred to the customer. It recognises revenue at the gross amount the customer pays.
Agent (in a sale)
In revenue recognition, a business that arranges for another party to provide a good or service. It recognises revenue at the amount of its fee or commission.
Net revenue
Gross revenue less sales returns and allowances and sales discounts; also called net sales. The first total on a multi-step income statement.
