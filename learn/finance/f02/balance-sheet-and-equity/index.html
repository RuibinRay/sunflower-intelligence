The balance sheet and equity

Put Lantern’s year-end balances into a classified balance sheet: decide what is current, split a loan into its current and noncurrent parts, and calculate working capital and the current ratio. Then look inside equity, at par value, additional paid-in capital, dividends and the statement of changes in equity.

About 90 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Classify assets and liabilities as current or noncurrent under US GAAP and IFRS, including the current portion of long-term debt.
  • Prepare a classified balance sheet in report form, with contra accounts shown on its face.
  • Calculate working capital and the current ratio, and the effect on them of debt classification and of when a dividend is paid.
  • Record the issue of par-value and no-par stock and the declaration and payment of a cash dividend.
  • Prepare a statement of changes in equity and reconcile it with the balance sheet.

Review first

  • Accounts, terminology and classifications — It introduced current and noncurrent items and the operating cycle.

  • Capital and liquidity — Working capital and the current ratio are defined there.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

In January Ana sends Lantern’s first annual statements to Coastline Bank and to the finance company that lent $20,000 for the van. Both lenders turn first to the balance sheet at 31 December 2026, and both ask the same questions. What must Lantern pay within the next year, and what will it have to pay it with? How much of the business is financed by its owner, and how much of that is profit Ana has left in the business rather than paid out?

The balance sheet answers those questions only if it is organised. A classified balance sheet groups assets and liabilities into current and noncurrent. A current asset is cash or an asset expected to be turned into cash, sold or used up within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer; a current liability is one expected to be settled within the same horizon. Everything else is a noncurrent asset or a noncurrent liability. The split lets a reader set what is due soon against what will be available soon, which is the first test of liquidity.

Within each group, US companies list assets in order of liquidity: cash first, then receivables, inventory and prepayments, then long-lived assets. Liabilities follow in roughly the order they fall due, then equity. Long-term loans need care: most are repaid in instalments, so the part due within the next year is a current liability and the rest noncurrent, even though it is one loan. That part is the current portion of long-term debt, and Lantern’s van note is the example.

The other half of the balance sheet is equity, the owners’ claim. It has two sources, and the balance sheet keeps them apart. Contributed capital is what owners paid in for their shares; retained earnings is the profit the business has earned and kept, net income since it began less every dividend. How contributed capital is split into par value and additional paid-in capital, how dividends are recorded, and how the statement of changes in equity ties the year together are the second half of this lesson.

Key idea: Classification changes ratios, not totals

Moving the van’s next instalment from noncurrent to current liabilities leaves total liabilities and total assets unchanged. It changes working capital and the current ratio, and those are the numbers lenders write into their agreements. Classifying correctly is therefore not a matter of tidiness.

In depth

Current or noncurrent: the rules

F01 introduced the test: an item is current if it will be realised, sold, used up or settled within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer. Three details matter when a balance sheet is prepared. Under US GAAP the operating cycle replaces the year only when it is longer than twelve months, and when there is no clearly defined cycle the one-year rule applies (ASC 210-10-45-3); Lantern, like most businesses, uses one year. Under IFRS, trade payables and accrued operating costs are current even if some are due after more than twelve months, because they belong to the operating cycle (IAS 1, paragraph 70). And a liability is current whenever the entity has no right, at the end of the reporting period, to defer its settlement for at least twelve months (IAS 1, paragraph 69(d), as amended from 2024; IFRS 18, which replaces IAS 1 for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027, carries these requirements forward unchanged).

Some businesses, such as banks, have no clearly identifiable operating cycle, and IAS 1 lets them present assets and liabilities in order of liquidity without a current and noncurrent split when that is more relevant (paragraph 60). IAS 1 also doesn’t prescribe the order or format of the statement (paragraph 57), so some IFRS reporters list noncurrent assets first and show equity before liabilities. The facts are the same; only the layout differs.

Lantern’s classified balance sheet

Current assets of $56,476 are dominated by cash ($42,940). Receivables are shown at the gross $7,380 with the allowance of $584 deducted on the face of the statement, so the reader sees both what customers owe and what Lantern expects to collect. Prepaid insurance of $300 is the cover for January and February 2027, the last two months of the policy. Property and equipment appear at cost less accumulated depreciation, $12,000 − $2,000 and $30,000 − $2,800, for a net $37,200. Total assets are $93,676.

On the other side, current liabilities of $26,289 are the six items built up in the receivables and payables lesson. Noncurrent liabilities of $25,000 are notes payable: Coastline’s $10,000, due in one sum on 1 March 2028, fourteen months after the balance sheet date, and the $15,000 of the van note due after 31 December 2027. Equity is $42,387: $20,000 of common stock and $22,387 of retained earnings. Liabilities plus equity, 51,289+42,38751{,}289 + 42{,}38751,289+42,387, equal total assets of $93,676. The form shown, with assets above and liabilities and equity below, is the report form; the account form sets assets on the left and liabilities and equity on the right, like the two sides of an account.

The current portion of long-term debt

Lantern’s van note was $20,000, repayable in four yearly instalments of $5,000 from 1 June 2027. At 31 December 2026 the first instalment is due within twelve months and will be paid from current assets, so it is a current liability; the other $15,000 is not due until 2028 and later. The ledger keeps the note in one account, so at the year end the accountant moves the current part with a reclassification entry, adjustment e8. The entry changes no total and involves no cash: it moves $5,000 from one liability line to another.

The split is redone at every balance sheet date. By 31 December 2027 Lantern will have paid the first instalment, and two amounts will be due within the following year: the van’s second instalment on 1 June 2028 and the whole of Coastline’s note on 1 March 2028. The current portion will rise to 10,000+5,000=15,00010{,}000 + 5{,}000 = 15{,}00010,000+5,000=15,000 and only $10,000 of van instalments, those due in 2029 and 2030, will stay noncurrent. A loan that is comfortably long-term this year can become a large current liability next year just because time passes, which is why lenders and borrowers plan refinancing well before that happens.

Two complications are left to intermediate accounting. If a borrower breaches a loan condition (a covenant) on or before the balance sheet date and the loan becomes repayable on demand, IFRS classifies the whole loan as current even if the lender agrees after the year end not to demand repayment (IAS 1, paragraph 74). And a short-term loan the company intends to refinance on long-term terms may in some circumstances be shown as noncurrent; US GAAP (ASC 470-10) and IFRS draw that line in different places.

Working capital and the current ratio

Two measures from F00 summarise the current section. Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities; the current ratio divides one by the other. For Lantern, working capital is 56,476−26,289=30,18756{,}476 - 26{,}289 = 30{,}18756,476−26,289=30,187 and the current ratio is 56,476÷26,289≈2.1556{,}476 \div 26{,}289 \approx 2.1556,476÷26,289≈2.15: about $2.15 of current assets for every dollar due within the year. Had the accountant forgotten e8, current liabilities would have been $21,289, working capital $35,187 and the ratio 2.65, a flattering picture of the same business.

The current ratio treats every current asset as equally able to pay bills, which is rarely true. Lantern’s cash alone, $42,940, exceeds all its current liabilities, while $6,100 of those liabilities will be settled by delivering events and coffee, not by paying cash. Judging whether 2.15 is comfortable for a coffee business, and comparing it with others, is the work of F03 Financial Statement Analysis; here the point is that the ratio is only as good as the classification behind it.

Contributed capital: par value and additional paid-in capital

A corporation’s charter states how many shares it may issue, its authorised shares, and for many companies a par value for each: a nominal amount, often a cent or less, fixed by the charter and given meaning by state corporate law rather than by accounting standards. In Delaware, for example, shares with a par value may not be issued for less than par (Delaware General Corporation Law, section 153), and the capital a corporation records for them must be at least their total par value (section 154). Because par is usually tiny, it says nothing about what a share is worth.

When shares are issued, US practice splits the proceeds: Common Stock is credited with the par value of the shares issued, and the rest goes to additional paid-in capital (APIC). No-par stock is credited in full to Common Stock, unless the board assigns it a stated value, which is then treated like par. Lantern’s shares have no par value, so all of Ana’s $20,000 went to Common Stock and its Additional Paid-in Capital account is empty. Had the same $20,000 bought 2,000 shares with a $1 par value, the entry would have split it: $2,000 to Common Stock and $18,000 to APIC. Total contributed capital is the same either way.

Shares are sometimes issued for something other than cash, such as equipment or professional services. The issue is then recorded at the fair value of the shares or of what is received, whichever can be measured more reliably, and split between par and APIC in the usual way.

The balance sheet or its notes give, for each class of stock, the par or stated value and the numbers of shares authorised, issued and outstanding. Shares are issued when first sold. Some may later be bought back and held as treasury stock, shown as a deduction from equity; issued shares not held in treasury are the outstanding shares, the ones that vote and receive dividends. Some companies also issue preferred stock, which ranks ahead of common stock for dividends; F04 meets it as a source of fixed payments. Companies reporting under IFRS, and UK companies, usually call common stock share capital and APIC share premium.

Retained earnings and dividends

A cash dividend involves three dates. On the declaration date the board votes the dividend, and it becomes a legal liability: debit Dividends (or Retained Earnings directly; Lantern uses a Dividends account, which is closed to Retained Earnings), credit dividends payable. The date of record fixes who will be paid, the shareholders on the register that day; no entry is made. On the payment date the company pays: debit Dividends Payable, credit Cash. Retained earnings fall when the dividend is declared, not when it is paid.

Lantern’s two dividends were declared and paid on the same day, $300 on 30 March and $4,700 on 15 December, so no dividend was owed at the year end. Suppose instead that the December dividend had been declared on 15 December and paid on 8 January 2027. Retained earnings and total equity would be exactly as reported, but the balance sheet would show $4,700 of dividends payable among current liabilities and $4,700 more cash. Working capital would not change (61,176−30,989=30,18761{,}176 - 30{,}989 = 30{,}18761,176−30,989=30,187), but the current ratio would fall from 2.15 to about 61,176÷30,989≈1.9761{,}176 \div 30{,}989 \approx 1.9761,176÷30,989≈1.97, because the same amount has been added to both sides of a ratio above one.

The statement of changes in equity

The statement of changes in equity explains how each component of equity moved from the start of the period to the end. For Lantern’s first ten months it is short: common stock rose from nothing to $20,000 when Ana’s shares were issued, and retained earnings rose by net income of $27,387 and fell by dividends of $5,000, ending at $22,387. Each closing balance is the figure on the balance sheet, which is the articulation F01 introduced: net income from the income statement enters here, and total equity leaves here for the balance sheet.

Note: Other components of equity

Larger companies’ statements have more columns. Additional paid-in capital and treasury stock each have their own. Under US GAAP so does accumulated other comprehensive income, which collects gains and losses that bypass net income, such as currency differences on foreign operations. IFRS reporters show similar amounts as reserves within equity, such as the revaluation surplus on property met in the long-lived assets lesson. The words vary too: statement of stockholders’ equity in much US practice, statement of changes in equity under IFRS.

Common mistakes

  • “A long-term loan is a noncurrent liability.”

    Only the part not due within the next year is noncurrent. Instalments due in the next twelve months are current, and a loan repayable in one sum becomes wholly current a year before it falls due: Coastline’s note will be current at 31 December 2027.

  • “Reclassifying the current portion means paying it.”

    Entry e8 moves $5,000 from Notes Payable to Current Portion of Long-Term Debt. No cash moves, and total liabilities don’t change; the payment is recorded on 1 June 2027.

  • “Par value is what a share is worth.”

    In states such as Delaware, par is a legal minimum issue price, usually a cent or less. Hazelmere’s shares sold at $15 with a par of $0.01; the other $14.99 is additional paid-in capital. Market value, book value and par are three different numbers.

  • “Dividends reduce retained earnings when they are paid.”

    Retained earnings fall on the declaration date, when the dividend becomes a liability. The payment settles Dividends Payable and doesn’t touch equity. No entry is made on the date of record.

  • “Accumulated depreciation and the allowance are liabilities because they have credit balances.”

    Both are contra-assets. They are deducted from the assets they belong to, on the face of the balance sheet or in a note. Listing them as liabilities would overstate both total assets and total liabilities.

In practice

Lenders read the classified balance sheet as the starting point for their own tests. Coastline’s loan officer checks that current liabilities include everything due within the year, including the van instalment and the tax, and that the current assets are what they claim to be: cash confirmed by the bank, receivables net of a supported allowance, inventory written down where needed. Loan agreements often set a minimum current ratio or working capital, and a breach can make a loan repayable on demand, which would itself turn a noncurrent liability into a current one. The applied example shows how much the timing of a single payment can matter.

Equity is read for what it says about the owners’ commitment. Lantern’s $20,000 of common stock is Ana’s investment; its $22,387 of retained earnings is profit she has left in the business, more than four times the $5,000 paid out. A lender likes that: retained earnings are a cushion that absorbs losses before the lender is affected. When Lantern takes on an outside investor, as the F00 scenario imagines, the statement of changes in equity will record the new shares, and the par and APIC split will depend on the charter.

For Ana the balance sheet is also a planning tool. It shows $42,940 of cash, but $20,189 of it is already spoken for by liabilities due in the next five months, and the first van instalment and the tax payment fall in the same weeks of the year. Before buying a new machine or paying a larger dividend she should prepare a cash budget for 2027, starting from this balance sheet. The statement of cash flows, the subject of the next module, explains how the year’s cash came to be $42,940 in the first place.

Summary

  • A classified balance sheet separates current items (within one year, or the operating cycle if longer) from noncurrent ones; US companies list assets in order of liquidity and IFRS doesn’t prescribe an order.
  • IFRS classifies a liability as current if it is due within twelve months or the entity has no right at the period end to defer settlement for twelve months (IAS 1, carried into IFRS 18 from 2027).
  • The current portion of long-term debt is the principal due within the next year; Lantern’s e8 moves $5,000 of the van note to current, leaving $25,000 of notes payable noncurrent.
  • Lantern’s balance sheet: total assets $93,676 = liabilities $51,289 + equity $42,387; working capital $30,187; current ratio about 2.15 (2.65 if e8 were forgotten).
  • Contributed capital is split between common stock (par or stated value) and additional paid-in capital; no-par stock without a stated value goes wholly to common stock, as Lantern’s $20,000 did.
  • A cash dividend reduces retained earnings on the declaration date and creates dividends payable; the date of record needs no entry; payment settles the liability.
  • The statement of changes in equity reconciles each component: Lantern’s retained earnings go from 0 + 27,387 − 5,000 to $22,387, and total equity to $42,387, the balance sheet figure.

Terms introduced

Order of liquidity
Listing assets from the most easily turned into cash (cash, then receivables, inventory and prepayments) to the least, and liabilities in roughly the order they fall due.
Current portion of long-term debt
The part of a long-term loan’s principal that is due within one year (or the operating cycle) after the balance sheet date, shown as a current liability; the rest remains noncurrent.
Report form
A balance sheet layout with assets listed first and liabilities and equity below them.
Account form
A balance sheet layout with assets on the left and liabilities and equity on the right, like the two sides of an account.
Authorised shares
The maximum number of shares of a class that a corporation’s charter allows it to issue.
Outstanding shares
Shares that have been issued and are held by shareholders, that is, issued shares less any held as treasury stock. They carry votes and receive dividends.
Treasury stock
A corporation’s own shares that it has issued and later bought back and holds. Shown as a deduction from equity; treasury shares have no votes and receive no dividends.
Par value
A nominal amount per share set in a corporation’s charter, often a cent or less, with legal rather than economic meaning. In states such as Delaware, shares with a par value can’t be issued for less than par.
No-par stock
Shares issued without a par value. Unless the board assigns a stated value, the whole amount received is credited to Common Stock.
Additional paid-in capital
The amount shareholders paid for shares above their par or stated value. Called share premium under IFRS and in the UK.
Declaration date
The date a corporation’s board declares a dividend, when it becomes a liability and retained earnings are reduced.
Date of record
The date that decides which shareholders will receive a declared dividend: those on the register that day. No journal entry is made.
Payment date
The date a declared dividend is paid, settling the dividends payable.
Dividends payable
A current liability for dividends declared but not yet paid.
