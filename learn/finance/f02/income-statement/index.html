The multi-step income statement

Rebuild Lantern’s first-year income statement in stages: net revenue, cost of sales and gross profit, operating expenses and operating income, interest, income tax and net income. Then calculate EBIT and EBITDA, see the rules for presenting non-GAAP measures, and meet earnings per share.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Prepare a multi-step income statement from an adjusted trial balance, and explain how it differs from a single-step statement.
  • Calculate gross profit, gross margin, operating income, income before income tax, income tax expense and net income.
  • Classify expenses by function and by nature, and place each account in the right section of a multi-step statement.
  • Calculate EBIT and EBITDA from an income statement, and explain the SEC rules for presenting EBITDA as a non-GAAP measure.
  • Calculate basic earnings per share and state which companies must report it.

Review first

  • Financial statements from the adjusted trial balance — You prepared Lantern’s single-step income statement for March there; this lesson builds the multi-step version for the year.
  • Inventory and cost of goods sold — Cost of goods sold and gross profit come from that lesson.
  • Value and valuation words — EBIT, EBITDA and earnings per share are defined there; here you calculate them from a full income statement.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Coastline Bank’s loan agreement also asks for annual financial statements within three months of the year end. In F01 Ana sent the bank a single-step income statement for March: revenues added up, expenses added up, one subtraction. For the first full year the accountant prepares a multi-step statement instead. It reaches the same net income, $27,387, but in stages that show the loan officer where the profit came from.

A single-step income statement lists all revenues, then all expenses, and subtracts once. For Lantern’s ten months: net revenue $193,320 minus total expenses $165,933 gives net income of $27,387. It is quick to prepare and hard to misread, but it treats the coffee in a bag, the rent of the kitchen, the interest on the bank loan and the income tax as one undifferentiated pile of cost.

A multi-step income statement subtracts the costs in a meaningful order and shows a subtotal after each group. Each subtotal answers a question a reader actually asks:

  • Gross profit: does the business sell its goods and services for more than they cost to provide?
  • Operating income: does that margin cover the costs of running the business, such as rent, wages, marketing and depreciation?
  • Income before income tax: what is left after the costs of borrowing and any other non-operating items?
  • Net income: what remains for the owners after income tax?

The staircase makes the structure visible. Cost of sales takes almost half of Lantern’s revenue; operating expenses take most of what is left; interest is a sliver; income tax takes a quarter of income before tax. Net income ends at about 14 cents for each dollar of revenue. The statement doesn’t change the arithmetic of F01, only the order in which the costs are subtracted, but that order is what lets a reader compare Lantern with other businesses and with its own next year.

Key idea: Same bottom line, different story

Single-step and multi-step statements always give the same net income: the accounts are the same, only grouped differently. What differs is the information in the subtotals. Gross profit and operating income can’t be read from a single-step statement, and they are where most of the analysis in F03 starts.

In depth

Lantern’s multi-step income statement

The statement below is prepared from Lantern’s adjusted trial balance at 31 December 2026, after the nine year-end adjustments. Its heading names the business, the statement and the period, which for a first year is “the ten months ended 31 December 2026”. Each section is explained in turn after it.

Net revenue

The statement opens with gross revenue by stream, less the contra-revenue account for refunds, giving net revenue of $193,320, as the revenue recognition lesson derived. Showing the streams separately costs a few lines and tells the reader at once that the shop provides more than half of Lantern’s revenue. A company with sales discounts would deduct them here too.

Cost of sales and gross profit

Cost of sales is the cost of providing what was sold. For the shop it is cost of goods sold, $52,800. For catering and workshops it is cost of services: the event baristas’ wages ($25,200) and the catering supplies and workshop materials used ($10,900), $36,100 in all. In F01’s March books every wage went to Wages Expense and every consumable to Supplies Expense. For the annual statements the accountant records the direct costs of events and workshops in Cost of Services instead, because without them the services would show a gross profit equal to their revenue and the comparison with the shop would be meaningless.

When comparing gross margins across companies, check what each puts above the line.

Operating expenses: by function or by nature

Operating expenses are the costs of running the business that are not part of cost of sales. Lantern’s statement lists them account by account, which is common for a small company. Larger companies group them, in one of two ways. The function of expense method groups costs by the activity they serve, such as cost of sales, selling, and general and administrative. The nature of expense method groups them by what they are, such as goods purchased, employee wages, depreciation and rent, without assigning them to activities.

The grouping is approximate: a full function analysis would allocate the kitchen rent and the espresso equipment’s depreciation between cost of services and administration, the van’s fuel and depreciation to delivery, and part of the shop staff’s wages to fulfilment. Lantern’s summary accounts don’t hold the information to split them, one reason small companies list expenses account by account.

The same year by nature looks quite different. There is no cost of sales, so there is no gross profit; instead the reader sees, for example, that wages for all staff come to $43,600, a figure the function method splits between cost of services and operating expenses.

IFRS requires an analysis of expenses by nature or by function, whichever gives information that is reliable and more relevant (IAS 1 paragraph 99). A company using the function method must show cost of sales separately and disclose additional information on the nature of its expenses, including depreciation and amortisation and employee benefits (paragraphs 103 and 104). US GAAP lets a private company such as Lantern choose a single-step or a multi-step format. IFRS 18, which replaces IAS 1 from 2027, changes these requirements; the course’s last lesson, US GAAP and IFRS, explains how.

Operating income

Gross profit less operating expenses, plus any gains on selling long-lived assets, is operating income (also called income from operations or operating profit): $37,716 for Lantern, which sold nothing in 2026. It measures what the business earns from its ordinary activities before the cost of its borrowing and before tax, so it can be compared across businesses financed in different ways. Lantern earns about 19.5 cents of operating income per dollar of revenue; turning such figures into ratios and comparing them is the work of F03.

Other income and expenses

Items that don’t arise from the business’s main operations are shown after operating income, as other income and expenses. Lantern has one: interest expense of $1,200, made up of the $450 paid on the Coastline note on 1 June, 1 September and 1 December, plus $750 accrued at the year end ($50 on the Coastline note for December and $700 on the van note for June to December). Interest revenue on savings would also appear here. Gains and losses on selling equipment would not: US GAAP requires them to be included in income from operations whenever that subtotal is presented (ASC 360-10-45-5), so Lantern’s chart adds a gain after operating expenses and includes a loss among them. IAS 1 doesn’t define operating income, so IFRS practice has varied; IFRS 18 changes this from 2027, as the course’s last lesson explains.

Income tax

A corporation pays income tax on its taxable profit. The US federal corporate rate is 21% of taxable income (26 U.S.C. §11(b)), a flat rate that applies to taxable years beginning after 31 December 2017; many states also tax corporate income. The Lantern case assumes a combined rate of 25% and, to keep the course introductory, that taxable income equals income before income tax. When the two differ, as they often do, deferred tax arises; later courses cover it.

Income tax expense for the year is therefore 0.25×36,516=9,1290.25 \times 36{,}516 = 9{,}1290.25×36,516=9,129 dollars. Lantern paid $6,000 of estimated tax during the year, so the year-end adjustment records the rest as a liability:

EBIT and EBITDA

F00 defined EBIT (earnings before interest and taxes) and EBITDA (EBIT plus depreciation and amortisation). With a multi-step statement both can be calculated in two directions. Working up from the bottom, as the SEC staff describe, EBIT is net income with interest and income tax added back: 27,387+1,200+9,129=37,71627{,}387 + 1{,}200 + 9{,}129 = 37{,}71627,387+1,200+9,129=37,716. Working down, it is operating income plus or minus any non-operating items other than interest: Lantern has none, so EBIT equals operating income, $37,716. Adding back depreciation of $4,800 (Lantern has no amortisation) gives EBITDA of $42,516.

EBIT=Net income+Interest expense+Income tax expense,EBITDA=EBIT+D&A\text{EBIT} = \text{Net income} + \text{Interest expense} + \text{Income tax expense}, \qquad \text{EBITDA} = \text{EBIT} + \text{D\&A}EBIT=Net income+Interest expense+Income tax expense,EBITDA=EBIT+D&A

EBITDA is often described as a proxy for operating cash flow, but it is not cash. Lantern’s net cash from operating activities was $39,940, not $42,516: it paid $6,450 of interest and tax, which EBITDA ignores, while unpaid bills and customers’ advance payments ($17,410) more than covered the cash tied up in receivables, inventory, supplies and prepaid insurance ($13,536). Module 3 reconciles the two. And depreciation, though not a cash payment this year, stands for equipment and a van that will have to be replaced.

Standards: EBITDA and the SEC’s rules on non-GAAP measures

Neither US GAAP nor IFRS defines EBITDA, so it is a non-GAAP financial measure. When a company registered with the SEC discloses one publicly, Regulation G requires it to present the most directly comparable GAAP measure (for EBITDA, net income) and a quantitative reconciliation between the two, and forbids a presentation that is misleading (17 CFR 244.100(a) and (b)). In filings, Item 10(e) of Regulation S-K also requires the GAAP measure to be shown with equal or greater prominence (Non-GAAP Compliance and Disclosure Interpretation 102.10). The SEC staff’s interpretations define EBITDA as net income plus interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation, and say a measure calculated differently should not be called EBITDA but given a distinct title, such as Adjusted EBITDA (Interpretation 103.01). Lantern is not an SEC registrant, so the rules don’t bind it, but they describe good practice for any business quoting EBITDA to a lender.

Earnings per share

Companies whose common shares, or securities that can become common shares, trade in a public market, or that are preparing to sell them in one, must report basic earnings per share, and diluted earnings per share as well when they have potential common shares such as options or convertible securities (ASC 260-10-15-2; IAS 33 sets a similar requirement under IFRS). Basic EPS divides net income available to common shareholders (net income less preferred dividends) by the weighted average number of common shares outstanding during the period; diluted EPS also counts shares that options and convertible securities could add. For example, a fictional listed company, Quarrybank Foods, with net income of $12.6 million, preferred dividends of $0.6 million, and 7.5 million common shares for the first half of the year and 8.5 million for the second, reports basic EPS of (12.6−0.6)÷8.0=1.50(12.6 - 0.6) \div 8.0 = 1.50(12.6−0.6)÷8.0=1.50 dollars. Lantern’s shares are privately held, so it reports no EPS.

Common mistakes

  • “A multi-step statement gives a different net income.”

    It groups the same accounts differently. Lantern’s net income is $27,387 in both formats; only the subtotals above it are new.

  • “Interest is an operating expense.”

    Interest is the cost of how the business is financed, not of running it, so the multi-step statement shows it after operating income. That is what lets operating income be compared between businesses with different amounts of debt.

  • “Income tax expense is the tax paid.”

    Tax expense is the tax charged on the year’s income, whenever it is paid. Lantern’s expense is $9,129; it paid $6,000 during the year and owes $3,129 at the year end.

  • “EBITDA is a GAAP figure, and it is the cash the business generates.”

    Neither US GAAP nor IFRS defines EBITDA. It ignores interest and tax actually paid, the cash tied up in receivables and inventory, and the spending needed to replace equipment. Lantern’s EBITDA is $42,516; its operating cash flow is $39,940.

  • “Dividends appear at the bottom of the income statement.”

    Dividends are distributions of net income to owners, not costs of earning it. Lantern’s $5,000 of dividends appears in the statement of changes in equity, where it reduces retained earnings.

In practice

The loan officer’s reading. Coastline Bank’s loan officer reads Lantern’s statement from the top. Gross profit of $104,420 says the products and services are priced well above their direct cost, and operating income of $37,716 says the business covers its rent, staff and overheads with room to spare. Interest of $1,200 is small against that operating income, so the loans look affordable from profit; whether they are affordable from cash is a question for the statement of cash flows. For a first year, the officer will mostly be looking for a base to compare 2027 against.

Ana’s pricing decision. The gross margin table changes how Ana thinks about growth. Each extra dollar of catering and workshop revenue brings about 57 cents of gross profit before any overheads, each extra dollar of shop sales about 52 cents, and the shop’s dollar then pays shipping and card fees as well. Before she spends more on shop advertising, she can ask whether the same money would win more catering clients. The multi-step format doesn’t make the decision, but without it the comparison couldn’t be made.

An investor reading a press release. When a listed company leads its results announcement with an adjusted figure, an investor reading “Adjusted EBITDA up 18%” goes first to the reconciliation the SEC requires, to see what was added back: interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation are standard; restructuring costs, share-based pay and “one-off” items that recur every year deserve scrutiny. Then the investor checks net income and earnings per share, which the rules require to be shown at least as prominently. F03 develops the analysis that follows.

Summary

  • A multi-step income statement deducts costs in stages: net revenue, cost of sales, gross profit, operating expenses, operating income, other income and expenses, income before income tax, income tax, net income. Net income equals the single-step figure.
  • Lantern, 2026: net revenue $193,320; cost of sales $88,900 (goods $52,800, services $36,100); gross profit $104,420 (54.0%); operating expenses $66,704; operating income $37,716; interest $1,200; income tax $9,129; net income $27,387.
  • Cost of services collects the direct costs of catering and workshops, so that gross profit is meaningful for a business that sells services as well as goods.
  • Expenses can be presented by function (cost of sales, selling, administrative) or by nature (goods, wages, depreciation); IAS 1 requires one or the other, with nature disclosures when the function method is used.
  • Income tax expense is the tax on the year’s income: 25% of $36,516 in the case, against a 21% US federal statutory rate. Lantern paid $6,000 and owes $3,129.
  • EBIT = net income + interest + tax = $37,716; EBITDA = EBIT + D&A = $42,516. EBITDA is a non-GAAP measure: SEC registrants must reconcile it to net income, give net income equal or greater prominence, and call a differently calculated figure something else, such as Adjusted EBITDA.
  • Public companies report basic and diluted earnings per share: net income less preferred dividends, divided by the weighted average common shares.

Terms introduced

Single-step income statement
An income statement that adds up all revenues and all expenses and subtracts once to give net income, with no intermediate subtotals.
Multi-step income statement
An income statement that deducts costs in stages and shows subtotals such as gross profit, operating income and income before income tax before net income.
Cost of sales
The direct cost of the goods and services sold in a period: cost of goods sold, plus cost of services for a business that sells services.
Cost of services
The direct costs of providing services sold, such as the wages of staff who deliver them and the materials they use.
Operating expenses
The costs of running a business that are not part of cost of sales, such as selling, general and administrative costs, rent and depreciation.
Operating income
Gross profit less operating expenses: what a business earns from its ordinary activities before interest, other non-operating items and income tax. Also called income from operations or operating profit. Under US GAAP it includes gains and losses on selling long-lived assets.
Other income and expenses
Income and expenses that do not arise from a business’s main operations, such as interest revenue and interest expense. Shown after operating income. Under US GAAP, gains and losses on selling long-lived assets are not among them: they are included in operating income.
Income tax expense
The income tax charged on a period’s income, whether or not it has been paid. Deducted from income before income tax to give net income.
Statutory tax rate
The rate of tax set by law, such as the 21% US federal corporate income tax rate.
Effective tax rate
Income tax expense divided by income before income tax.
Function of expense method
Presenting expenses grouped by the activity they serve, such as cost of sales, selling, and general and administrative.
Nature of expense method
Presenting expenses grouped by their nature, such as goods purchased, employee wages, depreciation and rent, without allocating them to activities.
Non-GAAP financial measure
A measure of financial performance or position, such as EBITDA, that is not defined by the accounting framework used in the financial statements.
Adjusted EBITDA
A label for an EBITDA-like measure with further adjustments beyond net income plus interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. Its definition is the company’s own, so it must be read with its reconciliation.
Basic earnings per share
Net income less preferred dividends, divided by the weighted average number of common shares outstanding in the period.
Diluted earnings per share
Earnings per share recalculated as if options, convertible securities and similar instruments had been turned into common shares, when that would reduce it.
