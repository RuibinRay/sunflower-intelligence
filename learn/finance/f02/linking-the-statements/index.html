Linking the statements

Put Lantern’s whole annual report together, check that its four statements articulate and find the error when they don’t, follow single transactions through all of them, see where the gap between profit and cash sits, and push one change through the three main statements the way analysts test a model.

About 80 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Trace a transaction through the income statement, the statement of changes in equity, the balance sheet and the statement of cash flows.
  • Use a preparer’s checklist of links to test whether a set of statements articulates, and locate the error when a link fails.
  • Locate each part of the gap between Lantern’s net income and its operating cash flow on the balance sheet, and judge which parts will reverse.
  • Calculate the effect of one change, such as $1,000 more depreciation with tax at 25%, on all three main statements, with the tax paid or still owed.
  • Interpret the linked statements to judge how much cash a business can afford to distribute.

Review first

  • Financial statements from the adjusted trial balance — Articulation was introduced there, on Lantern’s first month.
  • The balance sheet and equity — The statement of changes in equity and the classified balance sheet are two of the four statements linked here.
  • The indirect method — The reconciliation of net income to operating cash flow, its sign rules and the roll-forward of accounts are used here without being re-taught.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

It is January 2027. Lantern’s accountant has closed the books for the ten months to 31 December 2026 and drafted the four statements. Before they go to Coastline Bank and to the dealer’s finance company that lent Lantern $20,000 for the van, she checks that they tell one consistent story: every figure that appears in two places must agree, and every change on the balance sheet must be explained somewhere else.

F01 introduced articulation on a single month: net income flows into equity, ending equity into the balance sheet, and ending cash from the statement of cash flows into the balance sheet. A full year adds inventory, bad debts, depreciation on two assets, accrued interest, income tax and a second dividend, so there are many more places for a link to break. This lesson puts Lantern’s whole annual report together, shows how a preparer finds an error when a link fails, follows single transactions through all four statements, and walks one change through the three main statements, the way analysts test a financial model.

The idea underneath every link is that comparative balance sheets list balances at two moments, and the other three statements explain how those balances moved in between. The income statement explains most of the change in retained earnings; the statement of changes in equity explains all of the change in equity; the statement of cash flows explains all of the change in cash. Any other balance sheet change is the other half of an entry whose first half appears on one of those statements, or a noncash investing and financing activity disclosed in a note.

Lantern makes the links easy to see because it began on 1 March 2026 with nothing: every opening balance is zero, so every closing balance on its balance sheet is also the year’s change. Cash of $42,940 is the year’s increase in cash; retained earnings of $22,387 are the year’s net income less the year’s dividends. From 2027 onward the opening balances will be the 2026 closing balances, and every link will compare two balance sheets.

Key idea: Three links and one identity

Net income on the income statement equals net income in the statement of changes in equity. Ending equity there equals equity on the balance sheet. Ending cash on the statement of cash flows equals cash on the balance sheet. And the balance sheet must balance: assets equal liabilities plus equity. A set of statements that passes all four tests is not necessarily right, but one that fails any of them is certainly wrong.

Readers rely on the links as much as preparers do. A lender who sees profit rising while operating cash flow falls turns to the balance sheet to find where the cash went: into receivables, into inventory, or into paying suppliers faster. An owner who wants to know how much of the profit has been kept in the business looks at retained earnings. Each question is answered by reading two statements together, which only works if they articulate.

In depth

Lantern’s annual report

Here are the four statements the accountant drafted, in the order F01 taught. The income statement comes first, because net income feeds the statement of changes in equity; that statement comes next, because ending equity feeds the balance sheet; then the balance sheet; then the statement of cash flows, which explains the change in the balance sheet’s cash. The income statement is in the multi-step format and the statement of cash flows uses the direct method, followed by the indirect reconciliation of net income to operating cash flow.

The notes complete the report: Lantern’s accounting policies (first in, first out for inventory, straight-line depreciation with a full month in the month of purchase, the aging method for bad debts), the terms of its two notes payable, and the noncash part of the van purchase. Under the full disclosure principle a reader shouldn’t have to guess any of them.

The preparer’s checks

Before the statements leave the office, the accountant works through a list of links. Most are tie-outs, a comparison of the same figure in two places; the rest are roll-forwards, which need a short calculation. The last column says what a failed check usually points to, which is where the search for an error starts.

The last three checks are roll-forwards, which the indirect method lesson used to rebuild cash flows; here they test the statements. Lantern’s opening balances are zero, but from 2027 every roll-forward starts from the previous year’s closing balance. Accounts receivable rolls forward the same way: opening $0, plus catering invoiced of $59,920, less cash collected of $51,720, less the $820 written off, gives $7,380, the gross balance on the balance sheet.

Passing every check is necessary, not sufficient. An expense posted to the wrong expense account, a noncurrent asset shown as current, or an adjusting entry left out of the ledger altogether leaves every link intact, because all four statements are drawn from the same ledger. The checks catch omissions and copying errors between the statements; review and judgement catch the rest.

Finding the error when a link fails

When a link fails, the size of the difference is the first clue. A difference equal to one item suggests that item was left out, counted twice, or is a noncash item treated as cash; a difference of exactly twice an item suggests it was entered with the wrong sign. The direction narrows the search further: a statement of cash flows that ends below the balance sheet’s cash has too little coming in or too much going out. Two errors can partly offset, so after fixing the first, measure the difference again; the challenging example below works through such a case.

One transaction, four statements

Following single transactions shows why the links hold. Each journal entry changes at least two accounts. When one of them is a revenue, expense or dividends account, the entry reaches equity; when one of them is Cash, it reaches the statement of cash flows. Four of Lantern’s entries show the patterns, and every amount in the income statement column also reaches the statement of changes in equity through net income.

The van shows one decision touching every statement at different times. On 1 June it changed the balance sheet and, for the $10,000 paid, the investing section. The $20,000 borrowed from the dealer’s finance company never passed through Lantern’s bank account, so it is disclosed as a noncash investing and financing activity, and the instalments repaying it from June 2027 will be financing outflows. From June onward the van generated expenses: $400 a month of depreciation and $100 a month of interest (20,000 × 6% ÷ 12), both reducing net income and so retained earnings. At the year end, entry e8 moved the $5,000 due on 1 June 2027 into current liabilities, which changed the balance sheet’s classification and nothing else.

The write-down shows how an entry with no cash still reaches the statement of cash flows. In the indirect method it lowers income before tax by $400 and, after the $100 of tax it saves, net income by $300; it makes the increase in inventory $400 smaller (Lantern’s inventory line, $5,800, is measured after it); and, because Lantern’s 2026 tax is partly unpaid, it makes the increase in income taxes payable $100 smaller. The three effects, −300+400−100=0-300 + 400 - 100 = 0−300+400−100=0, cancel, as they must when no cash moves. Bad debt expense works the same way, through the net receivables line ($6,796, which already includes the $1,404 charge) and through income taxes payable.

Where the gap between profit and cash sits

The indirect method lesson reconciled Lantern’s net income of $27,387 with its $39,940 of cash from operations; the question here is where each part of the $12,553 gap sits and what happens to it next. Depreciation of $4,800 is the cost of assets already paid for or financed: the equipment and the van’s $10,000 down payment left through investing, and the $20,000 financed by the van note leaves through financing as it is repaid from June 2027. The $13,536 of operating current assets (net receivables, inventory, supplies and prepaid insurance) sits on the asset side of the balance sheet and turns into cash, or expense, in 2027. The $21,289 of operating current liabilities sits on the other side and financed more than the whole gap, but it reverses early in 2027, when Lantern pays the December bills, the wages and the tax, and delivers $6,100 of service it has already been paid for. A first year flatters operating cash flow this way, because a new business has no old liabilities to pay off, so a lender who projected $39,940 a year forward would be overestimating.

A three-statement walkthrough

Analysts test their understanding of the links, and their spreadsheet models, by pushing one change through all three main statements. The standard exercise: suppose depreciation is $1,000 higher, tax is 25% of income before tax, and the change in tax is paid in cash within the year.

  1. Income statement. Operating income and income before tax fall by $1,000. Income tax falls by 25%×1,000=25025\% \times 1{,}000 = 25025%×1,000=250, so net income falls by 1,000−250=7501{,}000 - 250 = 7501,000−250=750.
  2. Statement of cash flows. Net income is $750 lower, but the $1,000 of depreciation is added back, so operating cash flow rises by −750+1,000=250-750 + 1{,}000 = 250−750+1,000=250: the tax saved. Investing and financing are unchanged.
  3. Balance sheet. Cash is $250 higher. Accumulated depreciation is $1,000 higher, so property and equipment (net) is $1,000 lower, and total assets fall by $750. Retained earnings fall by the $750 drop in net income. Assets −750 = liabilities 0 + equity −750: the balance sheet still balances.

Lantern’s own books add a twist. Its estimated tax payments were fixed at $6,000, and entry e9 sets Income Taxes Payable at the year’s tax expense less that amount, so none of a change in its 2026 tax is paid within 2026. If depreciation were $1,000 higher, the tax expense would be $250 lower and so would the payable; the saving would reach cash only when the 2026 tax is paid in 2027. For 2026, then: net income −750, depreciation +1,000, change in income taxes payable −250, so operating cash flow is unchanged; on the balance sheet, assets −1,000 = liabilities −250 + equity −750.

Note: Book tax and real tax

Like the case, this lesson assumes income tax is 25% of income before tax. Real tax returns follow their own depreciation rules, so the tax a business pays for a year is not exactly 25% of its accounting profit; accountants record the difference as deferred tax, a topic for intermediate accounting.

Common mistakes

  • “Tax can be left out of a three-statement walkthrough.”

    Without tax, $1,000 more depreciation would lower net income by $1,000 and leave cash unchanged. With tax at 25%, net income falls by only $750, and the $250 of tax saved is cash once the tax is paid. Every pretax change has a tax echo on all three statements: in income tax expense, in cash or income taxes payable, and in retained earnings.

  • “If the balance sheet balances, the statements are right.”

    Balancing is one test among several. Hatherley Print Studio’s balance sheet balanced while its statement of cash flows was wrong in two places. Run every link, and remember that some errors, such as an expense posted to the wrong expense account, break none of them.

  • “A dividend reduces cash when it is declared.”

    Declaring a dividend reduces retained earnings and creates Dividends Payable; cash falls only when it is paid, and that payment is the financing outflow. A December declaration paid in January appears in this year’s statement of changes in equity and next year’s statement of cash flows.

  • “The tax saved by an extra expense is always cash this year.”

    Only if the change in tax is paid within the year. Lantern’s estimated payments were fixed at $6,000, so $1,000 more depreciation would lower income taxes payable by $250 and leave 2026’s operating cash flow unchanged; the cash saving arrives when the tax is paid in 2027.

  • “Operating cash flow above net income is always good news.”

    Lantern’s operating cash flow exceeds its net income by $12,553, but $21,289 of its operating cash came from liabilities that will be paid, or worked off, early in 2027. Read where each part of the gap sits before drawing a conclusion from its size.

  • “An entry with no cash never appears in the statement of cash flows.”

    It never changes a cash total, but it can still appear. The accrued interest (e5) shows up in Lantern’s indirect reconciliation as an increase in interest payable, offsetting its effect on net income and on income taxes payable, and the $20,000 van note appears in the noncash disclosure beneath the statement. Count an entry as a cash flow only when cash moves.

In practice

Coastline Bank’s credit analyst receives Lantern’s annual report in February 2027. Before calculating anything she repeats the accountant’s checks, because statements that don’t articulate can’t be relied on, and then reads the statements in pairs. The income statement and the statement of cash flows together show that Lantern’s operations produced more cash than profit; the balance sheet shows why: $21,289 of operating liabilities, most of which fall due early in 2027.

Her next step is a forecast for 2027, which she builds as a three-statement model. Assumptions about sales, margins, how quickly customers pay and suppliers are paid, and capital spending drive a forecast income statement and balance sheet; the statement of cash flows is derived from the changes between the two balance sheets. The links are the model’s skeleton. Forecast net income feeds retained earnings, forecast depreciation feeds accumulated depreciation and the add-back, and the van note’s $5,000 instalment reduces both cash and the liability. If the forecast balance sheet doesn’t balance, a link is missing, and she finds it the way the accountant finds errors in a draft: by the size and direction of the difference.

The model answers the lender’s real question: can Lantern pay the $5,000 instalment and $1,200 of interest due on the van note on 1 June 2027, and $600 of interest on the Coastline note during the year, from its own operations, now that it also pays Ana a salary? Read together, the 2026 statements suggest it can, provided the first year’s timing benefit is not mistaken for a permanent level of cash flow. Ratios that summarise this kind of judgement, such as cash flow relative to debt payments, belong to F03; the reasoning they rest on is the articulation in this lesson.

Summary

  • Comparative balance sheets list balances at two moments; the income statement, the statement of changes in equity and the statement of cash flows explain how they moved in between.
  • Three links and one identity: net income to the statement of changes in equity, ending equity to the balance sheet, ending cash to the balance sheet, and assets = liabilities + equity. Lantern: 27,387; 42,387; 42,940; 93,676 = 51,289 + 42,387.
  • Roll-forwards (opening + movements = closing) extend the checks to tax, interest, accumulated depreciation and receivables. When a link fails, a gap equal to an item suggests an omission and twice an item a sign error.
  • A transaction reaches equity through any revenue, expense or dividend it touches and the statement of cash flows through any cash it moves; an entry with no cash changes no cash total, though it may appear as offsetting reconciliation lines or in the noncash disclosure.
  • Lantern’s $12,553 gap between operating cash flow and net income sits in three places: depreciation of $4,800 on assets already paid for or financed, $13,536 of operating current assets, and $21,289 of operating current liabilities that reverse early in 2027.
  • One change through three statements: $1,000 more depreciation at 25% lowers net income by $750; operating cash flow rises by $250 only if the change in tax is paid within the year, otherwise income taxes payable fall instead.
  • Retained earnings show how much of the profit earned has been kept in the business; state law and lenders’ covenants set what it may legally distribute, and the balance sheet and the statement of cash flows show what it can afford.

Terms introduced

Annual report
The document a business issues after its financial year: the financial statements and their notes, and, for larger companies, management’s commentary and the auditor’s report.
Roll-forward
A check or schedule that starts from an account’s opening balance, adds and subtracts the period’s movements and arrives at its closing balance, with each movement traced to another statement or to the ledger.
Tie-out
Confirming that a figure shown in one statement or note agrees with the same figure wherever else it appears and with the ledger.
Three-statement model
A financial model in which the income statement, balance sheet and statement of cash flows are linked, so that a change in one assumption flows through all three and the forecast balance sheet still balances.
