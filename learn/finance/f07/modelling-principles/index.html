Modelling principles

A financial model is a machine for answering one question about the future, and someone else has to trust its answers. Learn how to lay out inputs, calculations and outputs, build the timeline, write one formula per row, and follow the conventions that let a lender check your work.

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Explain what a financial model is, and define the decision and the outputs it must serve before building anything.
  • Design a model with inputs, calculations and outputs kept apart, and each assumption in one labelled cell.
  • Construct a timeline of actual and forecast periods, and use flags and month counts for events that start part-way through a year.
  • Write one consistent formula per row that refers to its inputs instead of typing numbers into it.
  • Apply the conventions of colour, sign and units that let another person read and check a model.

The idea

Fictional case

It is February 2028. Lantern Coffee Co. has two years of accounts behind it, revenue of $577,860 in 2027, and three things starting this spring: a second kitchen on 1 April, the canned Cold Brew, and a new $60,000 term loan from Coastline Bank to pay for the kitchen. Coastline’s credit officer has one more request before the loan is signed. She wants a five-year forecast, 2028 to 2032, showing that Lantern can make the monthly payments and keep its current ratio at 1.2 or more, the condition written into the loan. Ana has a spreadsheet of the 2027 figures and a head full of plans. This course turns them into a model the bank can rely on.

This first lesson is about how a model is built, before any forecasting. A model that gives the right answer once but cannot be checked, changed or explained is of little use to Coastline, because the credit officer’s job is to ask “what if?” and see what happens.

What a financial model is

A financial model is a set of linked calculations that represents a business’s finances and answers questions about its future. Its parts are assumptions about the future (how fast sales grow, what things cost, what the loan’s interest rate is), the arithmetic that turns those assumptions into forecast statements, and the results someone will act on. A three-statement model, the kind this course builds, forecasts the income statement, the balance sheet and the cash flow statement together, linked so that a change in one assumption flows through all three.

Every useful model starts from a question, not from a template. Coastline’s question is narrow: will Lantern have the cash to pay the loan, and will it meet its covenant at every year end? A potential investor would ask what the business is worth. Ana herself wants to know whether she can afford to pay herself more in 2029. The same business can need three different models, or one model with three sets of outputs. Writing the question down first decides which outputs the model must produce, and from those, which calculations and inputs it needs.

Key idea: Work backwards from the decision

Name the decision, then the outputs that inform it, then the calculations that produce those outputs, then the inputs those calculations need. Build in the opposite order, inputs first, but plan in this one. Anything that serves no output can be left out.

Inputs, calculations, outputs

The most important design rule is to keep three kinds of cell apart. Inputs are the numbers you type: last year’s accounts, growth rates, prices, the loan’s rate and term. Calculations are formulas that turn inputs into forecasts: revenue, costs, the balance of the loan at each year end. Outputs are the few results someone looks at: the forecast statements, the current ratio each year, the cash at its lowest point. Information flows one way, from inputs through calculations to outputs, and never back.

Keeping them apart has three practical benefits. Each assumption sits in one cell, so changing it once changes it everywhere: when Coastline asks what happens if online sales grow 5% a year instead of 12%, Ana changes one cell, not forty. Inputs can be listed in one place with their sources, so a reader can judge them. And a reader can follow any result back to the inputs that drive it, which is how a model is checked.

Time runs across the page

A model’s periods run across the columns and its line items down the rows. Lantern’s model has one column for each year: 2026 and 2027 are actual results, marked A, and 2028 to 2032 are estimates, marked E. Every column covers the same length of time, here a calendar year ending 31 December, and its heading says which. Periods that are not all the same length (a 10-month first year, say) are a common source of silent errors, because a growth rate applied to a short year understates everything that follows.

Lantern has exactly that problem. Its two cafés opened on 1 March 2027 and traded for ten months, so their 2027 sales of $236,000 are not a year’s sales. Before growing them, the model annualises them: 236,000×12÷10=283,200236{,}000 \times 12 \div 10 = 283{,}200236,000×12÷10=283,200 dollars a year. The opposite problem arises when something starts part-way through a forecast year. The second kitchen opens on 1 April 2028, so it trades for nine months of 2028. A model handles this with a flag, a row that says whether (1 or 0), or for how many months, the kitchen is open in each period, multiplied into every kitchen line. Change the opening date and every kitchen figure moves with it.

One row, one formula

In a well-built model every forecast cell in a row holds the same formula, copied across. Online revenue for 2028 is last year’s online revenue grown by the 2028 growth rate; online revenue for 2029 is the 2028 figure grown by the 2029 rate; and so on. Written once with the right references and filled to the right, the formula is identical in structure in every column, so checking one cell checks the row.

The opposite is a hard-coded number: a value typed into a cell, or into a formula, where a reference to an input belongs. A forecast that says =C6*1.12 works today, but the 12% is now hidden inside a formula; when the assumption changes, someone has to find every formula that contains it. Worse is a forecast cell that holds a typed number, such as 231,000, with no formula at all. It looks the same as its neighbours until an input changes and it does not.

In depth

References that survive being copied

Filling a formula across a row moves its references. A relative reference such as C6 moves with the formula, so in the next column it becomes D6: exactly what “last year’s value” needs. An input that every year shares, such as the cost of goods as a share of sales, must not move, so it is absolute: $B$4. A row of yearly inputs, such as the growth rate for each year, needs the column to move but the row to stay: the mixed reference D$3. Getting these right is what makes one formula per row possible.

Conventions that let others read the model

Models are read by people who did not build them, so the profession uses shared conventions. The most widespread is colour: blue text for inputs, black for formulas, and green for formulas that pull a value from another sheet or file. A reader can see at a glance what was typed and what was calculated, and a blue number in the middle of a forecast row is an immediate warning. Lantern’s model also shades input cells, so the credit officer knows exactly which cells she may change.

  • Signs. Decide once whether costs are shown as positive numbers that are subtracted, or as negatives that are added, and keep to it. Lantern’s model shows revenues and costs as positive numbers and subtracts costs, as the income statement does; in the cash flow statement, cash going out is negative.
  • Units. State the unit in each label or heading (“$”, “cans”, “%”), and do not mix units in one row. If a model is in thousands of dollars, say so at the top of every sheet.
  • Labels. Every row has a label in the left column that says what it is; inputs say where they came from (“F03 accounts”, “Ana’s estimate”, “Coastline’s term sheet”).
  • Short formulas. A formula that needs a paragraph to explain should be broken into steps on separate rows. Long nested formulas are where errors hide.

The conventions are summarised in published standards. The ICAEW Financial Modelling Code, from the Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales, is a principles-based guide for anyone who builds or uses models. The FAST Standard (its letters stand for Flexible, Appropriate, Structured and Transparent) is more detailed and prescriptive, with rules down to the level of individual formulas. Neither is a law. Large lenders and investors often expect a model to follow one of them, or a house style like it, before they will rely on it.

The layout of a three-statement model

Professional models are usually split into sections, often one per worksheet, in the order the calculations flow. A typical order is shown below. Lantern’s model is small enough for one sheet with these sections stacked down the page, which is how this course builds it. The spreadsheet on this site has no references between sheets, and a single sheet makes the links easier to see.

  1. Cover and contents: what the model is for, who built it, the version and date, and the units.
  2. Inputs: every assumption, with its source. History is input too: the actual accounts are typed in, or linked from the accounting system.
  3. Revenue and cost build-ups: the forecasts of each revenue line and cost, from their drivers (lesson 3).
  4. Schedules: working capital, capital spending and depreciation, and debt (module 2).
  5. The three statements: income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement, built from the lines above.
  6. Outputs: ratios and covenant tests, valuation, scenarios and a one-page summary (module 3).
  7. Checks: the balance sheet balances, cash on the balance sheet equals cash from the cash flow statement, and other tests that should always be zero.

Errors by design, and how to avoid them

Most model errors are not arithmetic mistakes but structural ones: a hard-coded number, a reference that moved when it should not have, a total that misses a row, a part year treated as a full one. They are prevented more by layout than by care. One input in one place means there is nothing to forget to update. One formula per row means one cell to check per row. A check row that must always be zero, such as total assets minus total liabilities and equity, turns a silent error into a visible one. Module 3 returns to reviewing a model in detail.

One structural trap deserves a warning now. Interest on a loan depends on the loan’s balance, and if a model also lets the balance depend on interest (by borrowing to pay it, say), the formulas form a loop: a circular reference. Spreadsheets can iterate to a solution, but circular models are fragile and hard to check. Module 2 avoids the loop by charging interest on the balance at the start of each year, a common simplification that this course states wherever it is used.

Note: A model is not a forecast of the truth

Every number in Lantern’s forecast years is a consequence of Ana’s assumptions, not a prediction that will come true. A model’s value is that it makes the assumptions explicit, shows their consequences consistently, and lets anyone change them. Treat its outputs as “what follows if”, and test them with scenario analysis before relying on them.

Common mistakes

  • Typing numbers into formulas

    A formula such as =C6*1.12 hides an assumption where nobody will find it. Put every assumption in an input cell and refer to it; the only numbers in a formula should be constants that can never change, such as the 12 in “× 12 ÷ 10”.

  • A different formula in every column

    Patching one year by hand, or writing a slightly different formula in one column, breaks the rule that checking one cell checks the row. If a year genuinely needs different treatment, give it an input (a flag, a month count) and keep the formula the same.

  • Anchoring the wrong part of a reference

    Filled right, =C6*(1+$D$3) uses 2028’s rate for every year and =C6*(1+D3) drifts down the page when filled down. Ask of each reference: should it move across, down, both or neither? Yearly inputs in a row need D$3.

  • Growing a part year as if it were a full year

    A business that opened part-way through the last actual year has a short year in the history. Annualise it (amount × 12 ÷ months) before applying growth, and say so in the label.

  • Building before knowing the question

    Starting from a template produces a large model that may still not answer the question asked (Coastline’s covenant, say). Write down the decision and the outputs first; they decide what the model needs.

  • Mixing units and signs

    A row with some costs negative and some positive, or one figure in thousands among dollars, adds up wrongly without any error message. State the unit and the sign convention once, at the top, and follow it everywhere.

In practice

At a bank like Coastline, the credit officer who receives Lantern’s model does not start with its answers. She starts with its inputs. Are the growth rates plausible against the history? Are the cost shares close to what the accounts show? Is the loan in the model the loan in the term sheet? Then she changes them: online growth halved, the kitchen opening three months late, costs 5% higher. A model built on the principles in this lesson answers each of those questions in seconds and shows the effect on the current ratio. A model with hard-coded numbers gives the same answer whatever she changes, which tells her it cannot be relied on.

The same habits matter inside a business. Ana will update the model every quarter with actual results, replacing estimates with facts. If the forecast years are driven by inputs and the history is in its own columns, that is a matter of typing the new actuals in and rolling the timeline forward. If the forecasts were typed in, it is a rebuild.

Professional modellers are judged less on clever formulas than on whether a stranger can audit their work. In large deals, the lender often pays an independent firm to review the borrower’s model before funds are released, and the first things such a review looks for are the ones in this lesson: hard-coded numbers, inconsistent formulas across a row, inputs mixed into calculations, and checks that do not come to zero.

Summary

  • A financial model answers a question about the future; write the decision and the outputs down before building anything.
  • Keep inputs, calculations and outputs apart, with every assumption in one labelled input cell and information flowing one way.
  • Run time across the columns in periods of equal length, marked actual (A) or estimate (E); annualise part years in the history and use month counts or flags for events that start part-way through a forecast year.
  • Write one formula per row, filled across, with relative references for last year, mixed references for each year’s inputs and absolute references for inputs every year shares.
  • Never type a number where a reference to an input belongs: hard-coded numbers are the commonest model error and the hardest to see.
  • Follow shared conventions (blue inputs, black formulas, consistent signs and units, short formulas) so that another person can read and check the model.
  • Add check rows that must always be zero, and avoid circular references by design.

Terms introduced

Financial model
A set of linked calculations, usually in a spreadsheet, that represents a business’s finances and answers questions about its future from stated assumptions.
Input
In a model, a value that is typed in rather than calculated: an actual figure from the accounts or an assumption about the future. Each input sits in one labelled cell that formulas refer to.
Hard-coded number
A value typed into a cell or a formula where a reference to an input belongs, so that it does not change when the inputs change.
Flag
A row in a model that marks, for each period, whether something applies (1 or 0) or for how many months, and is multiplied into the lines it governs.
