Debt schedules

Every loan has its own timetable of interest and repayments, and a line of credit fills whatever gap is left. Build Lantern’s debt schedule loan by loan, let the line of credit balance the cash, keep the model free of circular references, and measure whether the business can carry its debt.

The idea

Fictional case

At the end of 2027 Lantern owes money four ways: the first Coastline note ($10,000, due on 1 March 2028), the van note ($15,000, repaid $5,000 each June), the cold-brew brewer’s instalment plan ($16,250), and $24,000 drawn on Coastline’s $30,000 line of credit. In March 2028 it adds a fifth: the $60,000 term loan for the second kitchen, repaid monthly over five years. Hollis Avery also pays $75,000 for 30% of the company. Coastline wants to see each of these in the model, with every payment on the right date.

One schedule per loan

A debt schedule sets out, for each loan and each year, the balance at the start, any new borrowing, the repayments, the balance at the end and the interest. Loans with different terms need their own rows, because they are repaid on different timetables at different rates, and because the covenant and the balance sheet need to know how much of each is due within a year. The total of all the schedules gives the model’s interest expense, its debt repayments in the cash flow statement and its debt on the balance sheet.

Lantern’s loans show the three common shapes. The first Coastline note is a bullet: interest only, then the whole $10,000 at maturity. The van note repays a fixed $5,000 of principal each year with interest on the balance. The brewer plan and the new term loan are amortising loans: level payments that each cover the month’s interest and repay some principal, a little more each month.

The term loan, month by month into years

Coastline lends $60,000 on 31 March 2028 at 7.5% a year, repaid in 60 equal monthly payments at each month end from 30 April. The payment comes from the annuity formula of F04: 60,000×0.00625÷(1−1.00625−60)=1,202.2860{,}000 \times 0.00625 \div (1 - 1.00625^{-60}) = 1{,}202.2860,000×0.00625÷(1−1.00625−60)=1,202.28 dollars. The first payment carries 0.625% of $60,000, $375.00, of interest and repays $827.28. An annual model needs the totals for each calendar year, so it adds up the months: 2028 has nine payments (April to December), with interest of $3,186.12 and principal of $7,634.37, leaving $52,365.63 owed at the end of the year.

This is the reliable way to put any loan into an annual model: build its schedule at the frequency it is actually paid (monthly here), then sum each calendar year’s interest and principal, and read off the balance at each year end. Approximating with an annual rate on an annual balance would get the first year badly wrong, because the loan exists for only nine months of it.

The line of credit as the balancing item

A model forecasts every flow of cash except one: how much the business must borrow at short notice, or can repay, to end each year with the cash it needs. That job goes to a revolver, a revolving line of credit the model draws on and repays automatically. Ana sets a minimum cash balance of $15,000, enough for a month of wages and suppliers. Each year the model works out the cash Lantern would have before the line moves; if that is below the minimum, it draws the difference, and if above, it repays the line, never below zero and never above the $30,000 limit.

Line of creditt=min⁡(Limit, max⁡(0, Linet−1+Minimum cash−Cash before the linet))\text{Line of credit}_t = \min\Big(\text{Limit},\ \max\big(0,\ \text{Line}_{t-1} + \text{Minimum cash} - \text{Cash before the line}_t\big)\Big)Line of creditt​=min(Limit, max(0, Linet−1​+Minimum cash−Cash before the linet​))

In 2028 Lantern’s cash before the line moves would be $33,259: the $6,531 it starts with, plus cash from operations, less the kitchen spending and repayments, plus the term loan and Hollis’s money. That is more than the $15,000 minimum, so $18,259 can go to the line, cutting it from $24,000 to $5,741. In 2029 the remaining $5,741 is repaid in full.

In depth

Reading the term sheet into the model

A new loan’s terms come from the lender’s term sheet, and each term has a place in the schedule. The amount and the date it is drawn give the drawing and the first year’s months. The rate, and whether it is fixed or floating, gives the interest; a floating rate needs its own input row for each year, so that a rise in rates can be tested. The repayment profile (amortising, fixed principal, or a bullet at maturity) gives the principal each year. Fees paid to arrange the loan are a cost on the day it is drawn. And the covenants, such as Coastline’s current ratio of at least 1.2, become output rows that the model tests every period. Coastline’s term sheet for Lantern is simple: a fixed rate, level monthly payments and one covenant.

Interest owed at the year end

Interest expense and interest paid are not always the same in a year. Lantern’s van note pays its interest once a year, each 1 June, so at 31 December seven months of interest have been charged but not paid: $350 at the end of 2028, a current liability called interest payable. The brewer plan pays each month’s interest at the start of the next month, so one month is owed at each year end. The term loan and the line of credit pay within the month, so nothing is owed. The model works out the interest owed at each year end loan by loan; the cash paid in the year is then the expense plus what was owed at the start, less what is owed at the end. Lantern owed $819 at the end of 2027, all of it paid early in 2028.

Circular references, and how the model avoids them

The line of credit creates a loop if the model is careless. Its interest depends on its balance; the balance depends on how much cash the business needs; the cash need depends on profit; and profit depends on interest. If interest were charged on the average of the opening and closing balances, the formulas would refer to each other in a circle. Excel can solve such a loop by iteration, but circular models are slow, can fail to settle, and make every error harder to trace.

Lantern’s model breaks the loop by charging the line’s interest on its opening balance: 9% × $24,000 = $2,160 for 2028. The opening balance is already known when the year starts, so nothing loops. The cost is accuracy: Lantern repays most of the line during 2028, so interest on the average balance would be lower. For a small line the difference is small, and the model says which convention it uses. Larger models often compute interest on the average balance and break the circle with a switch that turns the calculation off, or with a copy-and-paste routine, but the principle is the same: never leave a circular reference without a reason and a way to break it.

What is due within a year

The balance sheet splits each loan into the current portion of long-term debt, the principal due in the next twelve months, and the rest. The debt schedule supplies it directly: the current portion at 31 December 2028 is next year’s principal. For Lantern it is $5,000 of the van note, $4,159 of the brewer plan (its last payments) and $10,868 of the term loan, together $20,027. That figure matters more than its size suggests, because Coastline’s covenant divides current assets by current liabilities, and the current portion is a current liability. Repaying debt faster can therefore make the current ratio worse before it makes it better.

Can the business carry its debt?

Lenders look at two kinds of measure. Leverage measures compare the debt with the business’s earnings or value: debt to EBITDA, or debt to equity. Coverage measures compare what the business earns with what its debt costs each year. The most common for a term loan is the debt service coverage ratio, earnings available to pay debt divided by the interest and scheduled principal due. Definitions vary from lender to lender; a simple one uses EBITDA.

Lantern’s DSCR is 0.56 in 2028 and 3.45 in 2029, rising to 10.64 by 2032. The 2028 figure is below 1: EBITDA does not cover that year’s interest and repayments, which include repaying the whole first Coastline note and most of the brewer plan. The money comes from the new loan and Hollis’s equity, which is exactly what they were raised for, but a credit committee will want that said plainly. From 2029 the business covers its debt service more than three times over.

Note: Equity is not in the debt schedule, but it is in the cash

Hollis Avery’s $75,000 for new shares is financing too. It goes to common stock on the balance sheet and to financing cash flows, not to the debt schedule, and it carries no interest or repayments. It is why Lantern can repay most of the line of credit in its loss year.

Common mistakes

  • Annual interest on an annual balance for a part year

    A loan drawn in March is outstanding for nine months of that year. Build the schedule at its payment frequency and sum the calendar year, or at least pro-rate by months.

  • Counting interest as repayment

    A loan payment is part interest (an expense) and part principal (a reduction in debt). Only the principal reduces the balance and goes to financing cash flows; the interest goes to the income statement.

  • A revolver with no limit

    A line of credit that draws whatever the model needs hides a funding gap. Cap it at the agreed limit and show any shortfall in its own row.

  • Leaving a circular reference in place

    Interest on average balances makes the model loop. Charge interest on opening balances, or add a switch that breaks the circle, and say which you did.

  • Forgetting the current portion

    The principal due within a year is a current liability. Leaving it in long-term debt flatters the current ratio, which is exactly what a covenant tests.

  • Treating equity as debt

    New shares bring cash but no interest or repayments. They belong in equity and financing cash flows, not in the debt schedule.

In practice

Banks build their own version of a borrower’s debt schedule before lending. A credit analyst takes the term sheet, the existing loans from the accounts’ notes and the borrower’s forecast, and checks three things: that every existing loan is in the model with its real terms, that the new loan’s payments are right, and that the forecast does not quietly assume the line of credit is bigger than it is. Coverage and leverage ratios from the schedule then go into the bank’s internal rating, which sets the price of the loan.

In leveraged buyouts and project finance the debt schedule is the centre of the model: several layers of debt, cash sweeps that use spare cash to repay the most expensive layer first, and covenants tested every quarter. The principles are the ones in this lesson, applied to more loans.

Summary

  • A debt schedule gives each loan its own rows: opening balance, drawings, repayments, closing balance and interest for each year.
  • Build a loan at the frequency it is paid and sum each calendar year; part years matter.
  • An amortising loan’s level payment is P r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n); interest is front-loaded.
  • A revolver balances the model: it draws to keep cash at a minimum and repays when cash allows, within its limit; a need beyond the limit is a funding gap to show, not hide.
  • Charging interest on opening balances avoids circular references at a small cost in accuracy; state the convention.
  • The current portion of long-term debt is next year’s principal, a current liability that covenants count.
  • The debt service coverage ratio compares earnings with interest plus scheduled principal; read it with the reasons behind each year.

Terms introduced

Debt schedule
The part of a model that tracks each loan’s opening balance, drawings, repayments, closing balance and interest for each period, and supplies interest expense, debt cash flows and the current portion of debt.
Revolver
In a model, a revolving line of credit used as the balancing item: drawn when cash would fall below a minimum and repaid when cash allows, within its limit.
Minimum cash balance
The least cash a business plans to hold at each period end, set by the payments it must be able to make at short notice.
Debt service coverage ratio
A measure of a borrower’s ability to pay its debt: earnings available for debt service (often EBITDA, sometimes after tax or capital spending) divided by the interest and scheduled principal due in the period.
