Model review and presentation

A model is only useful if someone else can trust it and understand what it says. Review Lantern’s model the way a lender’s analyst would, with integrity checks, formula consistency and reasonableness tests, find errors from their symptoms, and present the result on one page that leads with the answer.

The idea

Fictional case

Before Ana sends her model and proposal to Coastline, Hollis Avery asks his accountant to look over it. Ana built it carefully and every check row reads zero, but she has been staring at it for three weeks, which is exactly when a builder stops seeing her own mistakes. A review by someone else is the last line of defence, and the bank will do its own anyway.

Why models need reviewing

Spreadsheet errors are common, and the people who build spreadsheets tend to be confident that theirs are right. Research on operational spreadsheets, summarised by Raymond Panko, found errors in a large share of the models examined and found that developers consistently overestimated how accurate their work was. The errors that matter in a financial model are seldom typing slips that a calculator would catch. They are a hard-coded number that stopped updating, a range that misses a row, a sign the wrong way round, a part year treated as a full one, or an assumption that is internally consistent but unrealistic. Each of the lessons in this course has shown one of them.

A review has five layers, from the mechanical to the judgemental, and an experienced reviewer works through them in order, because a model that fails the mechanical checks is not worth arguing about.

  1. Structure: inputs in one place, labelled with sources; calculations separate; outputs clear; units and signs stated.
  2. Integrity: the check rows are zero in every period: assets equal liabilities plus equity, the cash flow statement’s cash equals the balance sheet’s, retained earnings roll forward.
  3. Consistency: every forecast cell in a row has the same formula; no typed numbers among formulas; no circular references.
  4. Reasonableness: the forecast’s ratios make sense against the history and against what the business plans.
  5. Documentation: someone else can tell what the model is for, what each input means, and what has changed since the last version.

Integrity checks prove that the model is internally consistent. They do not prove that it is right: a model can balance perfectly and still forecast sales that will never happen. That is what the reasonableness layer is for.

In depth

Reasonableness: does the forecast make sense?

A reviewer compares the forecast with the history and asks whether each change has a reason. The useful measures are those that should be stable unless something changes them: margins, days of working capital, capital spending against depreciation, growth against the market.

The reviewer’s notes on Lantern would read something like this. Revenue growth of 48% in 2028 is explained by three new activities, then slows sensibly. Gross margin is stable because each line keeps its cost share. The EBITDA margin’s dip and recovery is explained by Cold Brew’s launch. Capital spending is below depreciation from 2029 to 2031 (about 0.58 times): defensible because the 2028 investment is new and will not need replacing for years, but worth a sentence in the notes, and it explains why the 2032 refit is large. No measure moves without a reason.

Reading errors from their symptoms

When a check row is not zero, its pattern points to the cause. A difference equal to one year’s figure for some item, appearing in one year only, suggests that item is missing from one statement. A difference that grows by a similar amount each year suggests a recurring flow is mishandled, and a cumulative pattern (the difference equals the running total of something) points straight at it: if the balance check equals minus the running total of dividends, dividends were paid in cash but never deducted from retained earnings. A difference of twice an item suggests a sign error. A reviewer forms a hypothesis from the pattern, then checks the formula, which is far faster than reading every cell.

Note: Formula consistency at a glance

Excel’s Show Formulas view, or a row of formulas compared with the row’s first forecast cell, reveals a different formula in a row in seconds. Many reviewers also colour-test: copy the model, delete every input, and see which cells still show numbers. Any that do were typed in.

Testing with extreme inputs

A quick way to find logic errors is to give the model inputs whose answer is obvious and see whether it agrees. Set every growth rate to zero: revenue should stay flat at its base, and nothing should jump. Set the term loan to zero: the line of credit should carry the strain, and if it cannot, the funding gap row should say so rather than the cash going negative. Set the tax rate to zero: tax should vanish and every after-tax figure should rise by exactly the tax it no longer pays. Set the interest rate to zero: interest should disappear without anything else changing except profit, tax and cash. Each test takes seconds, and a model that gives a surprising answer to an obvious question has a broken formula somewhere in the chain the test exercised.

The scenario explorer in the previous lesson is also a test harness: pushing each slider to its limits and watching for impossible results (negative inventory, a line of credit above its limit, a current ratio that rises when sales fall) is part of reviewing the logic, not only the numbers.

Comparing the model with what happens

Once the year starts, the model has a new job. Each quarter Ana types the actual results into the history columns and compares them with the forecast, line by line. The differences are variances: revenue lines ahead or behind, costs over or under, working capital days drifting. Large or persistent variances are evidence about the assumptions, and the forecast for the remaining years should be updated with what they teach, not left as it was in February. Lenders often require exactly this: a quarterly comparison of actual results with the forecast that supported the loan, with an explanation of any large differences.

Presenting the model

The people who decide rarely read the model; they read what the modeller tells them it says. A good one-page summary leads with the answer to their question, then gives the evidence and the risks, in that order. For Coastline the question is whether to lend, and on what terms.

  • The answer: Lantern asks for an $80,000 term loan with a stepped covenant (current ratio at least 0.9 at the end of 2028, 1.0 in 2029, 1.2 from 2030).
  • The evidence: in the base case the current ratio is 1.24, 1.74, 2.47, 3.21, 4.00 at the year ends 2028–2032; debt service coverage is 2.9 times in 2029 and rising; the line of credit is repaid in 2029.
  • The risks: under slow growth the ratio is 0.94 in 2028 and 1.01 in 2029, inside the stepped covenant with little headroom; the annual model does not show quarter ends, so Lantern will send a quarterly cash forecast for 2028.
  • The key assumptions, each with its source: growth by line, cost shares, working capital days, capital spending, the loan’s terms and the tax rate.
  • The checks: the model balances and its cash and retained earnings tie in every year.

Charts help when they show one thing each: the current ratio against the covenant line by scenario, cash by year, EBITDA and its margin. A model dashboard collects those charts and the key outputs on one page that updates when the inputs change, so the summary and the model can never disagree.

Documentation and versions

A model that only its builder understands is a liability. The cover sheet says what the model is for, who built it, its version and date, its units and its main simplifications (here: book depreciation used for tax, the second kitchen’s rent expensed, the line of credit charged on opening balances, an annual model for a quarterly covenant). A change log records what changed in each version and why. Every version sent outside the business is saved and never edited again, so that when Coastline refers to “the forecast you sent in February”, both sides are looking at the same numbers.

Common mistakes

  • Reviewing your own model and calling it reviewed

    Builders see what they meant to build. A review needs someone else, or at least a break and a checklist.

  • Treating integrity checks as proof of accuracy

    A model can balance and still forecast the impossible. Integrity first, then reasonableness.

  • Reading every cell instead of the symptoms

    Start from the check rows and the reasonableness measures; their patterns point to the error far faster than reading formulas one by one.

  • A summary that buries the answer

    Decision-makers read the first lines. Lead with the request or conclusion, then the evidence, then the risks.

  • Charts that try to show everything

    One message per chart: the covenant against its limit, cash by year. A chart with ten series is a table that is harder to read.

  • Editing a version that has been sent

    Once a model has gone to a lender or investor, freeze that version. Make changes in a new one and log them.

In practice

Large financings often require an independent model audit: an accounting or specialist firm reviews the model before the lenders commit, and its report lists every error, inconsistency and unsupported assumption. Smaller lenders do the same work internally. In both cases the reviewer’s first request is the model’s documentation and its check rows.

Inside companies, the same discipline applies to budgets and board papers. A finance team that sends the board a model that does not balance, or a summary that disagrees with its own appendix, loses credibility that takes years to rebuild. Simple habits (a checks block at the top, a frozen copy of everything sent, a change log) prevent most of it.

Summary

  • Review a model in layers: structure, integrity, consistency, reasonableness, documentation.
  • Integrity checks (balance check, cash link, retained earnings roll-forward) must be zero every period; they prove consistency, not accuracy.
  • Reasonableness compares the forecast with the history: stable measures should move only for a reason.
  • The pattern of a non-zero check identifies the error: one year, recurring, cumulative or twice an item.
  • Present the answer first, then the evidence, then the risks, with one message per chart.
  • Document the model and freeze every version that leaves the business.

Terms introduced

Model audit
An independent review of a financial model’s structure, formulas, assumptions and outputs, often required by lenders before a large financing.
Model dashboard
A one-page view of a model’s key outputs and charts, linked to the model so that it updates when the inputs change.
Change log
A record kept with a model of what changed in each version, when and why.
