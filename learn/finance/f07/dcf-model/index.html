A DCF model from the forecast

The forecast that convinces a lender can also value the business. Turn Lantern’s model into free cash flow to the firm, discount it at the cost of capital, add a terminal value built on a normalised year, bridge from enterprise value to equity, and test the answer against what Hollis Avery actually paid.

The idea

Fictional case

Hollis Avery is paying $75,000 for 30% of Lantern. That puts a value of $250,000 on the whole company after his money arrives, and $175,000 before it. Coastline does not care what Lantern is worth, only whether it can pay. Ana does: she is selling almost a third of her business. The forecast she built for the bank can answer her question too, because a discounted cash flow valuation needs exactly what the model already has.

From the model to free cash flow

Free cash flow to the firm (FCFF) is the cash the business’s operations generate after the investment they need, before any payments to lenders or shareholders. It is what all the providers of capital, together, could take out each year. The model supplies every part of it: operating profit (EBIT), the tax on it, depreciation, capital spending and the change in working capital.

FCFF=EBIT×(1−t)+Depreciation−Capital spending−ΔWorking capital\text{FCFF} = \text{EBIT}\times(1-t) + \text{Depreciation} - \text{Capital spending} - \Delta\text{Working capital}FCFF=EBIT×(1−t)+Depreciation−Capital spending−ΔWorking capital

Two choices make the FCFF consistent with the discount rate. Tax is charged on EBIT, not on the model’s pre-tax profit, as if Lantern had no debt; the tax saved by deducting interest is counted in the WACC instead, which uses the after-tax cost of debt. And interest and loan repayments are left out entirely, because they are payments to one group of the providers whose combined claim FCFF measures. In 2028 EBIT is negative, so the model charges no operating tax (and, conservatively, gives no value to the loss carried forward).

FCFF is negative in 2028 because of the $105,000 invested in the second kitchen and Cold Brew, positive and growing from 2029, and dips in 2032 when the cafés are refitted. A valuation that discounted only the first year, or only the best year, would get Lantern badly wrong; the model’s five years show the whole pattern.

Discounting at the cost of capital

F05 estimated Lantern’s weighted average cost of capital after the new financing at 11.16%: the return its providers of capital require, given the risk of its peers’ businesses, a premium for being small and private, and the mix of debt and equity it will have. Each year’s FCFF is multiplied by its discount factor, 1÷(1+0.1116)t1 \div (1 + 0.1116)^t1÷(1+0.1116)t, as if it arrived at the year end. The five present values add up to $123,675.

In depth

The terminal value, from a normalised year

Lantern will not stop in 2032, so the valuation adds a terminal value: the value at the end of 2032 of all the free cash flow after it. The usual method assumes FCFF grows at a constant rate g for ever, and uses the growing perpetuity from F04. Ana assumes 2.5% a year, below the economy’s long-run nominal growth, because no business can outgrow the economy for ever.

The trap is the year the perpetuity grows from. Lantern’s 2032 FCFF is unusually low, because it includes the $54,000 café refit; growing it for ever would undervalue the business. The model therefore builds a normalised first year after the forecast: NOPAT grows by 2.5%, capital spending equals depreciation (assets are replaced as they wear out), and working capital grows with sales, using 2.5% of 2032’s working capital of $16,093.

FCFF2033=NOPAT2032(1+g)−g×NWC2032\text{FCFF}_{2033} = \text{NOPAT}_{2032}(1+g) - g \times \text{NWC}_{2032}FCFF2033​=NOPAT2032​(1+g)−g×NWC2032​

That gives $79,804 × 1.025 − 0.025 × 16,093 = $81,397. The terminal value at the end of 2032 is 81,397÷(0.1116−0.025)=939,82981{,}397 \div (0.1116 - 0.025) = 939{,}82981,397÷(0.1116−0.025)=939,829 dollars, worth $553,720 today after five years of discounting.

Timing: the valuation date and mid-year flows

A DCF values cash flows at one date, here 31 December 2027, the day the history ends. Each forecast year’s FCFF is treated as arriving on the last day of the year, so 2028’s is discounted for one full year and 2032’s for five. In reality cash arrives through the year, and many practitioners use the mid-year convention, discounting each year’s cash for half a year less (t − 0.5 instead of t). That raises the value by roughly half a year of the discount rate, about 5% for Lantern. Neither convention is wrong; the model must say which it uses, and Lantern’s uses year-end flows, the more conservative of the two.

The valuation date also decides which balances count as net debt. The model subtracts the debt and cash of 31 December 2027, before the term loan is drawn and before Hollis pays. The forecast’s cash flows already include the second kitchen’s cost, so the value is that of Lantern with its plan, financed in whatever way it is. The new loan does not change the enterprise value: it is a way of paying for investments the FCFF already deducts.

Pre-money and post-money

Investors quote two values. The pre-money value is the business before the new investment; the post-money value adds the cash invested. Hollis’s $75,000 for 30% means a post-money value of $75,000 ÷ 0.30 = $250,000, and a pre-money value of $250,000 − $75,000 = $175,000. The model’s equity value at the end of 2027 is a pre-money figure, because the forecast deducts the investment his money pays for without counting his money as the company’s. That is the like-for-like comparison: $618,676 from the model against $175,000 from the deal.

From enterprise value to equity value

Adding the two parts gives the enterprise value, the value of the business’s operations to all its providers of capital: $123,675 + $553,720 = $677,395. Equity is what is left after the lenders: subtract the debt and add the cash at the valuation date, 31 December 2027. Lantern owed $41,250 on its loans and $24,000 on the line of credit, and had $6,531 of cash, so its net debt was $58,719. Equity value: $677,395 − $58,719 = $618,676.

Key idea: Leases: be consistent

Lantern’s café leases are not subtracted as debt here, because their cost (the $48,000 a year in occupancy) is already deducted before EBITDA, so the free cash flow is after paying them. A valuation can instead treat leases as debt, but then it must add the lease cost back to the cash flows and use a cost of capital that includes leases. Doing one without the other counts the leases twice.

Testing the answer

The terminal value is 82% of Lantern’s enterprise value. That is common for a young business whose cash flows come later, and it is a warning: most of the value rests on what happens after 2032, through two assumptions, the discount rate and the growth rate. Two checks help. The implied exit multiple divides the terminal value by the final year’s EBITDA: $939,829 ÷ $153,477 = 6.1 times. Brightcup, the listed chain in F06, trades at about 11.6 times its EBITDA; a small private business would normally be valued well below a listed chain, so 6.1 times is not obviously aggressive. The second check is a range of discount rates: at 9% the equity is worth $890,591; at 15%, $370,447.

What Hollis paid, and why

The model values Lantern’s equity at $618,676 before Hollis’s money, against the $175,000 his price implies. Either Ana sold too cheaply, or the model and the investor are answering different questions. Both are partly true. Hollis requires 20% a year on his investment (F05), far above the WACC, because he holds a large share of one small, illiquid business whose plan may not happen, and because he will help run it. Discounted at 20%, the same forecast gives an equity value of $213,984, close to what he paid. The CAPM-based WACC assumes a diversified investor who can sell at any time; Hollis is neither. And the forecast is Ana’s, made by the person who most wants it to come true.

The lesson is not that the DCF is wrong, but that a value is a conclusion from assumptions. A model lets each side see exactly which assumption separates their numbers, here mostly the discount rate, and argue about that instead of about the answer.

Common mistakes

  • Deducting interest from free cash flow to the firm

    FCFF is before any payment to lenders; interest is accounted for in the WACC. Deducting it as well counts the cost of debt twice.

  • Growing an unusual final year for ever

    A terminal value built on a year with heavy capital spending, or a one-off release of cash, multiplies that oddity for ever. Normalise the year first.

  • A growth rate close to the discount rate

    As g approaches the WACC the terminal value explodes. Long-run growth should be at or below the economy’s nominal growth.

  • Mixing lease treatments

    Subtracting lease liabilities as debt while the cash flows are already after lease payments counts the leases twice. Treat them one way throughout.

  • Netting the wrong date’s debt

    Subtract net debt at the valuation date, not at the end of the forecast; the forecast’s repayments are already financed by the cash flows being valued.

  • Reporting one number

    A DCF is a range. Show how the value moves with the discount rate and long-run growth, and the terminal value’s share.

In practice

Private-company valuations are usually negotiated, not calculated. Founders, investors and their advisers each build or adjust a model like Lantern’s, and the discussion focuses on the few assumptions that move the value most: growth, margins, the discount rate and the terminal value. Venture and growth investors often skip the WACC and discount the founder’s plan at a high target return, 20% to 40% or more, precisely because they expect plans to be optimistic.

In the 10-K-based valuations analysts publish for listed companies, the same structure appears with more detail: segment forecasts, a sensitivity table of value against WACC and growth, and a comparison with trading multiples. The method is identical; only the scale differs.

Summary

  • FCFF = EBIT × (1 − t) + depreciation − capital spending − increase in working capital: before any payment to lenders or shareholders.
  • Tax is charged on EBIT; the interest tax shield is in the WACC. Interest and repayments stay out of FCFF.
  • Discount each year’s FCFF at the WACC; add a terminal value built on a normalised year, growing at a rate below the WACC.
  • Enterprise value less net debt at the valuation date is equity value; treat leases the same way in the cash flows and in net debt.
  • Test the result: the terminal value’s share, the implied exit multiple, and a range of discount and growth rates.
  • A price actually paid can differ widely from a DCF; the model shows which assumption, here mostly the discount rate, explains the gap.

Terms introduced

Implied exit multiple
The terminal value of a DCF divided by the final forecast year’s EBITDA (or another measure): the multiple at which the valuation implicitly assumes the business could be sold, used as a sense check.
NOPAT
Net operating profit after tax: operating profit (EBIT) less the tax that would be due on it if the business had no debt.
Normalised terminal year
The first year after a forecast, rebuilt to represent a steady state (capital spending equal to depreciation, working capital growing with sales) before a terminal value is calculated from it.
