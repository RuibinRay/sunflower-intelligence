Setting up the history

A forecast is only as good as the history it starts from. Learn how to condense a set of accounts into the lines a model needs, find the ratios that will drive the forecast, adjust for one-off items and part years, and know where in an annual report each figure comes from.

About 60 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Prepare a condensed set of financial statements in the lines a model needs, and check that the condensed figures add back to the reported totals.
  • Calculate the historical ratios that become a forecast’s drivers: cost shares, margins and growth rates.
  • Normalise the history for one-off items and part years, and explain why reported growth can mislead after a structural change.
  • Locate in a Form 10-K the information a model needs: segment revenue, debt maturities, leases, risks and management’s outlook.
  • Evaluate whether a historical ratio is a sound basis for a forecast.

Review first

  • Modelling principles — The layout of the model the history goes into: inputs, a timeline of actual and forecast years, one formula per row.
  • Common-size statements — Expressing each line as a share of revenue, which is how most cost drivers are measured.
  • Benchmarking and annual reports — What an annual report and a Form 10-K contain, and how to read the notes.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana’s accountant sent Lantern’s 2027 income statement with over twenty lines: four kinds of revenue, a contra account for returns, two kinds of cost of sales, thirteen operating expenses, interest and tax. The 2026 statement has a different set of lines, because there were no cafés. Before forecasting anything, Ana has to put both years into the shape of her model, so that each forecast line has a history beside it. Coastline’s credit officer will read the model that way, comparing each forecast year with the actual years to its left.

What the history does in a model

The actual years at the left of a model do three jobs. They are the starting point: 2028’s forecast grows from 2027, and the balance sheet at 31 December 2027 is the opening balance sheet for 2028. They supply the drivers: the ratios between lines (cost of goods as a share of sales, wages per café) that the forecast assumes will continue or change. And they are the test of credibility: a forecast that jumps away from the history needs a reason, and a reader looks for one.

For all three jobs the history has to be in the same lines as the forecast. A model does not need every account in the ledger; it needs the lines that behave differently and are worth forecasting separately. Condensing too much hides what drives the business; keeping every account makes the model large without making it better.

Condensing the statements

Condensing means mapping each reported line to a model line and adding up. Lantern’s model keeps revenue in its four lines, because each grows for different reasons. Returns are netted against online sales, where they arise. Cost of sales stays in two lines, goods and services, because they follow different revenue lines. Operating expenses are grouped by what drives them: wages, occupancy (rent and the cafés’ lease cost), advertising, market research, and a group of smaller costs that move with sales (utilities, insurance, fuel, shipping, card fees, bad debts and sundries). Depreciation, interest and tax stay separate, because module 2 forecasts each from its own schedule.

The totals must agree with the reported statements to the dollar: revenue of $577,860, EBIT of $32,787 and net income of $22,431 in 2027. If they do not, a line has been dropped or counted twice. This check takes a minute and catches the commonest error in setting up a model.

Ratios that become drivers

With the history in model lines, each cost can be measured against whatever drives it. A driver is the quantity a line is forecast from, and the ratio between them is the assumption. Café cost of goods was 31% of café sales in 2027 (a figure from F03’s records). Taking café cost of goods out of the total leaves online cost of goods: 163,125−0.31×236,000=89,965163{,}125 - 0.31 \times 236{,}000 = 89{,}965163,125−0.31×236,000=89,965 dollars, or 48.0% of online sales. Cost of services was 39.6% of catering and training revenue together. Other operating costs were 9.1% of revenue, and advertising 2.0%.

Gross margin rose from 54.0% in 2026 to 61.2% in 2027. That is not because each product became more profitable; it is because the mix changed. The cafés, whose goods cost only 31% of sales, did not exist in 2026. A ratio for the whole business is an average of its parts, and it moves whenever the weights of the parts move. That is why the model forecasts each revenue line with its own cost share and lets the total margin follow.

In depth

The opening balance sheet

The income statement history supplies drivers; the balance sheet history supplies the starting point. Every balance in the forecast begins from the balance at 31 December 2027: cash, receivables, inventory, equipment, the line of credit, each loan. Lantern’s balance sheet that day totals $327,883 of assets, against $265,065 of liabilities and $62,818 of equity. The model condenses it the same way as the income statement, into the lines its schedules will roll forward in module 2.

  • Working capital lines (receivables net of the allowance, the contract asset, inventory, supplies and prepayments, payables, wages and interest payable, unearned revenue, taxes payable) feed the working capital schedule, which forecasts each from days or a share of sales.
  • Property and equipment (equipment, leasehold improvements and the van, at cost less accumulated depreciation) feed the capital spending and depreciation schedule.
  • Debt (the line of credit, the notes and the instalment note, current and long-term parts together) feeds the debt schedule, loan by loan, with each loan’s rate and repayments.
  • Leases. The cafés’ right-of-use assets ($173,379) and lease liabilities ($173,379) are large; Lantern’s model keeps them as their own lines and lets them run down with the lease payments, because Coastline’s covenant leaves lease liabilities out of the current ratio.
  • Equity (common stock and retained earnings) rolls forward with profit, dividends and new shares.

The same check applies: the condensed lines must add up to the reported totals, and assets must equal liabilities plus equity. A model whose opening balance sheet does not balance can never balance in any forecast year, however carefully the forecasts are built.

When reported growth misleads

Lantern’s revenue grew from $193,320 to $577,860: 198.9% in a year. No forecast should extrapolate that, because most of it came from opening two cafés, a one-time change in the shape of the business. Growth rates in the history are useful only for lines whose business did not change. Online sales grew 70.5% and catering 89.9%: closer to an ongoing trend, though still from a very young business, which is why Ana’s forecast assumes much slower growth.

The same caution applies to margins. EBITDA was 22.0% of revenue in 2026 and 9.1% in 2027. A reader who sees only those two numbers concludes the business got worse. In fact 2027 carried two things that will not recur in the same way: a one-off market research project and two cafés that traded for only ten months against costs that started before they opened.

Normalising the history

Normalising means adjusting the history to show what a typical year would look like, by removing items that will not recur and restating part periods as full ones. It produces the base a forecast should grow from. It does not change the reported accounts, which stay in the history columns as reported; the adjustments sit in their own rows, labelled, so that a reader can see and challenge each one.

  • One-off items. Lantern spent $21,000 on market research in 2027, including the May 2027 needs study behind Cold Brew; Ana plans none for 2028–2032, so she treats it as a one-off. Adding it back gives a normalised 2027 EBITDA of 52,587 + 21,000 = 73,587 dollars, a margin of 12.7%. Gains are treated the same way in the other direction: a profit on selling a van would be taken out.
  • Part years. The cafés traded ten months. Their sales, café wages ($92,000) and lease cost ($40,000) are each annualised: × 12 ÷ 10 gives $283,200 of sales, $110,400 of wages and $48,000 of lease cost.
  • Changes in accounting. If a business changes an accounting policy (how it depreciates, when it recognises revenue), the two years are not comparable until restated on one basis. Lantern’s two years use the same policies.

Caution: Every adjustment is a judgement

Companies often present an “adjusted” figure that adds back costs they call one-off. Some are; some recur every year under a different name. F02 called this adjusted EBITDA and warned that its definition is the company’s own. In your own model, show each adjustment on its own row with its reason, and be as willing to adjust for one-off gains as for one-off costs.

Where the figures come from: the Form 10-K

Lantern is private, so its history comes from its own accounts. For a listed US company the source is its annual report on Form 10-K, filed with the SEC. A large accelerated filer must file within 60 days of its year end, an accelerated filer within 75 days and other companies within 90. The form has a fixed structure, so the same information is always in the same place. These items are the ones a modeller reads most:

The notes in Item 8 are where most modelling inputs hide. The debt note gives each borrowing’s rate and the repayments due in each of the next five years, exactly what a debt schedule needs. The lease note gives future lease payments. The segment note splits revenue and profit by business line. A model built from the face of the statements alone misses all of this.

Testing a ratio before using it

A historical ratio is a good driver when three things are true: there is a reason for it (cost of goods rises with sales because each sale uses goods); it has been reasonably stable; and nothing planned will change it. Lantern has only two years, one of them with no cafés, so its ratios are thin evidence. Ana combines them with what she knows: her supplier’s price list, the cafés’ menu costing and the co-packer’s quote for Cold Brew. When the history and the business knowledge disagree, the model should follow the knowledge and say so in the input’s label.

Common mistakes

  • Condensing without checking the totals

    Mapping twenty lines into ten is where lines get lost or counted twice. Rebuild gross profit, EBITDA, EBIT and net income from the condensed lines and compare them with the reported statements before using any of them.

  • Extrapolating growth across a structural change

    Lantern’s 199% growth in 2027 came from opening two cafés. Use historical growth only for lines whose business did not change, and forecast new activities from their own drivers.

  • Reading a total-company margin as if it were one product’s

    A margin for the whole business moves when the mix moves. Measure cost shares line by line (café goods against café sales) and let the total margin follow from the forecast mix.

  • Overwriting the reported figures with normalised ones

    Adjust in separate, labelled rows. The reported accounts must stay visible so that a reader can see what you changed and why.

  • Treating every cost the company calls one-off as one-off

    Some “one-off” costs recur every year under different names. Ask whether a similar cost is likely in a typical future year; if so, keep it in the forecast.

  • Using only the face of the statements

    Debt repayments, lease payments, segment results and planned capital spending are in the notes and in MD&A, not on the statements themselves. A model that ignores them gets its schedules wrong.

In practice

Equity analysts who cover a listed company spend much of the week after it files its 10-K updating their models: typing in the new year’s actuals, re-condensing any lines the company has changed, and recalculating the drivers. The adjustments they make for one-offs are often the most debated part of their reports, because two analysts reading the same accounts can disagree about what is typical.

Lenders work the same way from the other side. A credit analyst reviewing Lantern’s model will first check that its 2027 column agrees with the accounts Lantern sent, then look hard at every normalising adjustment, because each one makes the base the forecast grows from look better. An add-back with a clear reason, such as a research project that has finished, is accepted. An add-back without one, or a pattern of “one-off” costs every year, is a warning sign.

Summary

  • The history is the model’s starting point, the source of its drivers and the test of its credibility; it must be in the same lines as the forecast.
  • Condense the statements by mapping each reported line to a model line, grouping costs by what drives them, and check that the condensed totals agree with the reported ones.
  • A driver is what a line is forecast from; the historical ratio between them (a cost share, a margin) is the starting assumption.
  • Total-company margins move with the mix; measure cost shares line by line.
  • Reported growth and margins mislead after a structural change; use them only where the business did not change.
  • Normalise for one-off items and part years in separate, labelled rows, and leave the reported figures visible.
  • For a listed US company, Form 10-K holds the inputs: the business and segments in Item 1, risks in Item 1A, explanations and plans in Item 7, market risk in Item 7A, and the statements and their notes in Item 8.

Terms introduced

Driver
In a forecast, the quantity a line item is calculated from, such as sales for cost of goods or the number of shops for revenue. The ratio between the line and its driver is the forecast’s assumption.
Normalising
Adjusting historical figures to show a typical year: removing one-off costs and gains and restating part periods as full ones, in separate rows beside the reported figures.
Condensing
Mapping the many lines of a set of accounts into the fewer lines a model forecasts, by grouping items that behave alike, and checking that the totals still agree.
