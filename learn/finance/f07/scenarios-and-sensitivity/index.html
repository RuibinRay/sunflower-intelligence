Scenarios and sensitivity

A forecast is one possible future. Build scenarios into the model so that a lender can see what happens when growth is slower or a kitchen opens late, find the input that just meets a covenant, rank which assumptions matter most, and use the answers to choose a financing plan that survives a bad year.

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Design scenarios as consistent sets of assumptions that run through the whole model, without copying the model.
  • Calculate the value of an input that just meets a target, such as the smallest loan that keeps a covenant, with Goal Seek or a search.
  • Construct a two-way sensitivity table and a tornado chart, and identify the assumptions that matter most.
  • Evaluate a financing plan by stress testing it, and recommend terms that survive a realistic bad case.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana’s model has told her that the base case breaches Coastline’s covenant at the end of 2028, with a current ratio of 1.01. Before she goes back to the bank she wants answers to the questions its credit committee will ask: what if sales grow more slowly? What if the kitchen opens late? How much would she need to borrow to pass, and would that still be enough in a bad year?

Three tools for “what if?”

A model answers what-if questions in three ways, and each answers a different question. Scenario analysis changes several assumptions together into a consistent story (slow growth: online sales, café sales and Cold Brew all weaker) and asks what the whole model says. Sensitivity analysis changes one assumption at a time to see how much an output depends on it. A stress test asks what has to go wrong, and how badly, before a limit is broken: a covenant, the line of credit, cash going below zero.

Scenarios must be consistent. A recession scenario in which sales fall but costs stay at boom-time levels is an error, but so is one in which everything bad happens at once with no reason. Each scenario should be a story someone could tell about the next five years, and its inputs should follow from that story.

Scenarios belong inside the model

The commonest way to build scenarios, saving a copy of the model for each, is also the worst: the copies drift apart as soon as one is corrected, and nobody can be sure the scenarios differ only in their assumptions. Instead, the model keeps one set of calculations and a small block of scenario inputs (the online growth multiplier, the café growth rate, the Cold Brew volume multiplier, the kitchen’s opening month, the loan amount), with a selector that chooses which column of inputs the model uses. Lantern’s scenarios change only those inputs; every schedule and statement recalculates.

In depth

Choosing the scenarios

Most models carry three to five scenarios. A base case is the plan the business is actually pursuing, built on its best estimates, not on hopes. A downside case asks what a realistic bad outcome looks like: not the worst imaginable, but one that would surprise nobody if it happened, such as a year of slower growth. An upside case keeps the plan honest from the other side, and shows whether success itself creates problems, such as needing more working capital or capacity sooner. Event scenarios test a single risk the business knows it faces: the kitchen opening late, a key supplier raising prices, losing the law firm’s contract.

For a lender the downside case matters most, because the lender gains nothing from the upside. For an investor the spread between downside and upside matters, because it shows how much is at stake. Lantern’s slow-growth scenario is its downside: online growth half of plan, cafés at 2% a year, and a quarter fewer Cold Brew cans, each plausible after a weak year for consumer spending.

What an annual model cannot show

Coastline tests the covenant at each quarter end, but Lantern’s model has one column a year. A business whose year-end ratio passes can still fail at the end of March or September, if cash dips during the year: before a large annual payment, say, or during a seasonal low. An annual model cannot see that. The fix for a covenant this close is a quarterly or monthly cash model for the first year or two, built from the same assumptions, with the annual model taking over after that. Ana’s proposal says so, and commits to sending Coastline a quarterly cash forecast for 2028.

Finding the input that just meets the target

Often the useful question is not “what happens if?” but “what would it take?”. Excel’s Goal Seek changes one input until a formula reaches a target value. Here the target is a condition, every year’s current ratio at least 1.2, so the model searches: it tries loan amounts, keeps the half of the range in which the answer lies, and repeats until the range is a dollar wide. The base case passes in every year with a term loan of at least $76,481; under slow growth it needs $107,940.

A borrower should not ask for the exact break-even amount: covenants need covenant headroom, a margin for the forecast being wrong. With $80,000, Lantern’s base case ratios are 1.24, 1.74, 2.47, 3.21, 4.00: comfortably above 1.2 in every year. Under slow growth, $80,000 would still leave 0.94 in 2028 and 1.01 in 2029.

One assumption at a time: the tornado

A tornado chart ranks assumptions by their effect. Each is moved to a plausible low and high value while everything else stays at the base case, and the resulting range of the output is drawn as a bar; the longest bars go at the top, which gives the chart its shape. For Lantern’s 2028 covenant the ranking is:

Cold Brew’s volume matters most to 2028, because the launch costs are fixed while its sales are not: every can short is margin lost against costs already committed. For the equity value the ranking is different: growth in the established lines matters more over five years, and the discount rate, below, matters more than any of them. A tornado is only as good as its ranges; giving one assumption a range twice as wide as the others will put it at the top whatever its real importance.

Weighting scenarios

Some analysts go one step further and weight each scenario by a probability, to give an expected value: if Ana thought the base case 60% likely, slow growth 30% and an upside 10%, the probability-weighted equity value would be the sum of each value times its weight. Expected values are useful for an investor comparing opportunities, because they put upside and downside on one scale. They are much less useful for a lender, whose concern is the downside alone, and they hide the fact that the probabilities are guesses. A covenant either holds or it does not; an average of a pass and a breach is not a meaningful number. That is why Lantern’s proposal to Coastline shows the scenarios side by side instead of averaging them, and why the stress test, which asks how bad things can get before the plan breaks, is the more useful tool for the bank.

From stress test to recommendation

Stress testing ends in a decision. Ana’s options are to borrow more, to cut or delay spending, or to negotiate the covenant. The model can show each. A $80,000 loan passes the base case in every year with headroom. Slow growth breaks the 1.2 covenant in 2028 and 2029 even then, but at 0.94 and 1.01 it would pass a covenant that starts at 0.9 for 2028, rises to 1.0 for 2029 and reaches 1.2 from 2030, a step-up that lenders often agree to for a business in its investment year.

Caution: Sensitivity is not probability

A scenario table says what happens if; it does not say how likely each scenario is. Weighting scenarios by probabilities, or running a Monte Carlo simulation, can help, but the probabilities are judgements too. The value of a stress test is that it shows the limits of a plan before reality does.

Common mistakes

  • A copy of the model for each scenario

    Copies drift apart. Keep one model and a block of scenario inputs that it reads.

  • Inconsistent scenarios

    A downturn in sales with no change in costs, or every risk happening at once with no story, is not a scenario. Change the inputs that would change together, and only those.

  • Asking for exactly the break-even

    A loan or a covenant set at the break-even leaves no room for the forecast being wrong. Build in headroom.

  • Ranges that are not comparable

    A tornado ranks assumptions by the ranges you give them. Choose ranges that are equally plausible for each input, or the ranking says more about the ranges than the business.

  • Reading sensitivity as likelihood

    A scenario table shows outcomes, not their probabilities. Say how likely you think each is, separately.

  • Testing only value, not survival

    A plan can be valuable and still fail a covenant or run out of cash in one year. Stress the cash and the covenants as well as the valuation.

In practice

Banks run their own downside cases on every loan above a modest size, often a standard haircut such as revenue 10% below the borrower’s plan. A borrower who arrives with a downside case already run, and terms that survive it, saves the bank work and usually gets a faster answer. Investors do the same with valuation: the sensitivity table is the first page many of them read.

Large companies formalise this as stress testing and reverse stress testing: start from a failure (a covenant breach, a credit downgrade) and work backwards to the conditions that would cause it. Lantern’s search for the smallest loan is a small reverse stress test.

Summary

  • Scenarios change several assumptions together into a consistent story; sensitivity changes one at a time; a stress test finds how badly things must go before a limit breaks.
  • Keep one model with a block of scenario inputs; never a copy per scenario.
  • Search for the input that just meets a target (Goal Seek or bisection), then add headroom.
  • A tornado chart ranks assumptions by their swing; it depends on choosing comparable ranges.
  • A two-way table shows how a value depends on two interacting inputs, such as the WACC and long-run growth.
  • Stress the plan’s survival (cash, covenants) as well as its value, and turn the results into terms: Lantern’s $80,000 loan and stepped covenant.

Terms introduced

Stress test
A test of how badly conditions must turn before a plan breaks a limit, such as a covenant, a credit line or a minimum cash balance; a reverse stress test starts from the failure and works back to its causes.
Scenario selector
An input that chooses which set of scenario assumptions a model uses, so that one set of calculations serves every scenario.
Stepped covenant
A covenant whose limit changes over time, typically looser in a borrower’s investment years and tightening as its forecast earnings arrive.
