Revenue and cost forecasts

Forecast each revenue line from what really drives it, spread a project’s years over calendar years, and build costs that behave as costs do: some with sales, some fixed and rising with inflation, some arriving in steps. Then read the forecast for what it says about the business.

About 60 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Choose a forecasting method for each revenue line (growth on a sound base, price times volume, a project schedule) and justify it.
  • Convert a project’s own years into the model’s calendar years.
  • Forecast variable, fixed and step costs from their drivers, including fixed costs that rise with inflation.
  • Build the income statement down to EBITDA, and calculate the contribution of each new activity.
  • Evaluate a forecast against the normalised history and explain what drives its margins year by year.

Fictional case A fictional coffee business founded by Ana Lim. It starts as a catering and barista-training service, opens its own cafés and an online shop for beans, and plans a canned cold brew.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana now has a clean history and a normalised 2027 EBITDA of $73,587. Coastline’s credit officer has seen enough start-up plans to know where they go wrong: revenue that grows smoothly forever, and costs that somehow do not. Ana’s forecast has to show where each dollar of revenue comes from, and costs that rise when the business grows and do not fall when it does not.

One method per revenue line

There is no single right way to forecast revenue. Each line is forecast from the driver that best explains it, and different lines in the same business usually need different methods. Four are common.

Growth on a base
Last year’s revenue grown by an assumed rate. Simple and transparent, and right for a line whose business is stable, provided the base is a full, normal year.
Price times volume
Units sold multiplied by the price per unit: workshops × participants × fee, cans × net price. It separates what the business sells from what it charges, so each can be tested.
Capacity
What the business can serve at most (seats × hours × turns, kitchen hours × covers) times a utilisation rate. Useful for cafés, hotels and factories, and a ceiling on any other method.
Market and share
The size of the market times the share the business expects to win. Common for new products; a check on optimism, since a forecast that implies an implausible market share is wrong.

Lantern’s six revenue lines use three of these. The cafés, the online shop and catering are established, so they grow on their bases: the cafés from their twelve-month figure of $283,200 (not the ten-month $236,000), online and catering from 2027’s actuals. Training is forecast as price times volume, because Ana plans the number of workshops and sets the fee. The second kitchen and Cold Brew are new: their revenue comes from the project forecasts F05 used to decide on them, Cold Brew as cans times the net price per can.

Calendarising a project

The second kitchen opens on 1 April 2028, and F05 forecast it in project years that run from April to March: $150,000 of revenue in its first year, $185,000 in its second, and so on. The model’s years run January to December. Calendarising converts one to the other: each calendar year takes the months of each project year that fall inside it.

The kitchen’s fixed costs ($40,000 a year) and rent ($30,000) are calendarised the same way, so 2028 carries $30,000 and $22,500 of them. Cold Brew is simpler: Ana counts its launch year as calendar 2028, so its project years and the model’s years coincide.

In depth

Costs that behave like costs

A cost forecast is right when each cost moves with what really drives it. Three kinds of behaviour cover most of Lantern’s costs.

  • Variable costs move in proportion to a revenue line: café goods at 31% of café sales, online goods at 48%, Cold Brew at $0.74 a can, cost of services at 39.6% of catering and training revenue, the kitchen’s ingredients and event staff at 42% of its sales, and event wages at 35.1% of catering revenue. Each share is measured against its own line, so the total cost of sales follows the mix.
  • Fixed costs do not change with sales in the short run, but they do change over time. Lantern’s fixed wages (the cafés’ staff on a twelve-month basis, the shop and office, and Ana’s salary) were $179,800 in 2027; Ana assumes pay rises of 3% a year, so they are $185,194 in 2028 and $208,437 by 2032. The café leases ($48,000) and the first kitchen’s rent ($14,400) are fixed by contract.
  • Step costs are fixed until the business reaches a limit, then jump. A second kitchen is the clearest example: Lantern’s catering could not grow past what one kitchen can produce, so growth required a new block of fixed costs (rent, a supervisor, utilities) at once. A forecast that grows sales past a capacity limit without the step in costs is too optimistic.

A few costs are forecast as a share of total revenue because they have many small drivers: card and platform fees, shipping, utilities and sundries together, at 9.1% of revenue, and advertising at 2%. This percent-of-sales shortcut, from F05, is fine for small, mixed costs. It is wrong for fixed costs: rent does not rise because sales do, and forecasting it as a share of sales makes the margin look stable when it would really improve with growth (or worsen in a downturn).

Nominal forecasts, and keeping inflation consistent

Lantern’s model is in nominal dollars: the dollars that will actually be paid and received in each year, including price rises. Its growth rates therefore include inflation. Ana’s 4% a year for the cafés is meant to cover menu prices rising with costs, a little under 3%, and slightly more trade. Its fixed costs rise with inflation too: wages by 3% a year. The café leases are the exception, because their payments are fixed by contract for five years, so they stay at $48,000 a year.

The rule is consistency. A model that grows revenue at nominal rates must grow its costs at nominal rates as well, or the margin will widen year after year for no reason except inflation. The opposite error is as common: costs grown with inflation and revenue held flat at today’s prices, which squeezes the margin just as artificially. F04’s lesson on real and nominal returns made the same point about rates; in a forecast it applies line by line. If a model is built in real terms (today’s prices) for a good reason, every line, and the discount rate used to value it later, must be real too.

Capacity as a ceiling

Growth forecasts should be checked against what the business can physically do. Lantern’s two cafés sold $141,600 each on a twelve-month basis in 2027. The forecast has them selling $168,169 each by 2032, 18.8% more. Is that possible with the same seats, opening hours and staff? Roughly a fifth more sales could come from higher prices, longer hours or quicker service, but if the cafés were already full at peak times in 2027, part of it may need another barista or longer hours, both of which cost money. A capacity check does not need precise numbers: it asks whether the forecast implies something the business cannot do without spending more than the model shows.

The same check applies to the online shop (can Lantern pack and ship 48% more orders by 2032 with the people it has?) and to catering. The second kitchen is itself a capacity decision: F05 justified it by the extra catering and wholesale bakery sales it makes possible, and the model gives those sales their own line rather than stretching the first kitchen’s.

Writing the assumptions down

Every input in the forecast should carry three things beside it: its source (“F05 project forecast”, “2027 accounts”, “Ana’s estimate”), the reason for it in a few words, and, in a model several people use, who owns it and when it was last reviewed. Lantern’s inputs block lists each growth rate with a sentence of reasoning. It takes a few minutes and repays them many times: when Coastline asks why online sales grow 12% in 2028, the answer is already in the model, and when an assumption turns out wrong, Ana can see which other numbers rested on the same thinking.

Contribution: what each new activity adds

For a new activity, the useful measure is its contribution: the revenue it brings in less the costs it causes, both variable and its own fixed costs, before the business’s shared overheads. It answers “is the business better or worse off for doing this?” year by year.

Read a forecast as a story, then test the story. Revenue rises 48% in 2028, mostly from three new things (the kitchen, Cold Brew and a full year of cafés), then growth slows to 3.4% by 2032 as the new lines mature. Gross margin hardly moves (61.4% to 61.0%) because each line keeps its cost share. EBITDA margin falls to 2.7% in 2028, below the normalised 12.7% of 2027, then climbs to 13.0% by 2032 as fixed costs are spread over more sales. A lender reads that as a business taking a year of risk to grow, and will want module 2’s cash flow to show that 2028 can be financed.

Caution: Test the forecast against the world

Every assumption should survive three questions. Has the history shown anything like it? (Online growth of 12% is slower than 2027’s, from a young business: plausible.) Does it imply something absurd? (By 2032 Cold Brew sells 60,000 cans; is there shelf space and demand for that?) Who would disagree, and why? Optimistic forecasts are the commonest cause of bad lending and investment decisions, which F05 called forecasting risk. Module 3 tests Lantern’s forecast with scenarios.

Common mistakes

  • Growing the wrong base

    A base year that is a part year (Lantern’s cafés) or contains one-offs makes every later year wrong. Forecast from the normalised, twelve-month figure.

  • One growth rate for the whole business

    Total revenue grown at one rate hides the fact that Lantern’s lines grow for different reasons and at different speeds, and makes it impossible to test any of them. Forecast each line from its own driver.

  • Treating fixed costs as a share of sales

    Rent, salaries and leases do not rise with sales. Forecast them as amounts that grow with inflation or step up with capacity; use percent-of-sales only for small, mixed costs.

  • Forgetting to calendarise

    A project forecast in its own years (April to March) dropped into calendar columns overstates the first year and misplaces every year after. Spread each project year over the calendar years it covers.

  • Growth without the step in costs

    Sales that grow past what the current kitchen, café or team can handle need new capacity. If the forecast grows sales but not the step costs that make the growth possible, it is too optimistic.

  • A smooth forecast with no story

    If every line grows at a steady rate and every margin is flat, the forecast probably reflects arithmetic, not the business. A real plan has launches, ramp-ups and slowdowns; show them and explain them.

In practice

In a company, the revenue forecast is usually built bottom-up by the people closest to each line (store managers, the head of online, the sales team) and then challenged top-down by finance, which compares the total with the market’s growth and the company’s history. Where the two disagree, the gap is the most useful part of the exercise: it shows which assumptions someone is optimistic about.

Lenders and investors read the cost side as closely as the revenue side. A forecast whose margins rise every year needs a reason: fixed costs spread over growing sales, as in Lantern’s case, or a cheaper supplier, or a price rise. A credit committee will ask what happens to the margin if revenue grows half as fast, and a model whose fixed costs are genuinely fixed will show the margin falling, which is the honest answer.

Summary

  • Forecast each revenue line from the driver that explains it: growth on a sound base, price times volume, capacity, or market size and share.
  • Grow established lines from a normalised, twelve-month base; forecast new activities from their own plans.
  • Calendarise projects whose years do not match the model’s: each calendar year takes the months of each project year that fall in it.
  • Forecast variable costs as shares of the lines they serve, fixed costs as amounts rising with inflation, and step costs as new blocks when capacity runs out.
  • Use percent-of-sales only for small, mixed costs; it misstates fixed costs.
  • Contribution (revenue less variable and its own fixed costs) shows what each new activity adds, year by year.
  • Read the forecast as a story, explain every dip and rise, and test the assumptions against the history and the world.

Terms introduced

Calendarising
Converting amounts forecast in a project’s own years (April to March, say) into the model’s calendar years, by giving each calendar year the months of each project year that fall within it.
Step cost
A cost that is fixed up to a level of activity and then jumps to a higher fixed level, such as the rent and staff of a second kitchen once the first is full.
Contribution
The revenue an activity brings in minus the variable costs and its own fixed costs, before the business’s shared overheads: what the business gains by doing it.
Percent-of-sales
Forecasting an item as a constant percentage of revenue. Suitable for costs that move with sales; misleading for fixed costs.
