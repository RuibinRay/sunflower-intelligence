Working capital and capital spending

EBITDA is not cash. Two schedules explain most of the gap: working capital, the cash tied up in customers who have not paid and stock not yet sold, and capital spending, the cash that buys the equipment depreciation later spreads over the years. Build both for Lantern.

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Forecast receivables, inventory, payables and other working capital balances from days and shares, each measured against the right driver.
  • Calculate the change in working capital and explain its effect on cash.
  • Build a fixed asset schedule: capital spending, depreciation by asset from the month it comes into use, and the roll-forward of property and equipment.
  • Evaluate capital spending against depreciation, asset lives and the business’s plans.
  • Explain why the model’s book depreciation and the tax depreciation used in F05 differ, and what the model leaves out.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana’s forecast shows EBITDA of $23,319 in 2028. Coastline’s credit officer is more interested in cash, because loans are repaid in cash, and in 2028 Lantern will spend $105,000 on the second kitchen and Cold Brew’s fridges, collect a large payment owed at the end of 2027, and carry more stock and more customer invoices as it grows. None of that is in EBITDA. This lesson builds the two schedules that turn profit into cash.

Why working capital uses cash

A business that sells on credit records revenue when it delivers, but collects the cash later; until then, the money sits in receivables. It buys stock before selling it, so cash sits in inventory. On the other side, it buys from suppliers on credit, so their money sits in payables, and some customers pay in advance, so theirs sits in unearned revenue. The difference is operating working capital: the cash the business has tied up in its day-to-day operations. When working capital grows, cash is used; when it shrinks, cash is released, even if profit is unchanged.

Growth is the usual cause. If Lantern’s catering doubles, so do the invoices waiting to be paid at any moment. That is why fast-growing businesses can be profitable and still run short of cash, the problem F00 and F05 both warned about, and why a lender wants to see working capital forecast line by line rather than ignored.

Forecasting balances in days

Each balance is forecast from the flow it comes from, measured in days. Days sales outstanding says how many days of credit sales are waiting to be collected; days inventory outstanding, how many days of cost of goods sit in stock; days payables outstanding, how many days of purchases are owed to suppliers. F03 measured them on Lantern’s 2027 accounts. Ana rounds them into assumptions:

Without it working capital would have risen. From 2029 the schedule settles into a pattern: a rise of $6,216 in 2029 as sales grow, then small changes as growth slows. A lender reads 2028’s release with care: it is real cash, but it will not happen again.

Key idea: The sign that trips everyone

An increase in a working capital asset (receivables, inventory) uses cash; an increase in a working capital liability (payables, unearned revenue) provides cash. In the cash flow statement, the change in operating working capital is subtracted from profit: a positive change means cash went into the business.

Choosing the right driver for each balance

Most errors in working capital forecasts come from measuring a balance against the wrong flow. Receivables come only from sales customers have not paid for yet, so they are measured against invoiced revenue: catering, training and the second kitchen’s wholesale bakery, and Cold Brew separately because grocers take longer to pay. Measuring them against total revenue would dilute the days with card sales that are never owed, and the forecast would go wrong as soon as the mix changed. Inventory and payables are measured against cost of goods sold, because stock is carried at cost and suppliers are paid at cost. Wages payable follow the wage bill, unearned revenue the revenue that customers pay for in advance (training places, subscriptions, catering deposits), and taxes payable the year’s tax charge.

Year-end balances can also mislead in a seasonal business. If Lantern’s catering peaked every December, its receivables at 31 December would be higher than in a typical month, and days measured at the year end would overstate the cash tied up during most of the year. A monthly model would catch that; an annual model can only use year-end balances consistently and note the risk. Lantern’s year-end is not unusually busy, so year-end days are a fair measure.

From days to the cash conversion cycle

The three days measures combine into the cash conversion cycle: days of inventory plus days of receivables minus days of payables. It is roughly how long a dollar spent on stock takes to come back as cash from a customer. For Lantern the measure needs care, because its receivables and its inventory belong to different lines (invoiced customers against goods sold in the cafés and online), so a single cycle for the whole business mixes unlike things. In a model it is more useful line by line, and as a check on the assumptions: a forecast whose cycle shortens sharply, without new payment terms or a change in how stock is bought, is manufacturing cash on paper.

One-off balances

Some balances at the start of a forecast will not recur and should not be grown with sales. Lantern’s contract asset is one: nine months of a year’s catering for a law firm, recognised as Lantern delivered it in 2027, but paid in one sum of $41,120 only when the contract year ends on 31 March 2028. No contract on those terms is planned again. The model therefore takes the balance to zero in 2028 instead of forecasting it in days. Other common examples are a large customer deposit received just before the year end, a tax refund due, or a one-time prepayment. Each needs its own line, run off on its own timetable, and a note saying why.

Working capital and the covenant

Most working capital lines are also current assets and current liabilities, so they feed straight into Coastline’s covenant, the current ratio of at least 1.2. That ties the working capital assumptions to the bank’s question in ways that are easy to miss. Faster collection lowers receivables and raises cash by the same amount, leaving current assets unchanged; but if the cash is then used to repay the line of credit, current liabilities fall too and the ratio improves. Paying suppliers more slowly raises payables, a current liability, and so lowers the ratio, even though it helps cash. Unearned revenue does the same: customers paying in advance is good for cash and bad for the current ratio. A model built with each balance on its own line lets Ana see these effects before Coastline does, and lesson 6 tests the covenant year by year once the statements are linked.

The fixed asset schedule

Capital spending is the cash paid for long-lived assets; depreciation spreads their cost over the years they are used. The model keeps them in a fixed asset schedule: one row per asset or group, its cost, the month it comes into use, its monthly depreciation and its life, and below them the totals for each year. Lantern’s schedule has the five groups of assets it owned at the end of 2027 (F02 and F03), the three it buys in 2028 (F05), and Ana’s allowance for replacements.

Testing the capital spending

Three checks catch most capital spending errors. First, compare it with depreciation over several years: a business that spends far less than its depreciation is letting its assets run down, and its forecast margins will not last. Ana’s first draft allowed $10,000 a year for replacements against depreciation of over $40,000; she raised it to $25,000 a year from 2029. Second, look at asset lives: Lantern’s café fit-out and espresso machines are fully depreciated by early 2032, when the café leases come up for renewal, so the model adds a refit of $54,000 in March 2032. Third, ask whether the plan needs assets the schedule leaves out: a third kitchen, more delivery vans. Capital spending of $105,000 in 2028, $25,000 a year from 2029 and $79,000 in 2032 is lumpy, as real capital spending is.

Book depreciation, tax depreciation and leases

The model depreciates every asset straight line, for its accounts and for its tax, which is a simplification. F05 used the US tax system’s MACRS schedules, which deduct more in the early years; the difference would create a deferred tax liability on Lantern’s balance sheet. For a small model the effect is small and is left out, and the lessons say so. The cafés’ leases are kept as a right-of-use asset and an equal lease liability that run down together, because their payments are level; their cost stays in occupancy, as in 2027. The second kitchen’s rent is treated as an expense when paid, another stated simplification.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring receivables against all revenue

    Café and online sales are paid by card on the day: they create no receivables. Measuring days against total revenue understates the days and misforecasts the balance when the mix changes.

  • Getting the sign of the change wrong

    An increase in receivables or inventory uses cash; an increase in payables provides it. Subtract the change in operating working capital from profit, and check the sign against common sense.

  • Forgetting one-off balances

    A large balance that will not recur (Lantern’s contract asset) must be run off explicitly, not grown with sales.

  • Depreciating from the start of the year

    An asset bought in April is used for nine months of that year. Depreciate from the month it comes into use, as the accounts do.

  • Capital spending far below depreciation

    A forecast that spends little on replacements lets the assets run down while margins stay high. Compare capex with depreciation and plan for assets reaching the end of their lives.

  • Putting cash and debt in working capital

    Cash, the line of credit and the current portion of debt are financing, not operations. Keep them out of the operating working capital schedule, or the cash flow will count them twice.

In practice

Lenders to small businesses watch working capital closely because it is where cash disappears without anyone noticing. A credit analyst will ask for the receivables ageing (who owes what, for how long) and compare the forecast days with the history. A forecast that improves collection from 25 days to 15 without a reason, such as new payment terms, is a red flag: it manufactures cash on paper.

In larger companies, capital spending is planned in a separate capital budget, project by project, and the model takes its totals. Analysts covering listed companies compare capex with depreciation and with the company’s stated plans in its 10-K, and a gap between them is one of the first questions they ask management.

Summary

  • Operating working capital is the cash tied up in receivables, inventory and prepayments, less what suppliers, staff and customers have advanced; a rise uses cash and a fall releases it.
  • Forecast each balance as a flow times days ÷ 365, measured against the right flow: invoiced revenue for receivables, cost of goods for inventory and payables.
  • Run off one-off balances explicitly, and watch for one-off releases that flatter a year’s cash.
  • A fixed asset schedule lists each asset’s cost, start month, monthly depreciation and life; depreciation runs from the month an asset comes into use.
  • Roll property and equipment forward: opening net book value + capital spending − depreciation = closing.
  • Test capital spending against depreciation and asset lives; plan replacements and refits, which make real capex lumpy.
  • State the simplifications: book depreciation used for tax, leases kept at equal right-of-use asset and liability, the second kitchen’s rent expensed.

Terms introduced

Working capital schedule
The part of a model that forecasts each operating current asset and liability (receivables, inventory, payables, unearned revenue and so on) from its driver, and the change in their total each year.
Fixed asset schedule
The part of a model that lists each long-lived asset’s cost, start date, depreciation and life, and rolls property and equipment forward each year from capital spending and depreciation.
Maintenance capital spending
Capital spending needed to replace and keep up existing assets, as opposed to spending on growth such as a new kitchen.
