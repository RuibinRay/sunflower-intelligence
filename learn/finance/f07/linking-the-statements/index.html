Linking the statements

The income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement are three views of one set of events, so a model must tie them together: profit to equity, schedules to the balance sheet, and every balance sheet change to cash. Link Lantern’s three statements, make them balance, find out why a model does not, and test Coastline’s covenant.

About 60 minutes

Before you start

You will be able to

  • Construct a forecast income statement below EBITDA: depreciation, interest, tax with losses carried forward, and net income.
  • Build the balance sheet from the schedules and roll equity forward with profit, dividends and new shares.
  • Prepare the cash flow statement by the indirect method from the income statement and the changes in the balance sheet, and link its closing cash to the balance sheet.
  • Diagnose a balance sheet that does not balance, using the size and sign of the difference.
  • Test a current-ratio covenant from the linked statements and explain the result to a lender.

Review first

  • Debt schedules — Interest, repayments, the line of credit and the current portion of debt, which the statements take from the debt schedule.
  • Linking the statements — How the three statements connect in a set of accounts; this lesson does the same in a forecast.
  • The indirect method — Operating cash flow from net income, depreciation and the changes in working capital.

The idea

Fictional case

Ana now has every piece: the forecast to EBITDA, working capital, fixed assets, and the loans. Coastline’s credit officer will look at none of them on their own. She will open the balance sheet for 31 December 2028, check that it balances, read the cash, and divide current assets by current liabilities. This lesson puts the pieces together so that she can.

Three statements, one set of events

A three-statement model forecasts the income statement, the balance sheet and the cash flow statement together, linked so that each event appears consistently in all three. F02 showed the links in a set of accounts; a model reproduces them with formulas. There are only a few, and every model has the same ones.

  1. Net income from the income statement increases retained earnings on the balance sheet, and starts the cash flow statement.
  2. Depreciation is an expense in the income statement, reduces property and equipment on the balance sheet, and is added back in the cash flow statement because it uses no cash.
  3. Working capital balances come from the working capital schedule; their changes appear in operating cash flow.
  4. Capital spending adds to property and equipment and is an investing cash outflow.
  5. Debt: drawings and repayments change the loans on the balance sheet and are financing cash flows; interest is an expense; the current portion is a current liability.
  6. Equity: new shares add to common stock and to financing cash; dividends reduce retained earnings and cash.
  7. Cash: the closing cash in the cash flow statement is the cash on the balance sheet.

If every link is right, the balance sheet balances by itself, without any plug. That is why the balance check, total assets minus total liabilities and equity, is the most important row in a model: it must be zero in every period, and when it is not, a link is broken.

Below EBITDA

The income statement continues from lesson 3’s EBITDA. Depreciation comes from the fixed asset schedule and interest from the debt schedule, giving pre-tax profit. Tax is 25% of pre-tax profit, with one complication: 2028 is a loss. A company that makes a tax loss pays no tax that year, and the loss can be carried forward to reduce the taxable profit of later years. Lantern’s 2028 loss of $19,292 is set against 2029’s profit, so 2029’s tax is 25% of $18,005 instead of $37,297. The model keeps the unused loss in its own row, a tax loss carryforward, and ignores the limits some tax systems put on how much can be used in one year.

The cash flow statement

The cash flow statement is built by the indirect method from the other two statements. Operating cash flow starts from net income, adds back depreciation, and adjusts for each change in working capital and in interest owed. Investing cash flow is capital spending. Financing cash flow is new loans and shares, less repayments and dividends, plus or minus the line of credit. The order of calculation

Inside each forecast year the model calculates in a fixed order, and seeing it makes the links easier to follow and to check. First the operations: revenue, costs and EBITDA (lesson 3), then the working capital balances and the fixed asset schedule (lesson 4). Then the loans whose terms are fixed: their interest and repayments for the year (lesson 5). With depreciation and interest known, the income statement can be finished down to net income, with tax after any loss carried forward. Next comes cash before the line of credit: last year’s cash, plus operating cash flow, less capital spending, plus new loans and shares, less repayments and dividends. Only then does the revolver decide how much to draw or repay, and only then is cash final. Last of all, the balance sheet is assembled and checked.

The order matters because each step uses only results from the steps before it, or from the year before. That is what keeps the model free of loops. The line of credit’s interest is the one place a loop could form, and charging it on the opening balance puts it in the first group of calculations instead of the last.

Reading the linked forecast

Once the statements link, the forecast can be read as one story. In 2028 Lantern loses money, builds the second kitchen and launches Cold Brew, and finances it with the term loan and Hollis’s equity, ending the year with cash at the $15,000 minimum. From 2029 profit returns, the line of credit is repaid, and cash builds: $33,490 at the end of 2029 and $162,280 by 2032, after dividends of 30% of each year’s profit from 2030. Debt falls from $72,266 at the end of 2028 to $3,562 by 2032. Property and equipment fall as the 2028 investment is depreciated, until the café refit in 2032.

Two features deserve a comment in the model’s notes. The large cash balance from 2030 is a question for Ana and Hollis: hold it for a third kitchen or another café, pay more out, or repay the term loan early? A model does not decide that, but it makes the question visible. And total assets jump in 2032 because the cafés’ leases are renewed: the right-of-use asset and the lease liability both return to their original size, which changes the balance sheet but not cash or profit.

When the balance sheet does not balance

Every modeller builds a model that does not balance at some point. The difference itself is the best clue to the cause, and a few checks find most errors.

  • Check the first forecast year first. If the opening balance sheet balances and the first year does not, the error is in that year’s links; later years inherit it.
  • If the difference equals a single amount in the model, that amount is probably missing from one side: a balance sheet change with no cash flow (a new loan added to debt but not to financing cash), or a cash flow with no balance sheet change.
  • If the difference is twice an amount, that amount probably has the wrong sign somewhere: an increase in receivables added in the cash flow statement instead of subtracted.
  • If the difference grows each year by the same amount, a recurring flow is missing, such as depreciation not added back, or dividends not deducted from retained earnings.
  • Never plug the difference. A typed adjustment that makes the check zero hides the error and breaks the model the next time an input changes.

Testing the covenant

With the balance sheet built, the covenant is one row: current assets divided by current liabilities, using Coastline’s definition, which leaves lease liabilities out. At 31 December 2028 Lantern’s current assets are $58,984 and its current liabilities $58,382: a current ratio of 1.01. The covenant requires at least 1.2. From 2029 the ratio is 1.63 and rising.

Caution: The model has found a problem

Lantern’s base case breaches its covenant at the end of 2028. The causes are visible in the model: a loss year, cash held only at the $15,000 minimum, the line of credit still drawn, and $20,027 of term loan, van note and brewer principal falling due within a year. F05 checked the ratio only at 31 March 2028; the full model shows a later quarter that fails. And Coastline tests the covenant every quarter end, so an annual model can only show year ends: the quarters inside 2028 need checking too. This is what models are for: finding a problem in February, on paper, instead of in December, with the bank.

Common mistakes

  • Plugging the difference

    A typed number that makes the balance check zero hides a broken link. Find the cause; the model will be wrong again the moment an input changes.

  • Calculating cash twice

    If the balance sheet’s cash is worked out separately from the cash flow statement, the two can disagree. Link the balance sheet’s cash to the cash flow statement’s closing cash.

  • Forgetting a flow that changes a balance

    Every change in a balance sheet line needs a cash flow or a profit item. A loan added to debt without a financing inflow, or dividends paid in cash but not deducted from retained earnings, breaks the balance.

  • Taxing a loss

    A loss year pays no tax, and its loss reduces later years’ taxable profit. A model that books a negative tax or ignores the carryforward misstates cash.

  • Reading the covenant with the wrong definition

    Covenants use the loan agreement’s definitions. Coastline’s current ratio leaves lease liabilities out; using the accounting current ratio would give a different answer.

  • Assuming the line of credit fixes a current ratio

    Borrowing short-term to hold cash adds equally to current assets and current liabilities. Only long-term money or less spending improves the ratio.

In practice

In practice the balance check is the first thing anyone receiving a model looks at, and a model that does not balance is sent back unread. Bankers, investors and auditors also check that the cash flow statement’s closing cash equals the balance sheet’s, and that retained earnings roll forward from net income and dividends, because those three tests catch most broken links.

Covenant tests are standard outputs in any model that supports a loan. Lenders set covenants with covenant headroom over the borrower’s forecast; a forecast that shows a breach, or very little headroom, changes the conversation before the loan is signed. Borrowers who show their lender a problem early, with a solution, usually get a better hearing than those whose lender finds it in the quarterly accounts.

Summary

  • A three-statement model links net income to retained earnings, schedules to the balance sheet, and every balance sheet change to cash.
  • Below EBITDA: depreciation from the fixed asset schedule, interest from the debt schedule, and tax on profit after losses carried forward.
  • Every balance sheet line comes from a schedule or a roll-forward; retained earnings = last year + net income − dividends.
  • Build the cash flow statement by the indirect method, and link its closing cash to the balance sheet.
  • The balance check must be zero every period; diagnose a difference from its size and sign, and never plug it.
  • Test covenants with the loan agreement’s definitions; short-term borrowing does not repair a current ratio.
  • Lantern’s base case breaches the 1.2 covenant at the end of 2028 (1.01), a problem the linked model finds before it happens.

Terms introduced

Balance check
A row in a model that subtracts total liabilities and equity from total assets for each period; it must be zero, and any other value shows a broken link.
Tax loss carryforward
A tax loss from one year that reduces taxable profit in later years, so that tax is paid on profits net of earlier losses.
Covenant headroom
The margin between a covenant measure and its limit, such as a current ratio of 1.5 against a minimum of 1.2; little headroom means a small shortfall in the forecast would cause a breach.
