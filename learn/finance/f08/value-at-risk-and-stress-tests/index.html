Value at Risk and stress tests

After the hedges, how much could still be lost? Measure the risk in Hollis Avery’s investments with Value at Risk and expected shortfall, by history and by the normal distribution, test whether the measure would have worked, and ask what the numbers miss with stress tests.

The idea

Fictional case

Hollis’s listed investments are worth $500,000: $42,000 in Brightcup, $308,000 in the Harbourline market fund and $150,000 in a bond fund. He has bought puts on Brightcup and is thinking about index futures. Before deciding, he wants one number: how much could he lose in a bad month? Ana uses F06’s fifteen years of monthly returns for the three holdings, which are simulated for teaching, not real market data.

Value at Risk

Value at Risk (VaR) answers a precise question: over a given horizon, what loss will be exceeded only with a small probability? A one-month 95% VaR of $25,000 means that in 95 months out of 100 the portfolio should lose less than $25,000, and in about 5 it should lose more. VaR has three parts: the horizon, the confidence level and the loss. Losses are written as positive numbers.

Pr⁡(Loss>VaRc)=1−c\Pr(\text{Loss} > \text{VaR}_c) = 1 - cPr(Loss>VaRc​)=1−c

Historical VaR

The simplest method uses history directly. Apply today’s holdings to each past month’s returns, sort the 180 monthly results, and read off the loss that only 5% of months exceeded. For Hollis’s book, the 95% one-month historical simulation VaR is 4.93% of its value, $24,631. At 99% it is 7.23%, $36,169. The worst month in the sample, March 2020, lost $58,644.

VaR from the normal distribution

The other classic method assumes returns are normal and uses only their mean and standard deviation. The book’s monthly returns averaged 0.72% with a standard deviation of 3.44%. A loss larger than 1.645 standard deviations below the mean has a 5% chance:

VaRc=−(μ+z1−c σ)×Vz0.05=−1.645, z0.01=−2.326\text{VaR}_c = -(\mu + z_{1-c}\,\sigma)\times V \qquad z_{0.05} = -1.645,\ z_{0.01} = -2.326VaRc​=−(μ+z1−c​σ)×Vz0.05​=−1.645, z0.01​=−2.326

That gives (1.645 × 3.44% − 0.72%) × $500,000 = $24,671 at 95%, and $36,383 at 99%. Here the two methods agree closely; they part company when returns have fatter tails than the normal, which real returns usually do.

Expected shortfall

VaR says where the bad tail begins, not how bad it is. Expected shortfall (ES), also called conditional VaR, is the average loss in the months beyond VaR. Historically, the 9 worst months of the 180 lost an average of $34,684, against the 95% VaR of $24,631; the normal distribution gives $31,852. The historical figure is higher because the sample’s worst months were worse than a normal distribution expects.

In depth

A third method: Monte Carlo simulation

Monte Carlo VaR draws thousands of possible months from a model of how the holdings’ returns behave together, revalues the portfolio in each, and reads the loss quantile from the simulated results. It can use distributions with fatter tails than the normal, and it can revalue options and other positions whose value is not a straight line in the market price, such as Hollis’s puts. The price is the model: the simulated months are only as good as the assumptions that produced them.

Choosing the confidence level and horizon

The confidence level and horizon depend on what the number is for. A trading desk that can close its positions in a day uses a one-day horizon; a pension fund that reviews its strategy once a year may look at a year. Higher confidence describes rarer losses but rests on fewer observations: at 99% with 180 months, the historical estimate depends on two months. Banks’ regulatory measures have used 99% over ten days for VaR and 97.5% for expected shortfall; Hollis, who looks at his portfolio monthly, uses one month at 95%.

Horizon and the square-root-of-time rule

VaR for a longer horizon is often scaled from a short one. If returns are independent from month to month, the variance over h months is h times the monthly variance, so the standard deviation grows with √h. A three-month 95% VaR is then about √3 × $24,631 = $42,663. The rule ignores the mean, which matters more over long horizons, and fails when returns are not independent: in crises, bad months tend to follow bad months.

VaRh≈h  VaR1\text{VaR}_{h} \approx \sqrt{h}\;\text{VaR}_{1}VaRh​≈h​VaR1​

Backtesting: would it have worked?

A VaR measure makes a testable promise: at 95%, losses should exceed it in about 5% of periods. A backtest checks the promise on data the measure did not see. Ana estimates the 95% historical VaR each month from the previous 60 months only, then checks the next month. Over the 120 months from January 2018 to December 2027, the loss exceeded the VaR 6 times, exactly the 6 expected.

Kupiec’s test asks whether the number of exceptions is consistent with the promised 5%. With 6 of 120 its p-value is 1.00: no evidence against the model. But when the exceptions came matters too. They fell in February 2020, March 2020, January 2022, February 2022, June 2022, July 2023. Christoffersen’s test of independence asks whether an exception makes another more likely next month; its p-value here is 0.021, below 0.05. The exceptions came mostly in clusters, early in 2020 and in the first half of 2022, when a measure built on five calm years was slow to see the new risk. A VaR can be right on average and still wrong when it matters most.

What VaR misses

  • The size of the tail. VaR says nothing about how much is lost beyond it. Two portfolios with the same VaR can have very different worst cases; expected shortfall partly fixes this.
  • The past as a guide. Historical VaR assumes the sample contains the kinds of month that can happen; normal VaR assumes the normal distribution. Neither sees a crisis unlike any in the data.
  • Liquidity. VaR assumes positions can be valued, and sold, at market prices. In a liquidity spiral (lesson 10) they may not be.
  • Adding up. VaR is not always subadditive: in some cases the VaR of two portfolios combined is more than the sum of their VaRs, which penalises diversification. Expected shortfall does not have this flaw.

For these reasons the Basel Committee’s rules for banks’ trading books moved their main measure of market risk from 99% VaR to 97.5% expected shortfall, and require banks to run stress tests alongside it.

Stress tests

A stress test asks what a specific, severe scenario would do to the portfolio, without asking how likely it is. Historical scenarios replay a real episode: the worst month in Hollis’s sample, March 2020, would cost $58,644 today. Hypothetical scenarios describe events that have not happened, chosen because they hit the portfolio where it is weakest. For Hollis, the obvious one is trouble at Brightcup during a market fall.

Using the measures together

Risk managers use the measures as a set. VaR and expected shortfall summarise ordinary risk in a number that can be compared across portfolios and over time, and used to set limits. Backtests check that the numbers can be trusted. Stress tests explore the rare events that statistics cannot see, and show which positions drive the losses. None of them says what the next crisis will be; together they make it less likely that a portfolio is exposed to one without anyone knowing.

Common mistakes

  • Reading VaR as the worst case

    A 95% VaR is exceeded in about one period in twenty. The worst case is much larger, which is why expected shortfall and stress tests exist.

  • Mixing horizons

    A monthly VaR is not an annual one. Scale with √h only if returns are independent, and remember the rule ignores the mean.

  • Trusting a 99% figure from a short history

    With 180 months, a 99% historical VaR rests on two observations. More confidence needs more data or a model.

  • Judging a backtest by the count alone

    The right number of exceptions can hide a model that fails in clusters, exactly when risk changes.

  • Stress-testing only the past

    Historical scenarios are useful, but the scenario that hurts most is often one that has not happened yet. Design hypothetical scenarios around the portfolio’s weak points.

In practice

Banks report VaR for their trading books every day, and regulators use VaR and expected shortfall, with backtests and traffic-light rules on the number of exceptions, to set the capital banks must hold against market risk. Since 2009, regulators’ stress tests, such as the Federal Reserve’s annual tests of large US banks, have become as important as the models: they set out a severe recession and market crash and require each bank to show it would still have enough capital. Asset managers, pension funds and insurers use the same tools to set risk limits and report to their boards.

VaR became an industry standard in the 1990s. A bank’s chief executive famously asked for a single daily figure for the firm’s market risk, and the method the bank developed, published in 1994 as RiskMetrics, made variance–covariance VaR widely available. Its appeal was a single number that a board could understand; its danger, the same number trusted too far, became plain in 2007 and 2008, when losses far beyond VaR arrived together.

Businesses use the ideas too. Cash flow at risk applies VaR to a company’s operating cash flow: for Lantern, the coffee price that would be exceeded only in one year in twenty, and the EBITDA it would leave, is a way to set how much coffee to hedge. Stress tests are a standard part of a lender’s credit review, as Coastline’s scenarios in F07 showed.

Summary

  • VaR at confidence c over a horizon is the loss exceeded with probability 1 − c; expected shortfall is the average loss beyond it.
  • Historical VaR reads the quantile from past returns applied to today’s holdings; normal VaR is −(μ + zσ) × V.
  • Longer horizons scale roughly with √h if returns are independent.
  • Backtests count exceptions (Kupiec) and check whether they cluster (Christoffersen).
  • VaR misses the size of the tail, unprecedented events and liquidity; regulators moved to expected shortfall and stress tests.
  • Stress tests revalue the portfolio under severe historical or hypothetical scenarios, and show how hedges behave when needed.

Terms introduced

Value at Risk
The loss over a given horizon that will be exceeded only with a stated small probability, such as 5% for a 95% VaR.
Expected shortfall
The average loss in the cases where the loss exceeds VaR. Also called conditional VaR.
Historical simulation
Estimating VaR by applying today’s holdings to past periods’ returns and reading the loss quantile from the results.
Backtest
A check of a risk measure against outcomes it did not use, such as counting how often losses exceeded the VaR forecast.
Stress test
An estimate of a portfolio’s or institution’s loss in a specified severe scenario, historical or hypothetical, without regard to its probability.
