Hedging with derivatives

A hedge rarely matches the exposure perfectly. Work out how many futures contracts minimise the risk when the futures track a related price, measure what the hedge leaves behind, follow basis risk through a grower’s hedge, change a portfolio’s market exposure with index futures, and choose between futures and options.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern is too small for futures, as lesson 2 showed, but Brightcup is not. Its treasurer, whose swap Ana followed in lesson 4, now wants to hedge next quarter’s purchases of roasted coffee, about $6,000,000, with the fictional green-coffee futures. Roasted coffee is not green coffee, so the futures will not track Brightcup’s costs exactly. Meanwhile Hollis, with $500,000 in listed investments, asks whether he could reduce his exposure to the stock market for a few months without selling anything.

When the hedge does not match the exposure

A hedge is perfect only when the contract is on exactly the asset being hedged, for exactly the quantity and date. More often the contract is on something related: green coffee for roasted coffee, a share index for a portfolio, crude oil for jet fuel. This is a cross hedge, and the question becomes how much of the contract to use. Hedging one for one is not necessarily best, because the two prices do not move by the same amounts.

Brightcup’s analysts estimated, from three years of quarterly data (fictional), that its roasted-coffee prices change with a standard deviation of 6% a quarter, the futures price with 9%, and the correlation between the two changes is 0.85. The futures move more than Brightcup’s costs, and not always together.

The minimum-variance hedge ratio

Choose the hedge ratio h (units of futures per unit of exposure, in value) to make the hedged position’s variance as small as possible. A little calculus, or a regression of the change in the spot price on the change in the futures price, gives the minimum-variance hedge ratio:

h∗=ρ σSσFh^* = \rho\,\frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F}h∗=ρσF​σS​​

For Brightcup, h* = 0.85 × 0.06 ÷ 0.09 = 0.5667. Each futures contract covers 5,000 kg at $6.40, $32,000 of coffee, so the number of contracts is:

N∗=h∗ VAVF=0.5667×6,000,00032,000=106.25N^* = h^*\,\frac{V_A}{V_F} = 0.5667 \times \frac{6{,}000{,}000}{32{,}000} = 106.25N∗=h∗VF​VA​​=0.5667×32,0006,000,000​=106.25

Brightcup buys 106 contracts. (It buys because it is exposed to a rise in the price it pays.) The share of the variance the hedge removes, its hedge effectiveness, is ρ² = 72.25%.

In depth

Basis risk in a single hedge

Even a hedge on the right commodity carries basis risk, because the hedger usually closes the futures before delivery, and the gap between spot and futures prices on that day is not known in advance. The basis is the spot price less the futures price.

A growers’ cooperative sells futures at $6.40 when the spot price is $6.30 (basis -0.10), to protect the price of a crop it will sell in a few months. When it sells the crop, the spot price is $5.90 and the futures $6.05 (basis -0.15). It sells the crop for $5.90 and gains 6.40 − 6.05 = $0.35 a kg on the futures, an effective price of $6.25.

Effective price=S1+(F0−F1)=F0+b1\text{Effective price} = S_1 + (F_0 - F_1) = F_0 + b_1Effective price=S1​+(F0​−F1​)=F0​+b1​

The effective price is the futures price when the hedge was set plus the basis when it is lifted: 6.40 + (-0.15) = $6.25. The futures locked in $6.40; the only uncertainty left is the closing basis. Here the basis weakened by 0.05, costing the seller 0.05 a kg against what it might have expected. A buyer would have gained from the same change. Basis risk is usually much smaller than the price risk it replaces, which is why hedgers accept it.

Hedging a share portfolio with index futures

Hollis’s book is $42,000 in Brightcup, $308,000 in the Harbourline market fund and $150,000 in a bond fund. Using F06’s betas (Brightcup 1.05, the bond fund 0.13 on the simulated data, the market fund 1 by definition), the book’s beta is 0.7431: it moves about 74 cents for each dollar the market moves, per dollar invested.

A fictional index futures contract on the Harbourline index is worth 5,000 points × $50 = $250,000. Selling index futures lowers the book’s beta without selling any investments. To remove the market exposure entirely:

N=βP VPF×mN = \beta_P\,\frac{V_P}{F \times m}N=βP​F×mVP​​

N = 0.7431 × 500,000 ÷ 250,000 = 1.49 contracts. Contracts are whole, so Hollis could sell one, cutting his beta to 0.7431 − 250,000 ÷ 500,000 = 0.2431, or two, which would take it slightly below zero. To move to a target beta β*, sell (βP − β*) × VP ÷ (F × m) contracts. An index hedge removes market risk only: the risk specific to Brightcup, the part of its movements that the market does not explain, stays with him.

Futures, forwards or options?

The choice of instrument depends on what the hedger is willing to give up. Forwards and futures fix the price: they remove the bad outcomes and the good ones, and cost nothing up front beyond margin. Options remove only the bad outcomes, and cost a premium. A collar sits between them: it buys a put and pays for it by writing a call, setting both a floor and a ceiling.

Hollis could collar his Brightcup shares by buying the $40 put at $2.18 and writing the $46 call at $2.37. The call brings in more than the put costs: a net credit of $0.19 a share, $190 on 1,000 shares. On 31 March 2029 his shares are worth between $40,000 and $46,000, whatever Brightcup does. He gives up gains above $46 to pay for protection below $40.

Estimating the hedge ratio

In practice h* is estimated by regressing past changes in the price being hedged on changes in the futures price over the same periods: the slope of the line is h*, and the regression’s R² is the hedge effectiveness. The periods should match the hedge’s horizon (quarterly changes for a quarterly hedge), and the data should be recent enough to reflect how the two prices move together now. Brightcup’s analysts used three years of quarters; a longer history gives a steadier estimate but may include years when the relation was different. Hedge accounting rules ask for the same evidence: a documented, reasonable expectation that the hedge will offset most of the exposure.

Lantern’s hedges, in review

Pulling the course together for Lantern: its coffee is hedged by Kilnworth’s fixed price, a forward that matches the exact quantity, grade and dates; its €38,000 bill by Coastline’s currency forward; its debt needs no swap because the term loan is fixed and the line of credit is being repaid. It looked at futures and at a price cap and chose not to use them: futures because the contracts are too large and of the wrong grade, the cap because Ana valued certainty more than the chance of cheaper coffee. Each choice follows the same steps: measure the exposure, choose the instrument that fits it best, decide how much to hedge, and know what risk is left.

Hedging in practice

A few practical points decide whether a hedge works as designed. Hedge ratios estimated from past data change, so they should be re-estimated and the hedge adjusted from time to time. Long exposures are often hedged by rolling short-dated futures forward, closing each contract before delivery and opening the next, which adds a basis risk at each roll. Futures hedges need cash for margin calls, as lesson 2 showed. And a hedge should be checked against the policy that authorised it: the exposure it covers, the share hedged, the instruments allowed and the limits. A position larger than the exposure, or in an instrument with little connection to it, is a speculation however it is labelled.

Common mistakes

  • Hedging one for one by default

    When the futures are more volatile than the exposure, or imperfectly correlated with it, a one-for-one hedge adds risk. Use h* = ρσS/σF.

  • Mixing price changes and proportional changes

    Estimate σS, σF and ρ on the same basis. With proportional changes, multiply h* by the value of the exposure over the value of a contract.

  • Thinking a futures hedge removes all risk

    It replaces price risk with basis risk: the effective price is F0 plus the basis when the hedge is lifted.

  • Using an index hedge for a single share’s risk

    Index futures remove market risk. A share’s own risk, the part not explained by the market, remains.

  • Forgetting that a collar caps the upside

    A zero-cost collar is not free: its cost is the gains given up above the call’s strike.

In practice

Airlines are the classic cross hedgers: jet fuel has few liquid futures, so many airlines hedge with crude oil or heating oil futures and options, and accept the basis risk between them. Food companies hedge grains with futures on the nearest traded grade; exporters hedge currencies with forwards; fund managers adjust their market exposure with index futures rather than selling shares, which would take longer and cost more. In each case the questions in this lesson are asked: which contract tracks the exposure best, how many contracts, and what risk remains.

Hedging can also fail spectacularly when the hedge and the exposure differ in timing or in cash. A company that hedges long-dated supply contracts with short-dated futures, rolling them over each month, can face large margin calls when prices fall, while its gains on the supply contracts arrive only over years. The economics may be sound and the company may still run out of cash, as lesson 2 warned. Good hedging programmes plan for the cash needed in the worst months, not only for the average outcome.

Accounting matters as well. Under hedge accounting (ASC 815 in US GAAP, IFRS 9 elsewhere) a company must document the hedge relationship and show that the hedge is expected to be effective; the ratio and correlation in this lesson are the evidence. Ineffective portions of a hedge go to profit as they arise.

Terms introduced

Cross hedge
A hedge using a contract on an asset different from, but related to, the one whose price is hedged.
Minimum-variance hedge ratio
The hedge ratio that makes the variance of a hedged position as small as possible: the correlation of spot and futures changes times the ratio of their standard deviations.
Hedge effectiveness
The share of the variance of an exposure that a hedge removes; for a minimum-variance hedge, the squared correlation between spot and futures changes.
Basis risk
The risk that the difference between the price of the hedged asset and the price of the hedging contract changes, so that the hedge does not offset the exposure exactly.
Collar
A long put and a short call on the same asset (with the call’s strike above the put’s), which keeps the asset’s value between the two strikes at little or no net cost.
