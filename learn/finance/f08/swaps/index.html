Swaps

A swap exchanges one stream of payments for another: most often a floating interest rate for a fixed one. Follow Brightcup Coffee as it fixes the rate on $200 million of floating-rate debt, work out the fixed rate that makes a new swap fair from the yield curve, and value the swap a year later when rates have risen.

The idea

Fictional case

Brightcup Coffee, the listed chain Ana studied in F06, has $630 million of debt. $200 million of it is a five-year bank loan whose interest rate is reset every year to a benchmark rate plus 1.5%. Brightcup’s board worries that rising rates would squeeze its profits just as it opens new cafés, and asks its treasurer whether the rate can be fixed without repaying the loan. The treasurer’s answer is an interest-rate swap.

A plain-vanilla interest-rate swap

In an interest-rate swap, two parties agree to exchange interest payments on an agreed amount, the notional principal, for a set period. In the commonest kind, the plain-vanilla swap, one party pays a fixed rate and the other pays a floating rate reset at each period from a benchmark. The notional itself is never exchanged; it only sets the size of the payments, and on each date only the difference between the two is paid. The party that pays fixed holds a payer swap; the party that receives fixed holds a receiver swap.

Brightcup agrees with its bank a five-year payer swap on $200 million with annual payments: Brightcup pays a fixed 4.4815% and receives the benchmark rate, each set at the start of the year and paid at its end. Combined with the loan, the floating payments cancel:

Whatever the benchmark does, Brightcup now pays 5.98% on the $200 million: the swap rate plus its own credit spread. The swap fixed the benchmark part of the rate; the 1.5% spread was fixed already in the loan.

The payments

In the first year the benchmark is set at 4.0%. At the end of the year Brightcup owes the bank 4.4815% × $200 million = $8,962,945 and is owed 4.0% × $200 million = $8,000,000, so it pays the difference, $962,945. If the benchmark is reset at 4.6% for the second year, the payment reverses: the bank pays Brightcup (4.6% − 4.4815%) × $200 million = $237,055.

Floating rates for US dollar swaps are now usually the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR), compounded over each period, which replaced the older LIBOR rates in 2023. The principle is the same whatever the benchmark.

In depth

A swap as two bonds

A payer swap has the same cash flows as two bonds held together: owning a floating-rate bond (receiving the floating payments) and having issued a fixed-rate bond (paying the fixed ones), both with face value N. At maturity the two face values cancel, which is why the notional need not change hands. So the swap’s value is the difference between the two bonds’ values.

Vpayer=Bfloat−BfixedV_{\text{payer}} = B_{\text{float}} - B_{\text{fixed}}Vpayer​=Bfloat​−Bfixed​

The floating-rate bond is easy to value on a reset date. Its next coupon is set at the market rate for the coming period, so it is worth exactly its face value: a bond that will always pay the going rate is always worth par when the rate is reset. The fixed-rate bond is valued like any bond, by discounting its coupons and face value. That needs a discount factor for each payment date.

Discount factors from the yield curve

A zero rate for t years is the yield on a zero-coupon bond paying in t years. The set of zero rates for different maturities is the zero curve, and each gives a discount factor, the value today of $1 at that date:

The swap rate

When a swap is agreed, its fixed rate is chosen so that neither side pays anything to enter: the swap is worth zero. With the floating bond worth N, the fixed bond must be worth N too, so the fixed rate is the coupon that prices a fixed-rate bond at par. That rate is the swap rate:

For Brightcup’s five-year swap: k = (1 − 0.802451) ÷ 4.408126 = 4.4815%. The swap rate is close to the five-year zero rate but a little below it, because the curve rises and the swap’s earlier payments are discounted at lower rates. Banks quote swap rates for each maturity, and the swap curve is one of the most watched curves in finance.

Valuing the swap a year later

A year later, just after the first payment, rates have risen. The new zero rates for the four remaining years are 4.8%, 4.9%, 5.0%, 5.1%. The floating bond is still worth par, $200 million, because its rate has just been reset. The fixed bond, paying 4.4815% for four more years, is now worth less, because its coupons are below the new market rates:

Bfixed=N(k∑t=14DFt′+DF4′)B_{\text{fixed}} = N\Big(k\sum_{t=1}^{4} DF'_t + DF'_4\Big)Bfixed​=N(kt=1∑4​DFt′​+DF4′​)

With the new discount factors summing to 3.546372 and the last one 0.819576, the fixed bond is worth $200 million × (4.4815% × 3.546372 + 0.819576) = $195.70 million. The swap is worth $200 million − $195.70 million = $4.30 million to Brightcup. Rising rates made the right to pay 4.4815% for four years valuable; the bank’s side has lost the same amount.

There is a quicker route to the same answer. A new four-year swap would now have a fixed rate of 5.0876%. Brightcup’s swap lets it pay 4.4815% instead, saving (5.0876% − 4.4815%) × $200 million a year for four years, and those savings discounted are the swap’s value: 0.6061% × 3.546372 × $200 million = $4.30 million.

A swap as a series of forwards

There is a second way to see a swap. Each payment exchanges a fixed amount for a floating one whose size is not yet known, which is a forward contract on the floating rate. A five-year swap is five such forwards, all at the same fixed rate. Because the market’s forward rates for later years are higher than for earlier ones when the curve rises, a single fixed rate is too high for the early forwards and too low for the later ones: Brightcup expects to pay on balance in the early years and receive later. Taken together, the five forwards are worth zero at the swap rate, but each one on its own is not.

This view explains why a swap’s value at a reset date depends on the whole curve, and why the counterparty that is expected to pay early in the swap, here Brightcup, carries the bank’s credit risk in the later years, when the bank is expected to pay it.

Why swaps exist

Textbooks often explain swaps by comparative advantage. Suppose a strong company can borrow at a fixed 5.0% or at the benchmark + 0.5%, and a weaker one at a fixed 7.0% or the benchmark + 1.5%. The weaker firm pays 2.0 points more for fixed-rate money but only 1.0 point more for floating, so it seems both can gain if each borrows where its disadvantage is smallest and they swap. The gain is often an illusion: the weaker firm’s floating loan can be repriced, or withdrawn, if its credit worsens, while fixed-rate money locks in its spread for the term. The difference in spreads is partly the price of that option, not free money.

The lasting reasons are simpler. Swaps let a borrower choose its interest-rate risk separately from its source of funds: it can borrow wherever money is cheapest or easiest, from banks or bond investors, in any currency, and then use a swap to get the kind of payments it wants. And swaps are cheap to arrange and to unwind compared with repaying and replacing a loan.

Currency swaps

A currency swap exchanges interest and principal in two currencies. A US company that borrows euros to build a plant in Germany can swap its euro payments into dollars: at the start it exchanges the euros it borrowed for dollars, each year it pays dollar interest and receives euro interest to pass to its lenders, and at maturity it exchanges the principals back at the rate fixed at the start. Unlike an interest-rate swap, the principals do change hands, because they are in different currencies. A currency swap can be valued like an interest-rate swap, as the difference between two bonds, one in each currency, with the foreign bond converted at today’s spot rate.

Counterparty risk and collateral

A swap that is worth $4.30 million to Brightcup is worth the same amount to the bank, negatively. If the bank failed, Brightcup would lose that value. Swap counterparties therefore exchange collateral as the value changes, under an agreement that sets thresholds and timing, so that neither is owed much at any time. Since the reforms that followed the 2008 financial crisis, most standard swaps between financial firms are cleared through central counterparties, like futures, and are reported to regulators. Swaps with companies such as Brightcup are often still private, with collateral or the bank’s credit limits managing the risk.

Common mistakes

  • Exchanging the notional in an interest-rate swap

    The notional only sizes the payments. In an interest-rate swap it is never paid; only the net interest changes hands. (In a currency swap, the principals are exchanged.)

  • Using the wrong period’s floating rate

    The floating rate is set at the start of each period and paid at the end. The payment at the end of year 2 uses the rate set at the end of year 1.

  • Forgetting the credit spread

    A swap fixes the benchmark, not the borrower’s spread. Brightcup’s all-in fixed rate is the swap rate plus its 1.5% spread.

  • Valuing the floating leg at anything but par on a reset date

    Right after a reset, a floating-rate bond pays the market rate for the next period and is worth its face value. Between reset dates it differs only by the coupon already fixed.

  • Getting the sign of the value wrong

    When rates rise, the fixed payer gains: it pays a rate now below the market’s. The receiver loses the same amount.

In practice

Interest-rate swaps are the largest derivatives market in the world by notional amount. Companies use them as Brightcup does, to fix the rate on bank loans or, the other way, to turn fixed-rate bonds into floating-rate debt when they expect rates to fall or want their interest costs to move with their revenues. Banks use them to manage the gap between fixed-rate mortgages and floating-rate deposits, and pension funds use long-dated swaps to match the interest-rate risk of their liabilities.

The swap curve is also a benchmark in its own right. Banks price fixed-rate loans and mortgages from swap rates of the same maturity plus a margin, and bond investors compare a company’s bond yield with the swap rate for its maturity: the difference, the swap spread of the bond, measures the credit risk investors charge for, separately from the general level of rates.

Companies report their swaps in the notes to their accounts, and under hedge accounting rules (ASC 815 in US GAAP, IFRS 9 internationally) a swap that qualifies as a cash-flow hedge can have its changes in value held in other comprehensive income until the hedged interest affects profit. Without hedge accounting, a swap’s value changes would pass through profit each period while the loan it hedges stays at cost, making earnings swing in a way that misrepresents the economics.

Summary

  • A plain-vanilla interest-rate swap exchanges fixed for floating interest on a notional amount that is never paid; only the net changes hands.
  • Paying fixed on a swap turns floating-rate debt into fixed: the all-in rate is the swap rate plus the borrower’s spread.
  • A payer swap is a long floating-rate bond and a short fixed-rate bond; on a reset date the floating bond is worth par.
  • The swap rate makes a new swap worth zero: k = (1 − DFn) ÷ ΣDFt, the par yield.
  • Later, a payer swap is worth N − (fixed bond) = (knew − k) × N × ΣDFt; rising rates help the fixed payer.
  • Currency swaps exchange interest and principal in two currencies.
  • Swaps carry counterparty risk, managed by collateral and, for standard swaps between dealers, central clearing.

Terms introduced

Interest-rate swap
An agreement to exchange interest payments on a notional principal for a set period, usually a fixed rate for a floating one.
Notional principal
The amount on which a swap’s interest payments are calculated. In an interest-rate swap it is never exchanged.
Payer swap
A swap position that pays the fixed rate and receives the floating rate. Its opposite is a receiver swap.
Zero rate
The annual yield on a zero-coupon bond maturing at a given date; the set of zero rates by maturity is the zero curve.
Swap rate
The fixed rate that gives a new interest-rate swap a value of zero: the coupon of a bond of the same maturity priced at par off the zero curve.
Currency swap
An agreement to exchange principal and interest payments in one currency for principal and interest in another, with the principals exchanged at the start and returned at maturity.
Collateral
Cash or securities one party gives another to cover what it owes if it fails; in derivatives, posted and returned as the contract’s value changes.
