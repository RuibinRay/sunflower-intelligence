Forwards and futures

A forward fixes a price privately between two parties; a futures contract does the same on an exchange, with the gains and losses settled every day through a margin account. Fix the dollar cost of Lantern’s euro bill with a forward, follow a week of a coffee futures position through its margin account, and compare the two.

The idea

Fictional case

Lantern owes Officine Varese (fictional) €38,000 on 31 March 2029, six months away. At today’s exchange rate of $1.0800 a euro that is $41,040, but the rate on the day matters, not today’s. Ana asks Coastline Bank what it would charge to fix the rate. Coastline quotes a six-month forward rate of $1.0920: if Lantern agrees now, on 31 March it will pay Coastline $41,496 and receive exactly €38,000 to pay the invoice. Meanwhile Hollis Avery, who trades a little himself, suggests that Lantern could hedge its coffee on an exchange instead of with Kilnworth.

A forward contract

A forward contract has four terms: what is bought and sold (the underlying asset and its quantity), the delivery price fixed today, the delivery date, and the two parties. The buyer holds a long position; the seller holds a short position. Nothing is paid when the contract is agreed. At maturity the buyer pays the delivery price and receives the asset, whatever the asset is then worth in the spot market, the market for immediate delivery.

The payoff of the long position at maturity is the spot price then, ST, less the delivery price K: the buyer receives something worth ST for K. The short position’s payoff is the mirror image. What one party gains the other loses, so a forward is a zero-sum contract.

Long: ST−KShort: K−ST\text{Long: } S_T - K \qquad \text{Short: } K - S_TLong: ST​−KShort: K−ST​

For Lantern, K is $1.0920 a euro. If the euro is worth $1.1500 on 31 March, the forward is worth (1.1500 − 1.0920) × 38,000 = $2,204 to Lantern: it buys euros at $1.0920 that would cost $1.1500 in the market. If the euro has fallen to $1.0300, the forward costs Lantern $2,356: it must still pay $1.0920. Either way the invoice costs $41,496 in total, because the payoff on the forward exactly offsets the change in the cost of the euros.

A futures contract

A futures contract is a forward with standard terms, traded on an exchange. The exchange sets the quantity in each contract, the grade of the asset, the delivery months and places, and the price steps. Because every contract for the same month is identical, buyers and sellers who have never met can trade with each other, and anyone can close a position later by making the opposite trade.

Two features make a futures contract safe to trade with strangers. First, the exchange’s clearing house stands between the two sides: once a trade is made, the clearing house becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer, so each trader’s counterparty is the clearing house, not the stranger. Second, gains and losses are settled every day. At the end of each trading day the exchange sets a settlement price and revalues every position at it, which is called marking to market: the day’s change in price times the contract size is paid by the losing side and received by the winning side, through accounts that each trader keeps with a broker.

The margin account

To trade, a trader deposits an initial margin with the broker, an amount set by the exchange to cover a large one-day move. Each day’s gain or loss is added to or taken from the account. If the balance falls below the maintenance margin, the broker makes a margin call, and the trader must pay in enough to restore the initial margin (this top-up is the variation margin) or the position is closed. Money above the initial margin can be withdrawn.

The case uses a fictional exchange contract on standard-grade green coffee: 5,000 kg a contract, priced in dollars a kg, with an initial margin of $2,500 and a maintenance margin of $1,800. Suppose Lantern bought one contract at $6.40 a kg, worth $32,000 of coffee. Each cent the price moves is $50 in the account.

On day 2 the balance falls to $1,750, below $1,800, so Lantern must pay in $750. By day 6 the price is $6.38, two cents below where Lantern bought, and the account holds $3,150. Lantern paid in $3,250 in all, so its loss is $100: exactly (6.38 − 6.40) × 5,000. The total is the same as a forward’s would be; what differs is that it arrived day by day, and that Lantern needed cash ready to meet the call.

In depth

Closing out, and delivery

Very few futures positions end in delivery. A trader who is long one March contract can leave the market at any time by selling one March contract: the two cancel at the clearing house, and the trader’s total gain or loss is the difference between the prices at which they bought and sold, times the size. Physical delivery is possible for most commodity contracts, but it happens at the exchange’s chosen places and grades, which rarely suit a particular business, so hedgers usually close out and buy what they need from their usual suppliers.

Convergence to the spot price

As the delivery date approaches, the futures price moves towards the spot price, and in the delivery period the two must be almost equal. If the futures price were above the spot price then, a trader could buy the asset in the spot market, sell a futures contract and deliver at once for a profit; if it were below, buyers would take delivery through futures rather than buy spot. This convergence is what lets a futures position held to the end fix a price, as a forward does. The difference between the spot price and the futures price before then is called the basis; how it moves is the main risk left in a futures hedge, and is taken up in module 3.

The trade-off for a business is between fit and safety. A forward can match the exact amount, date and grade, which is why Lantern buys its euros forward from its bank and fixes its coffee price with Kilnworth. But each side depends on the other to perform: if Kilnworth could not deliver at $37.50 in a year when coffee had doubled, Lantern’s hedge would fail just when it mattered. A futures position has almost no such counterparty risk, because the clearing house collects losses every day and holds margin against the next day’s, but it comes in fixed sizes and grades and needs cash for margin calls.

Leverage in a futures position

A futures position controls far more of the asset than the margin it needs. Lantern’s one contract covers $32,000 of coffee for $2,500 of initial margin, 12.8 times as much. A 7.8% fall in the price would take the whole initial margin. For a hedger that is not a problem in itself, since the loss on the futures is offset by a lower cost of coffee, but the futures loss is paid in cash now, while the saving on coffee arrives later. A hedger must be able to fund margin calls, or the hedge will be closed at the worst moment. Speculators use the same leverage to take large positions with little money, which is why futures markets need margin rules at all.

Cash settlement and the timing of gains

Some futures cannot be delivered at all. A contract on a share index or an interest rate is settled in cash: on the last day, the final settlement price is set equal to the index or rate, and the last day’s gain or loss closes the account. Commodity contracts usually allow delivery, but the economics are the same either way. What matters to a hedger is the total gain, which is fixed by the opening and closing prices, and the path, which decides how much cash it must put up along the way.

Because futures gains arrive daily and can be reinvested, and losses must be funded daily, a futures contract is not quite the same as a forward with the same delivery price. When interest rates are steady the difference is tiny, and this course treats futures and forward prices as equal. It matters for long-dated contracts on assets whose price moves with interest rates, such as bond futures.

Who trades futures

Three kinds of trader meet in a futures market. Hedgers have an exposure in the underlying: growers and exporters sell futures to fix what they will receive; roasters and food companies buy them to fix what they will pay. Speculators take positions in the hope of profit, and in doing so they take the risk that hedgers want to pass on and make it easy to trade. Arbitrageurs trade the futures against the spot asset whenever their prices move out of line, which keeps the futures price tied to the spot price by the cost of carry, the subject of the next lesson.

Common mistakes

  • Thinking a forward costs something to enter

    No money changes hands when a forward is agreed; its delivery price is set so that the contract is worth nothing to either side at the start. The bank earns its margin in the price it quotes.

  • Confusing the margin with the price

    Initial margin is a deposit against losses, returned when the position is closed. It is not a payment for the coffee and not a fee.

  • Forgetting the cash needed for margin calls

    A futures hedge’s losses are paid every day, while the offsetting gain on the business arrives later. A hedger without cash for margin calls may have to close the hedge at the worst time.

  • Getting the sign of a short position wrong

    A short position gains when the price falls and loses when it rises: its daily gain is (yesterday’s price − today’s) × size.

  • Assuming every futures position ends in delivery

    Most are closed out by an opposite trade. The total gain is the closing price less the opening price, times the size, whatever happened in between.

In practice

Futures exchanges list contracts on agricultural commodities (coffee, cocoa, sugar, grains), energy, metals, currencies, interest rates and share indexes. By value traded, financial futures on interest rates and share indexes far exceed commodity futures. The main arabica coffee futures contract is traded on an exchange in New York and covers many times Lantern’s annual needs in each contract, which is typical: futures are built for producers, large roasters and traders, and for investors who want exposure to a price without owning the goods.

Margin calls are where futures hedges are tested. When prices move sharply, a hedger’s losses on futures must be paid at once, even though the offsetting gain in the business arrives months later. In 2022, when European gas and electricity prices rose to many times their usual levels, several energy companies with large hedges faced margin calls so big that governments arranged emergency credit for them. Their hedges were doing their job; what the companies lacked was cash to carry them.

Businesses that need foreign currency usually buy it forward from their bank rather than through currency futures, because the bank will match the exact amount and date. The bank then manages its own position across all its customers, often hedging the net amount in the futures or interbank markets. A bank that sells a forward to a small business will also look at whether the business could pay if the contract moved against it, and may ask for security or set a limit.

Summary

  • A forward fixes a price today for delivery later; the long gains ST − K at maturity and the short K − ST.
  • A futures contract is a standardised forward traded on an exchange, with a clearing house as the counterparty to every trade.
  • Futures are marked to market daily: each day’s gain or loss passes through a margin account, and a balance below the maintenance margin brings a call back to the initial margin.
  • Most futures are closed out before delivery; the total gain is the change in price times the size, however it arrived.
  • Futures prices converge to the spot price at delivery; the gap before then is the basis.
  • Forwards fit a business’s exact needs but carry counterparty risk; futures are safer and easier to trade but come in fixed sizes and need cash for margin.

Terms introduced

Delivery price
The price fixed in a forward contract at which the asset will be bought and sold at maturity.
Long position
The position of the party who has agreed to buy (or who owns) an asset or contract, and gains when its price rises.
Short position
The position of the party who has agreed to sell (or who has sold) an asset or contract, and gains when its price falls.
Spot market
The market for immediate delivery of an asset, at the spot price.
Futures contract
A standardised agreement, traded on an exchange, to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a future date, with gains and losses settled every day through a clearing house.
Clearing house
The body that becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer of an exchange’s contracts, collecting margin so that each trade is honoured.
Marking to market
Revaluing a position at the current market price and settling the change, as a futures exchange does every day.
Initial margin
The deposit a trader must make to open a futures position, set to cover a large one-day loss.
Maintenance margin
The balance below which a futures margin account may not fall; below it, the trader receives a margin call.
Margin call
A demand to pay money into a margin account, usually enough to restore the initial margin, after losses take the balance below the maintenance margin.
Convergence
The movement of a futures price towards the spot price as the delivery date approaches.
Basis
The spot price of an asset less the price of a futures contract on it. It falls to about zero at delivery.
Counterparty risk
The risk that the other party to a contract fails to do what it promised.
