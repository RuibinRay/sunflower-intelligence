Calls, puts, payoffs and profits

An option gives the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell at a fixed price. Draw the payoffs and profits of calls and puts, see how Hollis Avery could protect his Brightcup shares or earn income from them, and compare a price cap on Lantern’s coffee with Kilnworth’s fixed price.

The idea

Fictional case

When Ana told Kilnworth she would fix her 2029 coffee price at $37.50, Kilnworth’s sales manager offered something else: Lantern could keep paying the list price, whatever it is, but never more than $40.00 a kg, for a fee of $1.20 a kg paid now. Ana now has a choice between fixing the price and capping it. At the same time, Hollis Avery, who owns 1,000 shares of Brightcup Coffee at $42.00, asks her how he could protect them from a fall without selling them. Both questions have the same answer: an option.

Calls and puts

An option gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price, the strike price, on or before a fixed date, its expiry. A call option is a right to buy; a put option is a right to sell. The holder pays the seller of the option, called its writer, a price for this right up front: the option premium. A European option can be exercised only at expiry; an American option at any time up to expiry. The diagrams in this lesson are for the value at expiry, where the two are the same.

Because the holder will use the right only when it pays to do so, a call’s payoff at expiry is the share price less the strike if that is positive, and zero otherwise; a put’s is the strike less the share price, or zero. The writer’s payoff is the negative of the holder’s: an option, like a forward, is a zero-sum contract once the premium is counted.

Payoff and profit

The payoff is what the option is worth at expiry; the profit is the payoff less the premium. Buying the $42 call at $3.99 pays off only if Brightcup ends above $42, and makes a profit only above $45.99, the strike plus the premium. At $50 the call pays $8.00 and the profit is $4.01 a share. Below $42 the buyer loses the whole premium, and no more. The writer’s profit is the mirror image: the premium if the share ends below $42, and an unlimited loss as the share rises. (These profits ignore the interest the premium could have earned until expiry, which is small over six months.)

Moneyness, intrinsic value and time value

An option that would pay off if exercised now is in the money: a call with a strike below the share price, or a put with a strike above it. One that would not is out of the money, and one with a strike at the share price is at the money. The amount an option would pay if exercised now is its intrinsic value; the rest of its price is time value, what buyers pay for the chance that the option ends further in the money before it expires.

With Brightcup at $42, the $40 call is in the money by $2.00, so its $5.05 is $2.00 of intrinsic value and $3.05 of time value. The $42 call is at the money: all of its $3.99 is time value. The $44 put is in the money by $2.00 and has $2.14 of time value. Time value is largest for options near the money, where the outcome is most uncertain, and it shrinks to nothing at expiry.

In depth

Protecting shares: the protective put

Hollis can insure his 1,000 shares by buying 10 contracts of the $40 put for $2.18 a share, $2,180 in all. However far Brightcup falls, he can sell his shares for $40 on 31 March, so his holding is worth at least $40,000, or $37,820 after the premium. If Brightcup falls to $34, he loses $8,000 on unprotected shares but only $4,180 with the puts: $2,000 down to the strike, plus the premium. If Brightcup rises, he keeps the whole gain, less the $2,180 he paid. A share plus a put is a protective put: like an insurance policy with a deductible of $2 a share and a premium of $2.18.

Earning income: the covered call

Alternatively, Hollis could write 10 contracts of the $46 call and receive $2.37 a share, $2,370. If Brightcup ends below $46 the calls expire worthless and he keeps the premium. If it ends above, his shares are called away at $46, so the most his position can be worth is $48,370. Owning a share and writing a call on it is a covered call: it earns income in a flat market by giving up the gains in a strong one. It does not protect against a fall beyond the premium received.

Combining options: spreads and straddles

Options can be combined to shape a payoff. A bull spread buys a call and writes another with a higher strike. Buying the $42 call at $3.99 and writing the $46 call at $2.37 costs $1.62 a share. It pays nothing below $42, rises dollar for dollar between $42 and $46, and pays at most $4.00 above $46: a best profit of $2.38 and a break-even of $43.62. It is a cheaper bet on a moderate rise than the call alone.

A straddle buys a call and a put with the same strike. The $42 straddle costs $3.99 + $3.07 = $7.06 a share and pays |ST − 42|. It makes money if Brightcup moves more than $7.06 in either direction, below $34.94 or above $49.06: a bet on volatility, not on direction. Its writer bets that the share will stay near $42.

Leverage in an option

An option magnifies the percentage gains and losses of the share. If Brightcup rises 19% to $50, the share gains $8.00 on $42.00, but the $42 call gains $4.01 on $3.99, about 101%. If Brightcup stays at $42, the share is unchanged and the call loses everything. That is why a speculator who expects a rise may buy calls instead of shares, and why the same speculator can lose all of the money in a few months. For a hedger the leverage cuts the other way: a small premium protects a large holding.

Exercise, assignment and closing out

Few options are held to expiry. Like futures, exchange-traded options can be closed at any time by the opposite trade: the holder sells the option back in the market and receives its current price, which includes any time value left. Exercising early throws that time value away, which is why the holder of an American call on a share that pays no dividend should sell the option rather than exercise it. When a holder does exercise, the exchange’s clearing house assigns the obligation to one of the writers, who must deliver the shares (for a call) or buy them (for a put) at the strike. Writers post margin with their brokers, as futures traders do, so that the clearing house is protected if they cannot.

Options on share indexes and many other options are settled in cash: the holder receives the payoff and no shares change hands. Options on futures, common for commodities, deliver a futures position at the strike. In every case the payoff at expiry is the one in this lesson’s formulas.

A cap is a call

Kilnworth’s offer is an option. Lantern pays the list price, but if it rises above $40.00 Kilnworth absorbs the excess: that is a call on the coffee price with a strike of $40.00, held by Lantern, bought for $1.20 a kg. Lantern’s cost a kg becomes:

Common mistakes

  • Confusing payoff with profit

    The payoff is what the option is worth at expiry; profit subtracts the premium. A call exercised in the money can still lose money overall.

  • Thinking the writer’s loss is limited

    The buyer of an option can lose only the premium. The writer of a call can lose without limit as the price rises, and the writer of a put up to the strike less the premium.

  • Mixing up calls and puts

    A call is the right to buy and gains when the price rises; a put is the right to sell and gains when it falls. Protecting shares needs a put; capping a cost needs a call.

  • Forgetting the contract size

    Share option premiums are quoted a share, but one contract covers 100 shares. A quote of $3.99 means $399 a contract.

  • Calling an option cheap because it is out of the money

    An out-of-the-money option costs less than an in-the-money one, but it is less likely to pay off. Its whole price is time value, which is lost if the price does not move.

In practice

Options on individual shares, share indexes, currencies, interest rates and commodities trade on exchanges around the world, and many more are arranged privately between banks and their customers. Investors use them as Hollis might: puts to protect a portfolio, covered calls to add income, spreads and straddles to express a view on direction or volatility. Companies use them as Lantern might: caps on interest rates on floating-rate loans, currency options to protect the value of foreign sales while keeping the upside, and commodity options to cap the cost of fuel or raw materials.

Brightcup could have hedged its floating-rate loan with options instead of the swap of lesson 4. An interest-rate cap pays the borrower whenever the benchmark rate is above the cap’s strike rate, so the loan’s rate can rise no higher than the strike plus the spread, while the borrower still benefits if rates fall. Like Kilnworth’s cap, it costs a premium up front; the swap costs nothing up front but gives up the benefit of a fall.

Options are also hidden in many ordinary contracts. A mortgage that can be repaid early contains a call option for the borrower on the loan; a convertible bond contains a call on the issuer’s shares; a price cap in a supply agreement, like Kilnworth’s, is a call held by the buyer. Executive share options and the real options of F05, to expand, delay or abandon a project, are valued with the same ideas. Recognising the option in a contract is the first step to pricing it.

Summary

  • A call is the right to buy at the strike; a put is the right to sell. The holder pays a premium; the writer receives it and bears the obligation.
  • Payoffs at expiry: call max(ST − K, 0), put max(K − ST, 0); profit = payoff − premium; the writer’s is the negative.
  • In-the-money options have intrinsic value; the rest of the price is time value, largest near the money and zero at expiry.
  • A protective put sets a floor under a holding; a covered call earns income and caps the gain.
  • Bull spreads bet cheaply on a moderate rise; straddles bet on a large move in either direction.
  • A price cap is a call held by the buyer: it removes the bad outcomes and keeps the good, for a fee up front.

Terms introduced

Option
A contract that gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price on or before a fixed date.
Strike price
The fixed price at which an option’s holder may buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying asset. Also called the exercise price.
Call option
An option to buy the underlying asset at the strike price.
Put option
An option to sell the underlying asset at the strike price.
Option premium
The price the buyer of an option pays its writer for the right it gives.
European option
An option that can be exercised only on its expiry date.
American option
An option that can be exercised on any day up to and including its expiry date.
Payoff
What a derivative position is worth at expiry or maturity, before counting what was paid for it.
In the money
Describes an option that would pay off if exercised now: a call with strike below the price of the underlying, or a put with strike above it.
Intrinsic value
What an option would pay if exercised now: max(S − K, 0) for a call, max(K − S, 0) for a put.
Time value
The part of an option’s price above its intrinsic value: what buyers pay for the chance of a better outcome before expiry.
Protective put
A holding of an asset together with a put option on it, which sets a floor under the holding’s value at the strike, less the premium.
Covered call
A holding of an asset together with a written call on it, which earns the premium and caps the holding’s value at the strike.
Bull spread
A long call (or put) at one strike and a short call (or put) at a higher strike, which gains from a moderate rise in price at a lower cost than a call alone.
Straddle
A call and a put on the same asset with the same strike and expiry, which gains when the price moves far in either direction.
