Black–Scholes–Merton and the Greeks

As a binomial tree’s steps shrink, its price becomes a formula: Black–Scholes–Merton. Price Brightcup’s options with it, read what each term means, measure how the price responds to the share, time, volatility and interest rates (the Greeks), hedge a written option with delta, and find the volatility the market’s price implies.

The idea

Fictional case

The binomial tree priced the $42 Brightcup call at $3.99 with hundreds of steps. Hollis wants to understand the formula the tree was heading for, and two practical things: how much his $40 puts protect him if Brightcup moves a little, and whether the exchange’s prices are telling him something about how risky the market thinks Brightcup is.

The formula

In 1973 Fischer Black and Myron Scholes, with Robert Merton, showed that if a share’s price moves continuously with constant volatility, a written option can be hedged continuously with the share, and the hedged position must earn the risk-free rate. The price that makes that true is the Black–Scholes–Merton model. For a European call and put on a share that pays no dividend before expiry:

N(·) is the cumulative standard normal distribution, which Excel gives as NORM.S.DIST(x, TRUE). The inputs are the five things the tree needed: the share price, the strike, the time to expiry in years, the continuously compounded risk-free rate and the volatility. As in the tree, the share’s expected return does not appear.

Brightcup’s $42 options

With S0 = $42.00, K = $42, T = 0.5, r = 4.402% and σ = 30%:

  • d1 = [ln(42/42) + (0.04402 + 0.045) × 0.5] ÷ (0.30 × √0.5) = 0.2098; d2 = 0.2098 − 0.2121 = -0.0023.
  • N(d1) = 0.5831; N(d2) = 0.4991.
  • Call: 42.00 × 0.5831 − 41.0857 × 0.4991 = $3.9851.
  • Put: 41.0857 × 0.5009 − 42.00 × 0.4169 = $3.0708.

These are the case’s quotes, $3.99 and $3.07, and they satisfy put–call parity exactly. Reading the call formula term by term: it is what the call holder expects to receive, the share when the option ends in the money, less what the holder expects to pay, the strike in the same cases, both measured with risk-neutral probabilities and discounted. N(d2), here 0.4991, is the risk-neutral probability that the call ends in the money. N(d1), 0.5831, is the call’s delta: the number of shares in the hedge.

What the model assumes

The formula rests on assumptions: the share’s log returns are normally distributed with constant volatility; the interest rate is constant; there are no dividends before expiry (the model extends easily to known dividends or a dividend yield); trading is continuous and costless, and short selling is allowed. The one that matters most is constant volatility. Real share prices sometimes jump, and their volatility changes over time, so the model is best understood as a consistent way to translate between prices and volatilities, not as a literal description of markets.

In depth

The Greeks

An option’s value depends on five inputs, and a holder or a dealer needs to know how much it changes when each one moves. The sensitivities are named after Greek letters, and the Black–Scholes–Merton model gives each in closed form.

Delta of a call is N(d₁), of a put N(d₁) − 1. Gamma and vega are the same for a call and a put with the same strike and expiry.

Delta runs from 0 to 1 for a call and from −1 to 0 for a put: near 0 for options far out of the money, near ±1 deep in the money, and about ±0.5 at the money. Gamma measures how fast delta changes; it is largest for at-the-money options close to expiry, where a small move can switch the option from worthless to valuable. Vega is positive for both calls and puts: more volatility means more chance of a large payoff, and the option’s downside is capped. Theta is usually negative: time value drains away as expiry approaches, faster at the end. Rho is positive for calls, since a higher rate lowers the present value of the strike the call holder pays, and negative for puts.

Time decay

Theta says how much value an option loses as a day passes with nothing else changing. The $42 call is worth $3.9851 today and $3.9730 tomorrow at the same share price: $0.0120 lost overnight, close to its theta of -0.0120. Time decay is not steady. An at-the-money option loses its time value slowly at first and fast at the end: with a week to go, the same call would be worth $0.71 and losing $0.0522 a day. Writers of options collect this decay; buyers pay it, and need the share to move enough, and soon enough, to make up for it.

Using delta and gamma

Delta gives a quick estimate of how the option moves with the share. If Brightcup rises $1 to $43, delta alone says the call rises by $0.5831; adding half of gamma times the move squared improves the estimate: 3.9851 + 0.5831 + ½ × 0.0438 = $4.5900. The formula at $43 gives $4.5897. Delta is the slope of the option’s price curve and gamma its curvature, just as duration and convexity describe a bond’s price curve against its yield.

Hedging a written option

A dealer who sells Hollis 10 contracts of the $42 call (1,000 options) is short 1,000 × 0.5831 = 583 deltas. It buys 583 Brightcup shares, so that a small move in the share leaves its position unchanged. If Brightcup rises to $43, the call’s delta rises to 0.6258, and the dealer must buy 43 more shares to stay hedged; if Brightcup falls, it sells. A delta-hedged dealer always buys after rises and sells after falls, and loses a little on each adjustment. Those losses are what the option’s time value pays for: a dealer earns theta and pays for gamma, and when the share moves as much as the volatility in the price assumed, the two balance.

Delta also measures Hollis’s position. His 1,000 shares have a delta of 1,000. The 1,000 $40 puts he bought each have a delta of -0.3300, so together his holding behaves like 670 shares for small moves: the puts have removed about a third of his exposure today, and they remove more as Brightcup falls towards $40 and the puts’ deltas move towards −1.

Dividends: Merton’s extension

Merton extended the formula to a share that pays a continuous dividend yield q: replace S0 with S0e−qT throughout, including inside d1, because the option holder does not receive the dividends that the share pays. The same change prices options on share indexes (q is the index’s dividend yield), on currencies (q is the foreign interest rate, as in interest-rate parity) and, with q = r, on futures. If Brightcup paid a 2% yield continuously, its $42 call would be worth $3.75 instead of $3.99.

Implied volatility

Four of the five inputs can be observed: the share price, the strike, the time and the interest rate. Volatility cannot; it has to be estimated, and different people estimate it differently. So traders turn the formula round. Given an option’s market price, they solve for the volatility that makes the formula give that price: the implied volatility. If the $42 Brightcup call traded at $4.50 instead of $3.99, its implied volatility would be 34.4%. The call’s vega tells roughly how far: the $0.51 difference ÷ 0.1159 a point ≈ 4.4 points above 30%.

Option prices are therefore quoted and compared as implied volatilities, which are comparable across strikes, expiries and shares in a way that dollar prices are not. A volatility of 25% would price the call at $3.41, 35% at $4.56. When traders say options are expensive, they mean their implied volatility is high relative to the volatility they expect.

The volatility smile

If the model were exactly right, every option on Brightcup with the same expiry would have the same implied volatility. In real markets it varies with the strike. For share indexes, puts with low strikes usually have higher implied volatilities than options near the money, a pattern called the volatility smile (or skew). It shows that the market prices large falls as more likely than a normal distribution with constant volatility allows, a lesson many traders took from the crash of October 1987. The model survives as a quoting convention, with a different volatility for each strike and expiry.

Common mistakes

  • Using an annually compounded rate in the formula

    Black–Scholes–Merton uses a continuously compounded rate. Convert: r = ln(1 + R). The case’s 4.5% a year is 4.402%.

  • Using time in months or days

    T is in years: six months is 0.5. Volatility is a year’s standard deviation; a monthly figure must be scaled by √12.

  • Reading N(d₁) as the probability of exercise

    N(d₂) is the risk-neutral probability that a call ends in the money; N(d₁) is its delta. Neither is a real-world forecast.

  • Treating a delta hedge as permanent

    Delta changes as the share and time move (gamma and the passage of time), so a hedge must be rebalanced.

  • Using the formula for American puts or dividend-paying shares

    The basic formula is for European options on shares with no dividends before expiry. Use a tree for American puts, and adjust the share price or use a dividend yield for dividends.

In practice

The Black–Scholes–Merton model, and models built on it, are used every day to quote options as implied volatilities, to calculate the Greeks that traders and risk managers watch, and to value options in company accounts. Scholes and Merton received the Nobel memorial prize in economics in 1997 for the work (Black had died in 1995). Index options markets publish volatility indexes built from option prices, such as the Cboe’s VIX for the S&P 500, which the press calls a “fear gauge” because implied volatility rises when investors pay up for protection.

Option pricing reaches well beyond options markets. Merton saw that a company’s shareholders hold, in effect, a call on the company’s assets with a strike equal to its debt: if the assets end worth less than the debt, the shareholders walk away and the lenders take the assets. That insight underlies models that banks and rating agencies use to estimate the probability that a company defaults, which F09 returns to. Warrants, convertible bonds and executive share options are all priced with versions of the formula.

The Greeks are also how risk is reported for whole books of options. A dealer adds the deltas, gammas and vegas of thousands of positions and sets limits on each: how much the book can lose if the market moves 1%, or if volatility rises a point. The final module applies the same thinking to whole portfolios, with Value at Risk.

He decides the insurance is worth that until Brightcup’s results in February.

Summary

  • Black–Scholes–Merton: c = S0N(d1) − Ke−rTN(d2), p = Ke−rTN(−d2) − S0N(−d1), with continuous r and T in years.
  • N(d2) is the risk-neutral probability a call ends in the money; N(d1) is its delta.
  • The Greeks: delta (share price), gamma (delta’s change), vega (volatility), theta (time), rho (interest rate).
  • ΔV ≈ Δ·ΔS + ½Γ(ΔS)²; a written option is hedged by holding delta shares and rebalancing.
  • Implied volatility is the σ that makes the formula match the market price; options are quoted and compared by it.
  • Implied volatility varies with the strike (the smile), showing where the model’s constant-volatility assumption fails.

Terms introduced

Black–Scholes–Merton model
The model that prices European options on an asset whose price follows a lognormal process with constant volatility, by continuous delta hedging: c = S0N(d1) − Ke−rTN(d2).
Gamma
The change in an option’s delta for a $1 change in the price of the underlying; the curvature of the option’s value.
Vega
The change in an option’s value for a one-point change in volatility.
Theta
The change in an option’s value as time passes, usually quoted for one day; usually negative for an option holder.
Rho
The change in an option’s value for a one-point change in the risk-free interest rate.
Implied volatility
The volatility that, put into an option pricing model, gives the option’s market price.
Volatility smile
The pattern of implied volatilities that differ by strike for options with the same expiry, contrary to the constant volatility the Black–Scholes–Merton model assumes. Also called the volatility skew.
