    Why firms hedge

    A business is exposed to prices it does not control: what it pays for its materials, the exchange rate on what it buys abroad, the interest rate on its loans. Measure Lantern’s exposure to the price of coffee, see what a fixed-price contract does to it, and work out when removing a risk adds value and when it only costs money.

    Before you start

    You will be able to

    • Identify a business’s exposures to commodity prices, exchange rates and interest rates, and measure each as the change in cash flow for a change in the price.
    • Explain what a derivative is and name the four basic contracts: forwards, futures, swaps and options.
    • Calculate a business’s earnings with and without a hedge across a range of prices, and explain why a hedge gives up gains as well as losses.
    • Explain why hedging at a fair price does not change a firm’s value in perfect markets, and the frictions (distress costs, covenants, taxes, investment plans and undiversified owners) that make it worth doing.
    • Distinguish hedging from speculation, and recognise natural hedges that reduce an exposure without a contract.

    The idea

    Fictional case

    It is September 2028. Lantern’s second kitchen is open, Coastline’s loan is drawn and Hollis Avery owns 30% of the company. Ana’s five-year model (F07) shows 2029 EBITDA of $84,693, enough to pay the year’s debt service of $24,553 three times over. But the model takes one thing for granted: what Lantern pays Kilnworth Roasting for coffee. Lantern buys about 2,400 kg of roasted coffee a year at $36.00 a kg, a bill of $86,400. Kilnworth has just written to say that green coffee prices have been rising, and offers to fix its price for every delivery in 2029 at $37.50 a kg if Lantern signs by the end of October. At the same time Ana has ordered two espresso machines from Officine Varese (fictional), €38,000 payable on delivery on 31 March 2029. She has to decide whether to fix these prices now or take her chances.

    Exposure: how much a price moves the business

    A business faces many risks, but some come from prices set in markets it cannot influence: the price of what it buys or sells, the exchange rate on payments in another currency, the interest rate on floating-rate debt. A risk exposure measures how much a cash flow, or earnings, changes when one of those prices changes. It turns a vague worry (“coffee is getting dearer”) into a number that can be compared with the business’s capacity to bear it.

    Lantern’s exposure to coffee is simple to measure. Every $1 a kg added to Kilnworth’s price costs 2,400 × $1 = $2,400 a year, and F07’s forecast assumed the cost of goods stays at 2027’s share of sales, which in effect assumes the price stays where it is. The exposure is $2,400 of EBITDA for each $1 a kg, for as long as Lantern cannot pass the change on in its own prices.

    EBITDA2029(P)=EBITDAplan−Q (P−P0)\text{EBITDA}_{2029}(P) = \text{EBITDA}_{\text{plan}} - Q\,(P - P_0)EBITDA2029​(P)=EBITDAplan​−Q(P−P0​)

    Here Q is 2,400 kg, P0 the $36.00 the plan assumes and P the price Lantern actually pays. A 20% rise, to $43.20 a kg, takes EBITDA from $84,693 to $67,413. A 50% rise, to $54, takes it to $41,493. Coffee prices can move that much in a year or two, because harvests in a few producing countries depend on the weather.

    A contract that fixes the price

    Kilnworth’s offer is a forward contract in everything but name: an agreement today to buy a set quantity at a set price on set future dates. If Lantern signs, its 2029 coffee bill is 2,400 × $37.50 = $90,000 whatever happens to the market, and its EBITDA is $81,093 in every case.

    The table shows the whole bargain. Fixing the price removes the bad outcomes and the good ones together: if coffee falls to $30, Lantern still pays $37.50 and is $18,000 worse off than if it had waited. A hedge is not a bet that prices will rise. It is a decision to give up what a favourable move would bring in return for protection against an unfavourable one.

    Derivatives: contracts on a price

    A derivative is a contract whose value depends on the price of something else, its underlying asset: a commodity such as coffee, a currency, an interest rate, a share or an index. Four kinds of contract are the building blocks of the derivatives market, and this course takes them in turn.

    • Forwards: a private agreement to buy or sell at a fixed price on a future date. Kilnworth’s offer, and the currency forward a bank would sell Lantern for its euros.
    • Futures: standardised forwards traded on an exchange, settled every day through a clearing house (lesson 2).
    • Swaps: an exchange of one stream of payments for another, such as a floating interest rate for a fixed one (lesson 4).
    • Options: the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell at a fixed price. An option protects against the bad outcome and keeps the good one, for a price paid up front (module 2).

    The same contract can serve opposite purposes. A café that buys coffee for delivery in 2029 at a fixed price is hedging: the contract offsets a risk it already has. A trader with no use for coffee who makes the same purchase is engaged in speculation: taking on a risk in the hope of profit. Whether a position is a hedge depends on what else its holder owns, not on the contract itself.

    In depth

    When hedging cannot add value

    Start with a world without frictions, the one Modigliani and Miller used for capital structure (F05): no taxes, no costs of financial distress, everyone able to trade at the same prices. In that world hedging cannot make a firm more valuable. A forward at a fair price costs nothing to enter and has an expected payoff of about zero, so it changes the spread of outcomes but not their average. And if shareholders want less coffee risk, they can hedge it themselves, or hold Lantern in a diversified portfolio where coffee prices hardly matter. What investors can do for themselves at the same cost, they will not pay a firm to do for them.

    The argument also shows how to measure what a hedge costs. The cost is not the difference between the fixed price and today’s price. It is the difference between the fixed price and the price expected when the coffee is bought, because that is what Lantern gives up on average.

    Expected cost of the hedge=Q (F−E[PT])\text{Expected cost of the hedge} = Q\,\big(F - \mathbb{E}[P_T]\big)Expected cost of the hedge=Q(F−E[PT​])

    Ana’s best guess of Kilnworth’s 2029 price, from its recent increases, is $37.00 a kg. On that view the fixed price costs 2,400 × ($37.50 − $37.00) = $1,200 a year in expected terms, not the $3,600 that comparing it with today’s $36.00 suggests. Kilnworth charges something to carry the risk Lantern is passing to it, and that charge is the price of the insurance.

    Frictions that make hedging worth doing

    Real markets have frictions, and each one gives a reason to hedge.

    • The costs of financial distress. A business that breaks a loan covenant or runs short of cash pays for it: waiver fees, a higher interest rate, suppliers who want cash on delivery, plans postponed. These costs fall only in the bad outcomes, so a hedge that removes the bad outcomes removes their expected cost. This is the main reason small and heavily borrowed firms hedge.
    • Covenants and lenders. A lender that can see a hedge in place may lend more, or more cheaply, because the borrower’s cash flow is more predictable.
    • Investment plans. A firm that pays for investment from its own cash flow may have to cut good projects in a bad year if raising outside money is expensive. Hedging keeps the money for the plan available when it is needed.
    • Taxes. When losses earn no immediate tax credit, a firm with volatile profits pays more tax on average than one with steady profits of the same average. Smoothing taxable income can lower the expected tax bill.
    • Owners and managers who cannot diversify. Ana has most of her wealth in Lantern. The diversified investor of the frictionless argument does not describe her, and a risk she cannot spread is worth paying something to remove.

    Ana puts rough numbers on the first reason. If coffee rose by half or more, EBITDA would fall to about $41,493 and, with the kitchen’s first year still uncertain, she thinks there is roughly a 10% chance that 2029 would then end with Lantern needing a waiver from Coastline, new terms and some postponed spending, costing perhaps $20,000. The expected cost of distress the hedge removes is 0.10 × $20,000 = $2,000, more than the $1,200 the hedge is expected to cost. These are judgements, not facts, but writing them down makes the decision one that can be argued with.

    Hedge ifPr⁡(distress)×Cost of distress avoided>Q (F−E[PT])\text{Hedge if}\quad \Pr(\text{distress}) \times \text{Cost of distress avoided} > Q\,\big(F - \mathbb{E}[P_T]\big)Hedge ifPr(distress)×Cost of distress avoided>Q(F−E[PT​])

    How much to hedge

    Hedging is not all or nothing. The hedge ratio is the share of the exposure covered. If Lantern fixed the price for half its coffee, EBITDA at $43.20 would be $74,253, halfway between the unhedged $67,413 and the fully hedged $81,093. Many firms hedge a high share of the next year’s needs, which they know well, and less of later years, which are less certain. Hedging more than the exposure, or for a quantity the business may not need, is no longer a hedge: the excess is a position that gains or loses on its own.

    Natural hedges and pass-through

    Some exposures shrink without any contract. A business can raise its own prices when its costs rise: if Lantern could pass on half of any rise in the price of coffee through its menu, its exposure would be $1,200 a year for each $1 a kg, not $2,400. A business with costs and revenues in the same foreign currency has a natural hedge: a fall in the currency lowers both. Measuring the exposure net of these offsets comes before choosing a contract, because hedging an exposure that is already offset creates a new risk instead of removing one.

    A hedging policy

    Firms that hedge write down a policy, approved by the board or the owners, so that hedging decisions are not made trade by trade on a feeling about prices. A policy answers a few questions: which exposures are hedged and which are accepted; how much of each, and for how far ahead; which contracts may be used and with whom; who may sign; and how results are reported. Lantern’s policy can be one page. A large company’s runs to many, but the questions are the same.

    Common mistakes

    • Measuring the cost of a hedge against today’s price

      What a forward costs is the fixed price less the price expected at the date of purchase. Comparing it with today’s price overstates the cost when prices are expected to rise, and understates it when they are expected to fall.

    • Judging a hedge by whether it made money

      A hedge that loses money because prices fell has done its job: the business paid more for coffee than it might have, but it knew the price in advance. Judge a hedge by whether it reduced the risk it was meant to reduce.

    • Hedging more than the exposure

      Fixing the price of more coffee than Lantern will use, or for years it may not trade, creates a position that gains and loses on its own. That is speculation, whatever the policy calls it.

    • Ignoring natural hedges

      If a business can pass a cost on to its customers, or earns in the same currency it spends, part of the exposure is already offset. Hedging the gross amount over-hedges.

    • Assuming hedging always adds value

      In frictionless markets it adds none. The case for hedging rests on specific frictions, such as distress costs and owners who cannot diversify, and should name them.

    In practice

    Businesses of every size hedge the prices that matter most to them. Airlines fix the price of part of their fuel; food manufacturers buy wheat, sugar and cocoa forward; exporters sell foreign currency forward to fix the value of their sales; companies with floating-rate loans swap them into fixed rates. Large companies’ annual reports describe these hedges and their policies in the notes on financial risk management, usually exposure by exposure: what the risk is, how much is hedged and how. Reading those notes is a good way to see the ideas of this lesson applied.

    Most firms hedge less than all of an exposure and for a limited period, often one or two years ahead, because their needs further out are uncertain and because a competitor that does not hedge will have lower costs if prices fall. A café that fixed its coffee price high while the café across the street bought cheaply could find itself unable to match its rival’s prices. Hedging is a way to buy time to adjust, not a way to escape the market for ever.

    Terms introduced

    Risk exposure
    How much a cash flow or earnings changes for a given change in a market price, such as a commodity price, an exchange rate or an interest rate.
    Derivative
    A contract whose value depends on the price of an underlying asset or rate. Forwards, futures, swaps and options are the basic kinds.
    Underlying asset
    The asset, rate or index whose price determines a derivative’s value: for example coffee, a currency, an interest rate or a share.
    Forward contract
    A private agreement to buy or sell a set quantity of an asset at a price fixed today (the delivery price), on a set future date.
    Speculation
    Taking on a risk in the hope of profit, rather than to offset a risk already held.
    Hedge ratio
    The size of a hedge relative to the exposure it covers: the share of a quantity whose price is fixed, or, for a futures hedge, the number of units of futures per unit of exposure.
    Natural hedge
    An offset that reduces an exposure without a financial contract, such as costs and revenues in the same currency or the ability to pass cost increases on to customers.
