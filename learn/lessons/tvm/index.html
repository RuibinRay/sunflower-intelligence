A dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year. Discounting puts every future cash flow into today’s money, and a DCF adds them up.

The idea

Money you have today can be invested to earn a return, so a promise of money later is worth less than the same amount now. How much less depends on the return you give up by waiting: the discount rate.

Discounting is compounding run backwards. At 10%, $100 grows to $110 in a year, so $110 a year from now is worth $100 today. Two years away, the same $110 is worth 110 ÷ 1.10² = $90.91.

A discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation does this for every year of a business’s forecast free cash flow, then adds a terminal value for everything after the forecast. For a company, the discount rate is its cost of capital (WACC).

The formula

Value = CF1(1 + r) + CF2(1 + r)2 + … + CFN + TVN(1 + r)N, TVN = CFN × (1 + g)r − g

CFt
free cash flow in year t
r
discount rate per year (WACC for a whole company)
N
the last year of the forecast
g
growth every year after N, forever; it must be below r
TVN
terminal value: what all the years after N are worth at the end of year N (the Gordon growth formula)

Most of the value comes after year 5, so the forever-growth rate and the discount rate matter more than the forecast itself.

The common mistake

Discounting the terminal value by the wrong number of years

The Gordon formula uses year N+1’s cash flow to give a value at the end of year N, so it is discounted N years, exactly like year N’s cash flow. Discounting it four years in the example gives $1,515.7k; forgetting to discount it gives $2,025.6k. Both are wrong.

Remember

  • Present value = cash flow ÷ (1 + r)t. A higher rate or a later date means a lower value.
  • A DCF is the present value of the forecast plus the present value of the terminal value. Check the terminal value’s share; it is often most of the answer.
  • Growth forever must stay below the discount rate, or the formula breaks.
