Split a market into groups that want different things, describe each group against the average customer, and choose the one you can serve best.

The idea

A needs-based segmentation groups customers by what they value, using survey ratings such as price, quality or convenience. Each rating is first standardised, (answer − mean) ÷ standard deviation, so a 1–10 question doesn’t outweigh a 1–5 one. Then k-means puts respondents who answered alike in the same segment.

k-means returns as many groups as you ask for, structure or not, so choose the number with the silhouette: how much closer each respondent sits to its own segment than to the next. Above 0.5 is clear structure, 0.25 to 0.5 weak, and below 0.25 the segments are a convenient cut rather than a discovery.

Describe each segment with an index against the average respondent, segment mean ÷ overall mean × 100. Then size it in people and in spend, and score how well its needs fit what you do well. The target is a segment that is big enough, spends enough and wants your strengths.

The formula

z = answer − meanstandard deviation, index = segment meanoverall mean × 100, spend per head = share of spendshare of people × average spend

The common mistake

Grouping on raw answers

Say spend has a standard deviation of $150 and a rating one of 1.5. A typical difference in spend then adds 150² = 22,500 to a squared distance, and a typical difference in a rating only 1.5² = 2.25: ten thousand times less. Unstandardised, k-means splits shoppers by spend alone. Standardise the ratings, and use spend to size segments, not to find them.

Remember

  • Standardise the answers, let k-means group respondents who answered alike, choose the number of segments with the silhouette, and check the groups make business sense.
  • Index = segment mean ÷ overall mean × 100. Read a profile by what stands out from 100.
  • Target on size, spend per head and fit together. Spend per head = share of spend ÷ share of people × average spend.
