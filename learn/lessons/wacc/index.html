  4. The cost of capital

Investors lend and invest only if they expect a return. The cost of capital is that expected return, and it is the rate a DCF discounts at.

The idea

A company raises money from two kinds of investor. Lenders want interest; shareholders want a return high enough to compensate for the risk they carry. The cost of capital is the blend of the two: the return the company must earn on a project just to leave its investors as well off as before.

The cost of equity can’t be read off a contract, so it is estimated. The capital asset pricing model (CAPM) starts from the risk-free rate and adds the market’s equity risk premium, scaled by beta: how much the share tends to move with the market. Only that market risk earns a premium, because company-specific risk can be diversified away.

Debt is cheaper. Lenders are paid before shareholders, so they take less risk, and interest is tax-deductible, so each dollar of interest costs the company less than a dollar. The weighted average cost of capital (WACC) weights each cost by its share of the company’s market value.

The formula

kE = rf + β × ERP, WACC = EV × kE + DV × kD × (1 − T)

kE
cost of equity: the return shareholders expect
rf
risk-free rate, such as a government bond yield
β
beta: the share’s sensitivity to market moves (the market itself is 1)
ERP
equity risk premium: how much more the market is expected to return than rf
E, D, V
market values of equity and debt, and their total V = E + D
kD, T
pre-tax cost of debt, and the tax rate that interest is deducted at

The common mistake

Forgetting the tax saving on interest

Using the 7% pre-tax cost of debt gives 0.75 × 9.45% + 0.25 × 7% = 8.84%. That overstates the cost of capital by more than a third of a percentage point, and every DCF built on it comes out too low.

Remember

  • Cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium. Beta scales the premium, not the risk-free rate.
  • Debt costs kD × (1 − tax rate) after tax, because interest is deductible.
  • Weight by market values, and use a rate that matches the project’s risk: a riskier project needs a higher rate than the company average.
