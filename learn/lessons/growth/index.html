Is each customer worth more than it costs to win? Lifetime value against acquisition cost, how fast that cost comes back, and how to read a test of a new idea.

The idea

A subscriber pays every month until they cancel. The gross profit they bring each month is revenue × gross margin, and if a share c of customers cancels each month, the average customer stays 1 ÷ c months. Lifetime value (LTV) is that monthly gross profit × the lifetime.

Customer acquisition cost (CAC) is sales and marketing spend ÷ customers won. An LTV:CAC of 3 or more is the usual comfort zone, and payback (CAC ÷ monthly gross profit) says how many months the cost takes to come back. Net revenue retention follows last year’s customers: above 100% means their upgrades outweigh cancellations.

Growth ideas get tested. An A/B test’s p-value is the chance of a gap at least this large if the two versions were really the same; it is not the chance that B is better. To detect a lift half the size, you need about four times the visitors, because the sample grows with 1 ÷ (lift)².

The common mistake

Using revenue instead of gross profit

Dividing revenue by churn gives $32 ÷ 0.06 = $533 and an LTV:CAC of 11.9, more than twice the real 5.3. Revenue also pays for the boxes; only gross profit is left to repay the cost of winning the customer.

Remember

  • LTV = monthly gross profit ÷ monthly churn; CAC = acquisition spend ÷ customers won. Aim for an LTV:CAC of 3 or more.
  • Payback = CAC ÷ monthly gross profit. Net revenue retention above 100% means existing customers grow on their own.
  • p = 0.03 means a gap this big would turn up 3% of the time if the versions were the same, not that B is 97% likely to be better. Halving the lift you want to detect quadruples the sample.
