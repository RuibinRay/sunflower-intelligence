How much do sales fall when the price rises? Elasticity answers in percentages, and with the cost of a unit it points to the price that earns the most.

The idea

The price elasticity of demand is the percentage change in units sold for a 1% change in price. It is almost always negative. At −2, a 1% price rise loses about 2% of units; at −0.5, only about 0.5%.

Revenue is price × units, so elasticity tells you which way revenue moves. When demand is inelastic (between 0 and −1), units fall by a smaller percentage than price rises, and a price rise lifts revenue. When it is elastic (below −1), a rise cuts revenue.

To estimate elasticity from sales at different prices, fit ln(units) on ln(price): the slope is the elasticity, the same at every price. With the cost of one unit, a constant elasticity also gives the profit-maximising price directly.

The formula

b = % change in units% change in price ≈ ln(Q2 ÷ Q1)ln(P2 ÷ P1), P* = cost × b1 + b

b
the elasticity: the slope of ln(units) on ln(price)
Q, P
units sold and price, at two prices (or many, in a regression)
cost

P*

The common mistake

Using simple percentage changes

From $4 to $5 the price rises 25% and units fall 36%, which gives −36 ÷ 25 = −1.44. Measured the other way, from $5 down to $4, units rise 56% while the price falls 20%: −2.81. Simple percentages give a different answer depending on the direction; logs give −2.0 both ways.

Remember

  • Elasticity is the percentage change in units for a 1% change in price. Estimate it as the slope of ln(units) on ln(price).
  • Inelastic demand (between 0 and −1): a price rise lifts revenue. Elastic demand (below −1): it lowers revenue.
  • With a constant elasticity b below −1, the profit-maximising price is cost × b ÷ (1 + b). Near −1 that price runs away, so test it before trusting it.
