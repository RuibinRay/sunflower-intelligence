Before any model, describe the data: where its middle is, how far it spreads, and which columns move together. Each summary can mislead in a known way.

The idea

The mean adds everything up and divides by the count; the median is the middle value once the data are sorted. When a few values are far out on one side (skew), the mean follows them and the median barely moves. For incomes, house prices and order sizes, report the median, or both.

Spread has the same split. The standard deviation uses every value’s distance from the mean, so one extreme value can inflate it. The interquartile range (IQR), the distance between the 25th and 75th percentiles, describes the middle half and ignores the extremes.

Pearson’s correlation r measures how closely two columns follow a straight line, from −1 to +1. Spearman’s is Pearson’s on the ranks, so it resists outliers and catches any steadily rising or falling pattern. Neither says anything about cause: a third factor can drive both.

The formula

x̄ = Σ xin, s = √[ Σ (xi − x̄)2 ÷ (n − 1) ], r = Σ (xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ)√[Σ (xi − x̄)2 × Σ (yi − ȳ)2]

x̄
the mean of the values x1 … xn
s
the sample standard deviation; dividing by n − 1 corrects for measuring spread around an estimated mean
IQR
Q3 − Q1, the 75th minus the 25th percentile
r
Pearson’s correlation; Spearman’s ρ is the same formula applied to the ranks of x and y

Percentiles here interpolate between neighbouring sorted values, as spreadsheets and NumPy do by default.

The common mistake

Reading a correlation as a cause

Over twelve months of invented data, ice-cream sales and drownings correlate at r = 0.97. Concluding that ice cream causes drowning, or that banning it would save swimmers, is wrong: hot weather raises both. A strong correlation says two things move together, not why.

Remember

  • Skewed data: the mean chases the long tail. Report the median, and the IQR for spread.
  • Pearson’s r measures straight-line association only; a strong curve can score 0. Spearman’s ρ handles outliers and steady curves.
  • Correlation is not cause. Look for a third factor that could drive both columns.
