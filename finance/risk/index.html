Market risk first: how much a portfolio could lose, measured three ways and checked against what actually happened. Then credit risk: a screen for a company in financial distress.

Value at Risk

The loss a portfolio should exceed only rarely: at 99% confidence, on one day in a hundred. Expected Shortfall answers the next question, how bad those days are on average. Three ways to estimate both, side by side.

Confidence
—
  • Historical replays past returns as they were; it knows nothing worse than its sample. Normal assumes the bell curve, which understates fat tails. Monte Carlo here simulates 20,000 outcomes from a Student-t fitted to the returns’ mean, volatility and tail weight (kurtosis), compounding across the horizon.
  • Longer horizons scale the historical numbers by the square root of time, the Basel convention. That assumes returns are independent from one period to the next, which calm-then-stormy markets break.

Backtest

Was the model right? Each day, estimate VaR from the window of returns before it, then check whether that day’s loss broke it. A 99% VaR should be broken on about 1% of days, and the breaks shouldn’t bunch together.

Method
Window

The confidence level is the one set under Value at Risk.

  • Kupiec’s test asks whether the share of breaks is consistent with the confidence level. Christoffersen’s test asks whether a break makes another more likely the next day; bunched breaks mean the model reacts too slowly.
  • The Basel traffic light grades a bank’s 99% model on its last 250 days: up to 4 breaks is green, 5 to 9 yellow, 10 or more red. Here it is applied to the whole backtest, scaled by the same binomial probabilities.

Stress scenarios

VaR describes ordinary bad days. A stress test asks what a specific shock would do: shares fall, rates jump, credit spreads widen. Each position is revalued under the scenario.

The scenario

Illustrative shocks, not forecasts. Move any slider to make the scenario your own.

—
  • Bonds are revalued with duration and convexity: price change ≈ −duration × change in yield + ½ × convexity × change in yield². Corporate bonds feel rates and spreads together.
  • A first-order revaluation: no second-round effects, no options, no liquidity costs of selling into a falling market.

Altman Z-score

A five-ratio screen for financial distress, from Edward Altman’s 1968 study of manufacturers, with his later versions for private companies and for non-manufacturers.

Invented figures for practice, not real companies.

—
  • It is a screen, not a forecast. Altman built it on 66 US manufacturers from 1946–1965; treat the zones as prompts for a closer look.
  • Not meant for banks, insurers or other financial firms, whose balance sheets work differently.
  • Each version has its own cut-offs. Use Z″ for retailers and service companies, which carry fewer assets per dollar of sales.
