Describe a share’s return and risk, measure its beta against the market, and find the portfolio mix that earns the most for the risk it takes.

Prices

Daily closing prices for a few shares and a market index. Use the simulated set, or bring your own: a table with dates in the first column and one column of prices per share, or one or more price files downloaded from a site such as Yahoo Finance.

Prices

A file with an “Adj Close” or “Close” column becomes one share, named after the file. Several files are lined up on their common dates. Read in this browser tab and never uploaded.

Return and risk

How much a share earned, how bumpy the ride was, and the worst fall from a peak. Every share is in the table; the charts follow the one you pick.

  • Annual return is the average daily return × 252; growth rate (CAGR) is what the price actually compounded at. Volatility drags the second below the first.
  • Sharpe divides the return above the risk-free rate by volatility; Sortino counts only the downside.
  • Prices should be adjusted for dividends and splits, or the returns will be wrong on those days.

Beta and alpha

The capital asset pricing model regresses a share’s daily excess return on the market’s. The slope, beta, is the market risk you can’t diversify away; the intercept, alpha, is return the market doesn’t explain.

Efficient frontier

Markowitz’s question: of all the ways to split money between these shares, which give the most return for each level of risk? No short selling: every weight is zero or more.

Shares to combine (two to eight)
Portfolio to show
  • Built from past average returns, volatilities and correlations, assumed to hold in future. They won’t exactly.
  • Solved exactly: for every subset of shares the best mix has a closed form, and the best subset with no negative weights is the answer.

Sources

  3. Sortino, F. A. and Price, L. N. (1994). Performance measurement in a downside risk framework. The Journal of Investing, 3(3), 59–64.
  4. The simulated prices follow a GARCH(1,1) market with Student-t shocks (Bollerslev, 1986) and a one-factor model for each share. The companies are invented.
